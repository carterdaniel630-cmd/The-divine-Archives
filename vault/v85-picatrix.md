# The Picatrix: The Goal of the Sage

*Vault entry V85 · Texts & Tablets · Arabic original compiled in al-Andalus, 10th–11th century; Castilian translation 1256–1258 · Recently added — pending full review.*

Take a stone engraved at the right hour, it says, when the Moon is in the right mansion and the right planet is rising, and the power of the heavens will pour down into the image you have cut. Burn the right incense, speak the prayer to the planet in its own words, and wear the talisman. This is the teaching of the ***Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm***, "**The Goal of the Sage**", an Arabic handbook of astral magic written in Muslim Spain. In Latin Europe it was called ***Picatrix***, and for five centuries it was the most complete manual of talismanic magic that European readers had.

## The book

- Written in **Arabic**, probably in **al-Andalus** in the **tenth or eleventh century**. It is a compilation, drawing on Hermetic, Sabian (Harranian), Neoplatonic, Indian and Persian material, and names dozens of earlier works.
- Four books: on the heavens and the nature of magic; on the figures of the heavens and the making of talismans; on the properties of the planets and their prayers; on spirits, the Sabian rites, and potions and fumigations.
- It teaches that the universe is one, that the lower world receives the influence of the higher, and that the sage who understands the correspondences can draw down celestial power, the ***rūḥāniyyāt***, "spiritual forces" of the planets.

## Who wrote it?

- Many manuscripts name **Maslama al-Majrīṭī** (d. c. 1007), the Córdoban astronomer; modern scholarship calls this **pseudo-Majrīṭī**.
- In **1996** **Maribel Fierro** argued for another Córdoban, **Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī** (d. 964), and later scholars (Godefroid de Callataÿ, Sébastien Moureau) have supported her. Others keep the question open.

## The translation

At the court of **Alfonso X of Castile**, the text was put into **Castilian** between **1256 and 1258**, and from the Castilian into **Latin**. The Latin *Picatrix* shortened the philosophy and changed or dropped passages tied to Islamic practice. It was copied throughout late medieval and Renaissance Europe and was known to **Marsilio Ficino** and **Cornelius Agrippa**. The origin of the name "Picatrix" is itself uncertain; the Latin preface presents it as the name of the author.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Lunar mansions.** The Moon's monthly course is divided into **28 mansions**, each with its own image and uses; a talisman is made when the Moon is in the mansion that suits the work.

**Planetary images.** For each planet the book gives the figures to be engraved, the metals, stones, colours, incenses and animals that belong to it, and the hours and days it rules: a complete system of **correspondences**.

**Prayers to the planets.** The planets are addressed as powers with names and costumes, drawing on the traditions attributed to the star-worshipping **Sabians of Harran**.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- The *Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm* is an **Arabic** compilation of astral magic from **al-Andalus**, translated into **Castilian** under **Alfonso X (1256–1258)** and then into **Latin** as *Picatrix*.
- It was widely copied and read in Renaissance Europe.

**What is not supported**

- That its talismans work: the book records what its authors believed about celestial influence; there is no evidence that engraved images draw down planetary powers.
- The traditional attribution to **Maslama al-Majrīṭī**, which is widely rejected.

**What's genuinely open**

- Whether the author was **Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī** or someone else, and the exact date.
- Where the Latin title "Picatrix" came from.
