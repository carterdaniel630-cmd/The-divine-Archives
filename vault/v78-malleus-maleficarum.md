# The Malleus Maleficarum: The Hammer of Witches

*Vault entry V78 · Texts & Tablets · First printed at Speyer, 1486*

In the autumn of **1485** a Dominican inquisitor named **Heinrich Kramer** (in Latin, *Institoris*) was questioning women in **Innsbruck**. One of them, **Helena Scheuberin**, a burgher's wife, had defied him; Kramer's questions about her sexual life scandalised the court. Her lawyers had the proceedings dismissed on procedural grounds, and the local bishop, **Georg Golser of Brixen**, told Kramer he had "presumed much that had not been proved" and ordered him out of the diocese, calling him, in a letter, a man who seemed to have become childish with age. Kramer left, and wrote a book. It was printed at **Speyer in 1486** as the ***Malleus Maleficarum***, "the Hammer of Witches".

## The book

- **Three parts.** Part I argues that witchcraft is real and that denying it is heresy; Part II describes what witches do (pacts with the devil, harming livestock and crops, impotence, flight) and the remedies; Part III is a **legal manual** for prosecuting them, including the use of **torture** and of deception to obtain confessions.
- **The bull.** Kramer printed at the front the papal bull ***Summis desiderantes affectibus***, issued by **Innocent VIII in 1484** at his request, which empowered him and a colleague to act against witches in German lands. Printing it made the book look papally endorsed; the bull itself endorses the inquisitors, not the book.
- **The approbation.** The book also carried an approval from the theology faculty of the **University of Cologne**. The faculty had in fact given a qualified opinion, and the printed document presents it as unanimous and unreserved; most historians regard this as **manipulated or partly forged** by Kramer.
- **Sprenger.** From **1519** the Dominican theologian **Jacob Sprenger** is named as co-author. Several historians argue that Sprenger had little or nothing to do with it, and even that he opposed Kramer; others defend a shared role. The question is **contested**.
- **Women.** The book insists that witchcraft is chiefly a female crime and offers a notorious false etymology: *femina* from *fe* (faith) and *minus* (less), "because she is always weaker to hold and preserve the faith".

## How much did it matter?

The *Malleus* was reprinted many times: around **thirteen or fourteen editions by 1520**, and another burst of editions from **1574 to 1669**. The **Inquisition** itself soon distanced itself from Kramer's procedures, and the **Spanish Inquisition** in 1538 warned its judges not to believe everything the book said. Its use was greatest in **secular courts**, especially in the German lands. How far it actually *caused* trials is **debated**: it gave prosecutors a ready-made theory and procedure, but the great waves of trials came decades later, and many courts never cite it.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The hammer.** The title copies the style of inquisitors' manuals that called themselves "hammers" of heretics: the book is a weapon aimed at a category of person.

**The printed page as authority.** Its most powerful signs are paratextual: a papal bull at the front and a university approbation at the back, both used to make a private campaign look like the voice of the Church.

**The witch as inversion.** The book builds the image of the witch as the **mirror of the sacraments**: a pact instead of baptism, the devil's service instead of the Mass, the body given to a demon instead of to God.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- Kramer wrote the book after his failed Innsbruck trials of **1485**, and it was first printed at **Speyer in 1486**, with the **1484** bull at the front.
- It went through many editions in two main waves (to 1520, and 1574–1669), and was used above all by secular courts.

**What is not supported**

- That the Church formally adopted the *Malleus* as its official manual. It never did.
- That it was the direct cause of most executions for witchcraft; trials depended on local law, politics and panic, and most came long after its first editions.

**What's genuinely open**

- Jacob **Sprenger's** real part in the book.
- How far the Cologne approbation was forged or merely misrepresented.
- How much the book shaped the trials, as against reflecting ideas already current.
