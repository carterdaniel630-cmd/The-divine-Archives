# Codex Sinaiticus: The Bible in Four Libraries

*Vault entry V18 · Manuscripts & Codices · British Library (London), Leipzig University Library, St Catherine's Monastery (Sinai), National Library of Russia (St Petersburg) · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **1844** the young German scholar **Constantin von Tischendorf** was visiting **St Catherine's Monastery** at the foot of Mount Sinai. By his own later account, which the monks have always disputed, he saw old parchment leaves in a basket and was told they were bound for the fire. He left with **43 leaves** of an ancient Greek Bible and gave them to **Leipzig**. In **1859** he returned under the patronage of the Russian Tsar and was shown the rest. The manuscript went to **St Petersburg**. How it came to leave Sinai, whether as loan, gift or appropriation, is a **dispute that has never been resolved**. In **1933** the Soviet government, needing hard currency, **sold** most of it to the **British Museum** for £100,000.

What they had found is the **Codex Sinaiticus**, written around **330–360 CE**. It is one of the **two oldest near-complete Bibles** in the world, and it contains the **oldest complete New Testament**.

## The object

- Originally over **730 leaves** of fine parchment. About **400 survive**, divided among **four institutions**:
  - **British Library, London**: 347 leaves, the main part.
  - **Leipzig University Library**: 43 leaves (1844).
  - **St Catherine's Monastery, Sinai**: 12 leaves and fragments, found in **1975** in a sealed room.
  - **National Library of Russia**: fragments of 3 leaves.
- Written in Greek **uncials**, capital letters with **no spaces between words** (*scriptio continua*), in **four columns** per page for most books.
- **Several scribes**, usually counted as three, and **thousands of later corrections** by many hands. The corrections are themselves a record of how the text was read over centuries.
- In **2009** the four institutions completed the **Codex Sinaiticus Project**, reuniting the whole surviving manuscript **online**, with transcription.

## What is in it, and what is not

- The **Greek Old Testament (Septuagint)**, including books that later Protestant Bibles would call apocrypha, such as Tobit, Judith and 1 and 4 Maccabees.
- The **whole New Testament**, together with two early Christian works **later left out of the canon**: the ***Epistle of Barnabas*** and part of the ***Shepherd of Hermas***.
- **Mark ends at 16:8**, with the women fleeing the empty tomb "for they were afraid". The longer ending (Mark 16:9–20) that most later Bibles print is **absent**. That fact, shared with Codex Vaticanus, is central to modern textual study of Mark.

## Symbology and scribal encoding

**Sacred names in shorthand.** Like other early Christian manuscripts, Sinaiticus writes the holiest names as **contractions with a line over them**: ΘΣ for *theos* (God), ΚΣ for *kyrios* (Lord), ΙΣ for *Iēsous*, ΧΣ for *Christos*. These ***nomina sacra*** are a **real, deliberate scribal marking of holiness**. They are closely related to the way Jewish scribes set the divine name apart (V02).

**The text as a living layer-cake.** The corrections, which run into the thousands, show later readers adjusting the text towards the form that later became standard. The manuscript preserves **a text in motion**, not a single fixed original.

## Connections

Early Christianity (ch16), Patristic Christianity (ch22, for the formation of the canon), the Dead Sea Scrolls (V02, for the Jewish scribal handling of the divine name), and the Nag Hammadi Codices (V08), another fourth-century library of Christian books.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **fourth-century Greek Bible** (c. 330–360), about **400 surviving leaves**, now divided among **London, Leipzig, Sinai and St Petersburg** and reunited online in **2009**. It contains the **Septuagint**, the **complete New Testament**, **Barnabas** and part of **Hermas**, and ends **Mark at 16:8**.

**What is not supported**

- Claims that Sinaiticus is a **modern forgery**. Its parchment, scripts, corrections and history all argue against that.

**What's genuinely open**

- **How it left Sinai.** Tischendorf's "fire basket" story and the terms of the 1859 transfer are disputed, and **St Catherine's continues to regard the manuscript as taken**.
- **Where it was written**. Egypt, Caesarea and elsewhere have been proposed.
