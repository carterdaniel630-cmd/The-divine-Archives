# The Codex Borgia: A Painted Book of Days

*Vault entry V41 · Manuscripts · Vatican Apostolic Library (Borg.mess.1) · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **1805** the naturalist **Alexander von Humboldt**, in Rome, examined the collections of the late **Cardinal Stefano Borgia**, a passionate collector of antiquities from every continent. Among them was a long strip of **deerskin**, folded like a screen and painted on both sides with gods, day-signs, numbers and brilliantly coloured scenes. How it came to Italy from Mexico is unknown. Humboldt published pages from it. When the cardinal's collection went to the **Vatican**, the book went with it and took his name. It is one of the **very few religious books from central Mexico** to survive the Spanish conquest.

## The book

- A **screenfold** of **fourteen pieces of deerskin** joined into a strip over **10 metres** long, coated with white gesso and folded into **39 leaves**. It is painted on both sides, making **76 pages** (the end panels are unpainted).
- Painted with mineral and plant colours in the **Mixteca-Puebla** style, probably in the **Puebla–Tlaxcala** region.
- Dated to the **late fifteenth or early sixteenth century**, most likely just before the conquest.
- It belongs to the **Borgia Group**, a set of related divinatory books that also includes the Codex Vaticanus B, the Codex Cospi, the Codex Fejérváry-Mayer and the Codex Laud.

## A manual of the days

The book is built on the **tonalpohualli**, "the count of days": **260 days**, made by combining the numbers **1 to 13** with **twenty named day-signs**, from Crocodile to Flower. Priest-diviners (*tonalpouhque*) used books like this to read a person's **fate** from their birth-day, and to choose good days for marriages, journeys, planting and war. The Borgia sets out the count in many ways:

- The **twenty trecenas** (thirteen-day periods), each with its presiding gods.
- The days divided among the **four directions** and the **world-trees**.
- The **nine Lords of the Night** and the **thirteen birds**.
- A **Venus almanac** (pages 53–54) on the dangerous first appearance of the morning star, the same concern as the Dresden Codex (V31).

## The central section

**Pages 29–46** break the pattern. They show a continuous series of scenes that are not a calendar table: dark, night-time spaces, the wind god **Quetzalcoatl-Ehecatl**, deities emerging from a body of night, fire, sacrifice and rebirth. Interpretations include:

- a **ritual journey** of a priest or a god through the underworld and the night sky;
- an account of **Venus** in its hidden phase;
- a sequence of **temple ceremonies** in the order they were performed.

These are serious proposals, and **none is proven**. The section is often called the most enigmatic group of pages in Mesoamerican art.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Signs as beings.** Each day-sign (Crocodile, Wind, House, Lizard, Serpent, Death and the rest) is a **living power** with its own gods, directions and colours. A day's name is its **character**.

**The body and the calendar.** One page sets the twenty day-signs on the **body of a god**, each sign tied to a part of the body. It shows that time, the cosmos and the person were read as one system.

**Colour and direction.** Red, black, yellow, blue-green and white are tied to the **four directions** and the centre, a cosmic map painted in pigment.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **pre-conquest or contact-period** divinatory screenfold of **deerskin**, **76 pages**, from **central Mexico**, in the **Vatican Library** since the early nineteenth century, named after **Cardinal Stefano Borgia**.
- Its content is structured by the **260-day count**, with trecenas, directional almanacs and a **Venus almanac**.

**What is not supported**

- Claims that it records **prophecies** of modern events, or knowledge from outside Mesoamerica.

**What's genuinely open**

- The meaning of the **central section** (pages 29–46).
- **Where exactly** and **when** it was painted, and **how it reached Europe**.
