# The Stećci: Medieval Tombstones of Bosnia

*Vault entry V71 · Relics & Objects · In place across Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Montenegro and Serbia*

On a hillside at **Radimlja**, near Stolac in Herzegovina, about 130 great blocks of limestone lie in rows beside the road. Some are flat slabs, some chests, some shaped like little houses with gabled roofs. Their sides are carved with spirals, vines, crescents, horsemen hunting deer, and a line of dancers holding hands. On one, a man stands with his **right hand raised and open**, the hand carved larger than life. These are **stećci** (singular *stećak*), the medieval tombstones of Bosnia and its neighbours, and for a century and a half they were wrongly known as the graves of heretics.

## The tombstones

- About **70,000** stećci are recorded at some **3,300** sites, most in **Bosnia and Herzegovina**, others in **Croatia**, **Montenegro** and **Serbia**.
- They date from the **twelfth to the sixteenth centuries**, most from the fourteenth and fifteenth.
- Most are undecorated; a minority carry carved ornament or figures, and a smaller number **inscriptions** in *bosančica*, the Bosnian form of Cyrillic.
- In **2016**, **28** of the sites, with some **4,000** stones, were inscribed together as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site**.

## The Bogomil myth

In the nineteenth century, scholars linked the stećci to the **Bogomils**, a dualist movement of the medieval Balkans that taught that the material world was the work of an evil power (see Gnosticism, ch17, and Manichaeism, ch55). Medieval Bosnia had its own church, the **Bosnian Church**, which its Catholic and Orthodox opponents accused of that heresy. The idea that the stećci were "Bogomil tombstones" became popular and is still repeated.

Modern historians and archaeologists reject it. Stećci are found in areas that were **Catholic** and **Orthodox** as well as in the lands of the Bosnian Church; some bear crosses and Christian inscriptions; and they were used by **all three** communities, as UNESCO's description states. Whether the Bosnian Church itself was dualist is also debated.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The raised hand.** The best-known figure has been read as a greeting, an oath, a gesture of prayer, or a sign of rank. There is no medieval explanation.

**Sky signs.** **Crescents**, **stars** and **discs** are common. Because the crescent later became associated with Islam, some have read them as Muslim, but they appear on stones made before the Ottoman conquest of Bosnia in **1463**, and were part of older Christian and folk symbolism.

**Life carved in stone.** Hunting scenes, tournaments and the **kolo**, the circle dance still danced in the region, may show the life of the dead person, a funeral rite, or both.

**House-shaped tombs.** Gabled stećci resemble **houses**, echoing the widespread idea of the grave as the home of the dead.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- About **70,000** medieval tombstones from the **12th to 16th centuries**, at some **3,300** sites in four modern countries, **28** of them a UNESCO site since **2016**.
- Their use by **Catholic, Orthodox and Bosnian Church** communities.

**What is not supported**

- That they are **Bogomil** tombstones, or the work of any single religious group.
- That their crescents and stars are **Islamic** symbols.

**What's genuinely open**

- The meaning of motifs such as the **raised hand**.
- The beliefs of the **Bosnian Church**, and whether it was dualist.
