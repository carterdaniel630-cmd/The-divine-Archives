# The Bardo Thödol: "Liberation Through Hearing in the In-Between"

*Vault entry V82 · Texts & Tablets · Revealed as a treasure text by Karma Lingpa, 14th century · Recently added — pending full review.*

In Tibet, when someone dies, a lama may sit beside the body and read aloud. The reading continues for days, as long as **forty-nine**, addressed not to the mourners but to the dead person, by name: "O child of noble family, listen without distraction. Now what is called death has arrived." The text tells the consciousness where it is, what it is seeing, and how to be free. Its Tibetan name is ***Bardo Thödol*** (*bar do thos grol*), "**Liberation through Hearing in the Intermediate State**". Since **1927**, English readers have known it as ***The Tibetan Book of the Dead***.

## A treasure text

- In Tibetan tradition the teachings were given by **Padmasambhava**, the Indian master who helped establish Buddhism in Tibet in the **eighth century**, written down by his consort **Yeshe Tsogyal**, and **hidden** as ***terma***, "treasures", to be found when the time was right.
- In the **fourteenth century** the treasure-revealer (***tertön***) **Karma Lingpa** (1326–1386) is said to have recovered them from **Mount Gampodar** in central Tibet, as part of a larger cycle, the *Peaceful and Wrathful Deities: Self-Liberation through the Wisdom Mind*.
- It belongs to the **Nyingma** school, though it is used more widely, and is one of many funeral texts in Tibet.

## The bardos

*Bardo* means an "in-between" state. The book guides the dead through three:

- **The bardo of dying** (*chikhai*): the elements of the body dissolve and the **Clear Light** of reality dawns. To recognise it is to be liberated at once.
- **The bardo of reality** (*chönyi*): the **forty-two peaceful** and **fifty-eight wrathful deities** appear over many days. The text insists they are the mind's own projections, and recognising them as such frees the dead.
- **The bardo of becoming** (*sidpa*): if no recognition comes, the mind is driven by karma toward rebirth, and the text teaches how to choose a good womb or "close the womb door".

Later teachers count **six bardos** in all, adding birth and life, dream, and meditation.

## The English book

The Sikkimese lama **Kazi Dawa Samdup** translated it; the American **W. Y. Evans-Wentz** edited it and gave it its title in **1927**, by analogy with the equally misnamed Egyptian "Book of the Dead" (V20). Evans-Wentz framed it with Theosophical ideas; **Carl Jung** wrote a commentary for a later edition; **Timothy Leary** and colleagues adapted it as a manual for psychedelic experience in **1964**. Newer translations (Thurman 1994; Dorje, Coleman and Jinpa 2005) work from the Tibetan and its whole cycle.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Lights.** In the bardo of reality each buddha appears with a **brilliant coloured light** and, beside it, a **dull, comforting light** leading to one of the six realms of rebirth. The instruction is always the same: go toward the dazzling light, not the soft one.

**The mandala of deities.** The hundred peaceful and wrathful deities form a **mandala** inside the body: the peaceful in the heart, the wrathful in the brain.

**Hearing.** Its power is said to lie in being **heard**: the dead are assumed to keep a subtle hearing, and the words themselves are the path.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- The text belongs to a **treasure cycle** revealed by **Karma Lingpa** in the **fourteenth century** and is read to the dying and dead in Tibetan practice.
- The English title "**Tibetan Book of the Dead**" was coined by **Evans-Wentz** in **1927**.

**What is not supported**

- That the experiences it describes are observed facts about what happens after death; that is a matter of Buddhist belief. Modern parallels drawn with near-death experiences or psychedelics are interpretations, not evidence.

**What's genuinely open**

- How far the text goes back before Karma Lingpa: tradition places it in the eighth century; historians date the cycle as we have it to the fourteenth.
