# The Papyrus of Ani: A Heart on the Scales

*Vault entry V20 · Manuscripts & Codices · British Museum, London (EA 10470) · Recently added — pending full review.*

A scribe named **Ani** stands with his wife **Tutu** in a hall of judgment. In the centre stands a **balance**. On one pan lies **Ani's heart**. On the other lies a single **feather**, the sign of ***Ma'at***: truth, order, rightness. The jackal-headed **Anubis** checks the plumb line. The ibis-headed **Thoth** stands ready with his palette to record the verdict. Crouched beside the scales is **Ammit**, the "Devourer", part crocodile, part lion, part hippopotamus, waiting to eat any heart heavier than truth. This scene, painted in the thirteenth century BCE, is the most famous image of judgment after death from the ancient world. It comes from the **Papyrus of Ani**.

## The object

- A roll of papyrus about **24 metres long**, made for **Ani**, a royal scribe and overseer of offerings at **Thebes**. It dates to the **19th Dynasty**, around **1250 BCE**.
- A copy of the **"Book of Going Forth by Day"**, better known as the **Book of the Dead**. This is a collection of **spells** that helped the deceased pass through the dangers of the afterlife. The Papyrus of Ani is considered **one of the finest surviving copies**, and it has an unusually large number of painted **vignettes**.
- **Acquired in 1888** in Egypt by **E. A. Wallis Budge** for the **British Museum**, from the antiquities trade. Budge **cut the roll into sheets** for display and study, a practice modern conservators regret.

## Spell 125: the Negative Confession

The judgment scene illustrates **Spell 125**. There the deceased declares his innocence **before Osiris** and then, one by one, before **forty-two divine judges**, each named. He addresses each judge with a denial of one wrongdoing, which is why it is called the **"Negative Confession"**. Among the denials, in this archive's working renderings:

> I have not robbed with violence.
> I have not killed man or woman.
> I have not stolen the offerings of the gods.
> I have not told lies.
> I have not caused anyone to weep.

The standard of judgment is **Ma'at**, which is **social as much as ritual**: honesty, not cheating with weights and measures, not taking what belongs to others, not causing grief. The declarations are a **documented ancient moral code** in the first person.

**True of voice.** If the heart balanced against the feather, the deceased was declared ***maa-kheru***, "**true of voice**" or "justified". Thoth recorded the verdict, and the dead person could pass on to the realm of Osiris.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Heart and feather.** For Egyptians the **heart** (*ib*) was the seat of thought, memory and will, and it **could testify against its owner**. For that reason **Spell 30B**, often inscribed on **heart scarabs** placed on the mummy, commands the heart not to speak against the deceased. The **feather** is the hieroglyph for *ma'at*. The scene is a **visual equation**: a life is weighed against truth itself.

**Words that work.** The Book of the Dead's spells were **effective by being written**. Hieroglyphs themselves were considered potent, and in some tombs dangerous signs, such as snakes and scorpions, were **drawn cut in half** so they could not harm the dead. The papyrus is a **ritual instrument**, not only a book.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **24-metre Book of the Dead** papyrus made for the scribe **Ani** at **Thebes**, c. **1250 BCE** (19th Dynasty), in the **British Museum** since **1888**. It contains **Spell 125**, with the **weighing of the heart** against the **feather of Ma'at** and the **Negative Confession** to **42 judges**.

**What is not supported**

- Claims that the papyrus encodes **secret initiatory teachings** of a hidden priesthood. It is a **funerary text** of a type widely produced for paying clients.

**What's genuinely open**

- The **exact findspot and circumstances** of its acquisition, given that it came from the antiquities trade in 1888.
- How closely **ordinary Egyptians' beliefs** matched the ideals of the Book of the Dead, which was made mainly for the **elite**.
