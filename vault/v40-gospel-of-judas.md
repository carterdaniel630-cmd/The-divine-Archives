# The Gospel of Judas: A Lost Gospel, a Looted Codex and a Disputed Translation

*Vault entry V40 · Manuscripts · Coptic Museum, Cairo (Codex Tchacos) · Recently added — pending full review.*

Around **180 CE**, **Irenaeus**, bishop of Lyon, listed the books of groups he called heretics. One group, he wrote, honoured **Cain** and the men of Sodom, and "they produce a fictitious history of this kind, which they style **the Gospel of Judas**." For 1,800 years that one sentence was all that was known of it. Then, in the **1970s**, a leather-bound **papyrus codex** in **Coptic** was found, probably in a burial cave near **al-Minya** in Middle Egypt. On its pages, after a letter of Peter and a text about James, was a work ending with the words **"The Gospel of Judas."**

## The codex's journey

- Dealers took it out of Egypt. Offered for sale in **1983** for a huge price and unsold, it spent **sixteen years** in a **safe-deposit box in Hicksville, New York**, where damp and heat crumbled it.
- In **2000** the Zurich dealer **Frieda Tchacos Nussberger** bought it and named it **Codex Tchacos** after her father. After another failed sale it was placed with the **Maecenas Foundation**, which partnered with the **National Geographic Society** to conserve and publish it. The codex was to be **returned to Egypt**, and is now in the Coptic Museum.
- Restorers reassembled **about a thousand fragments**. Radiocarbon dating by the **University of Arizona** placed the codex between **220 and 340 CE**. Ink analysis was consistent with antiquity.
- The Gospel is a **Coptic translation** of a **Greek** original, most likely the one Irenaeus knew, so written before c. 180.

## What the gospel says

The text is a **dialogue**. Over a week before Passover, Jesus **laughs** at the disciples' piety, telling them they worship a lesser god. He takes **Judas** aside and reveals the **mysteries of the kingdom**: an unseen realm, a great invisible **Spirit**, luminous aeons, and below them rulers such as **Nebro/Yaldabaoth** and **Saklas** who made this world. It is the cosmology of **Sethian Gnosticism**. It ends abruptly with Judas taking money and **handing Jesus over**.

## Hero or demon? The translation dispute

On **6 April 2006** National Geographic unveiled the text, in a television documentary and a book, as the story of a **good Judas**, the only disciple who understood Jesus, acting on his instructions in betraying him. Almost at once specialists challenged it. **April DeConick** of Rice University, in *The Thirteenth Apostle* (2007) and in *The New York Times*, argued that the team had made crucial choices in the most flattering direction:

- *daimōn*, which in early Christian texts normally means **demon**, was rendered **"spirit"**, so "thirteenth demon" became "thirteenth spirit".
- A line saying Judas would **not** ascend to the holy generation was read as saying he **would**.
- "**You will exceed them all**" was read as praise. DeConick and others read it as saying Judas would do the **worst** deed of all.

Other scholars, including members of the original team, defended parts of their reading. A newly surfaced fragment later helped settle some readings and not others. Most specialists now see Judas in this text as a **tragic or demonic figure**, not a hero, although the question remains **debated**.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Thirteen.** Judas is the **thirteenth**, set apart from the Twelve. In Sethian cosmology the **thirteenth aeon** is the realm of the lower ruler, so the number may place Judas with the world-maker, not with the divine.

**Laughter.** Jesus' laughter, a motif in several Gnostic texts, marks the gap between true knowledge and the disciples' misplaced devotion.

**The star.** Jesus tells Judas that "the star that leads the way is your star". Astral fate, and escape from it, runs through the text.

Gnosticism (ch17), Early Christianity (ch16) and Pistis Sophia (ch45). Compare the Nag Hammadi Codices (V08), which contain other Sethian texts, and the Gospel of Jesus's Wife (V17) for how a sensational announcement can shape a manuscript's reception.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- **Codex Tchacos** is an **authentic ancient Coptic codex**, radiocarbon-dated to **220–340 CE**, containing a **Gospel of Judas**. It is probably the text **Irenaeus** mentioned c. 180.
- It is a **Sethian Gnostic** text, not a historical source for the life of Judas or Jesus.

**What is not supported**

- That it reveals what **really happened** between Jesus and Judas. It was written about a century later, within a sectarian theology.
- That its heroic-Judas reading is **settled**. The 2006 interpretation has been widely challenged.

**What's genuinely open**

- **How Judas is portrayed**: tragic, demonic, or privileged.
- The circumstances of the codex's **discovery** and whether any **missing pages** survive elsewhere.
