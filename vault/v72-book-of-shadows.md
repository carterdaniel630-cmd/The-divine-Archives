# The Book of Shadows: The Making of Wicca

*Vault entry V72 · Manuscripts · Gerald Gardner's manuscripts in private hands; copied by initiates · Recently added — pending full review.*

Some time in the late **1940s**, a retired British customs officer named **Gerald Gardner** began filling a leather-bound notebook with rituals, charms and invocations, written in mock-archaic spelling: *Ye Bok of ye Art Magical*. Gardner said he had been initiated in **1939** into a coven of hereditary witches in the **New Forest**, the survivors of an ancient pagan religion. His notebook, renamed the **Book of Shadows**, became the ritual book of the religion he launched, **Wicca**. Every new initiate was expected to copy it out by hand.

## The book

- A working **ritual manual**: the casting of the circle, the calling of the quarters, initiation rites for three degrees, seasonal festivals, and the **Charge of the Goddess**.
- It changed constantly. Gardner revised it through the 1950s, and his students adapted it; there was never one fixed text.
- It is **oath-bound**: in traditional (Gardnerian and Alexandrian) covens, its contents are kept secret. Versions have been published, and they differ.

## Doreen Valiente

In **1953** **Doreen Valiente** was initiated into Gardner's coven. She saw that much of the book came from the writings of **Aleister Crowley** (see V73) and from ceremonial magic, and she feared this would discredit the Craft. Gardner invited her to rewrite. Her poetic version of the **Charge of the Goddess**, in which the Goddess speaks to her worshippers, is now one of the best-known texts of modern Paganism. In **1964** she gave the first known public form of the **Wiccan Rede**: "Eight words the Wiccan Rede fulfil: an it harm none, do what ye will."

## Where did it come from?

Gardner presented the rites as ancient. Historians, above all **Ronald Hutton** in *The Triumph of the Moon* (1999), have traced them to sources of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries: Crowley, the Golden Dawn, **Charles Leland**'s *Aradia* (1899), Freemasonry, folklore, and **Margaret Murray**'s theory of a surviving witch cult, which historians of the witch trials reject (see The Witch Trials, ch32). Whether Gardner met any real group of practising witches before he wrote is **uncertain**; some researchers have argued for a New Forest group of some kind, but no evidence shows it continued an ancient religion.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The circle and the pentacle.** The rites centre on the **magic circle**, cast to make a sacred space, and the **pentacle**, a disc marked with a five-pointed star and other signs, taken from ceremonial magic.

**A copied book.** The instruction to copy the book by hand made each initiate's copy a record of their own lineage, and handwriting itself part of the tradition.

**The Goddess and the God.** The book gave form to Wicca's pair of deities, the Goddess and the Horned God (see the Pantheon).

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A ritual manual begun by **Gerald Gardner** in the late **1940s**, revised with **Doreen Valiente** in the 1950s, and the foundation of **Wicca**.
- Its borrowings from **Crowley**, ceremonial magic, Leland and folklore.

**What is not supported**

- That its rites preserve an **ancient pagan witch religion**.

**What's genuinely open**

- Whether Gardner met a **pre-existing group** in the New Forest, and what it practised.
