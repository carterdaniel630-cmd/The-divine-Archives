# The Mesha Stele: A King of Moab Answers the Bible

*Vault entry V22 · Texts & Tablets · Musée du Louvre, Paris (AO 5066) · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **1868** an Anglican missionary, **F. A. Klein**, was shown a black basalt stone, about a metre tall and rounded at the top, lying at **Dhiban** in what is now Jordan. It was covered with **34 lines** of writing in an alphabet close to early Hebrew. European consulates began bidding for it. Before it could be removed, the local **Bani Hamida** people **heated it in a fire, poured cold water over it and shattered it**. Accounts differ on whether they wanted to spite the Ottoman authorities or to multiply its value. Fortunately the French scholar **Charles Clermont-Ganneau** had already obtained a **squeeze**, a papier-mâché impression, of the intact text, torn in haste while still wet. From that squeeze and most of the recovered pieces, the stone was **reassembled**. It is now in the **Louvre**.

## The object and its text

- A **basalt stele**, about **1.2 m** tall, inscribed around **840 BCE** in **Moabite**, a language closely related to Hebrew, written in the **palaeo-Hebrew / Phoenician-type script**.
- The speaker is **Mesha, king of Moab**. It begins:

> I am Mesha, son of Chemosh[…], king of Moab, the Dibonite.

- Mesha says that **Omri, king of Israel**, had oppressed Moab for many years, "for **Chemosh**, the god of Moab, was **angry with his land**". Chemosh then returned, and Mesha drove Israel out, took its towns and **dedicated their people to Chemosh**.
- At the town of **Nebo**, Mesha says he took the **vessels of YHWH** and dragged them before Chemosh. This is **the earliest certain reference outside the Bible to YHWH as the God of Israel**.

## Mesha and the Bible

**2 Kings 3** tells the same conflict **from the other side**. **Mesha, king of Moab**, rebels after the death of Ahab. A coalition of Israel, Judah and Edom invades Moab. At the climax Mesha **sacrifices his firstborn son on the wall**, and "great wrath" comes upon Israel, which withdraws.

The two accounts **agree** on a Moabite king named Mesha, Israelite domination under the **Omride** dynasty, and a successful Moabite revolt. They **disagree** on outcome and emphasis, as war reports on opposite sides usually do. Their **shared theology** is striking: in both, a **national god's anger** explains defeat, and his favour explains victory.

**"House of David"?** In **1994** the epigrapher **André Lemaire** proposed reading a damaged line (line 31) as ***bt[d]wd***, "House of David". That would make it the **second** such reference after the Tel Dan Stele (V23). **The reading depends on a restored letter and remains disputed.**

## Symbology

**Ḥerem: devotion to destruction.** Mesha says he killed the inhabitants of captured towns and **"devoted" them to his god**, using the same word, ***ḥerem***, that the Bible uses for the conquest traditions of Joshua. The stele shows that this **total-war theology** was **shared** across the region, not unique to Israel.

**The stone as monument.** A royal inscription in the god's sanctuary is **a message to the god as much as to people**. It is a record of fulfilled duty, set up in the precinct of Chemosh at Dibon.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **Moabite basalt stele**, c. **840 BCE**, found at **Dhiban** in **1868**, shattered in **1869**, and **reconstructed** from pieces and **Clermont-Ganneau's squeeze** (Louvre). It records **Mesha's revolt** against the **Omride** kings of Israel, credits **Chemosh**, and mentions the **vessels of YHWH**.
- It parallels **2 Kings 3** in its main facts.

**What is not supported**

- Treating the stele as a neutral historical record. Like the biblical account, it is **royal propaganda** with a theological frame.

**What's genuinely open**

- The **"House of David"** reading of line 31, and the precise **chronology** of the revolt relative to 2 Kings.
