# The Dresden Codex: The Maya Book of Venus

*Vault entry V31 · Manuscripts · SLUB Dresden, Mscr.Dresd.R.310 · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **1739** **Johann Christian Götze**, director of the Royal Library in Dresden, bought from a private owner in **Vienna** a folded strip of painted bark-paper, covered in pictures and signs no one in Europe could read. Nobody knows how it reached Vienna, though a gift sent to the Habsburg court after the conquest of Mexico is one guess. In **February 1945**, in the firebombing of Dresden, the vault where it was kept was flooded, and some pages were **water-damaged**. It survived. It is the finest of the **four Maya books** known to have outlasted the Spanish conquest, when friars burned such books as works of idolatry.

## The book

- A **screenfold**: a long strip of **fig-bark paper** (*huun*) coated with fine **lime plaster**, folded like a fan into **39 leaves** painted on both sides. It is about **3.5 metres** long when unfolded.
- Painted in **red, black, blue** and other colours by several scribes, in **Maya hieroglyphs** with **bar-and-dot numerals** and pictures of the gods.
- Usually dated between the **11th and 14th centuries**. It may be a copy of older material, and its date is debated.
- The other three: the **Madrid** and **Paris** codices, and the **Maya Codex of Mexico** (formerly the Grolier Codex), whose authenticity was long disputed and was confirmed by Mexican authorities in 2018.

## What is in it

- **Almanacs** for the **260-day ritual count** (*tzolkʼin*), giving the gods and omens for days, in fields such as medicine, farming, weaving and marriage.
- **The Venus table** (pages 24 and 46–50). It follows the **584-day cycle of Venus** through five appearances, which add up to **eight 365-day years**. It repeats until the three counts, of Venus, the 260-day count and the 365-day year, all come back into step after **104 years**. The table divides each cycle into **236** days as morning star, **90** hidden, **250** as evening star and **8** hidden. These are **canonical** numbers, chosen to make the calendar work, not direct observations.
- **The eclipse table** (pages 51–58). It lists **405 lunar months**, about 33 years, grouped to mark the seasons when **eclipses were possible**.
- **Rain and the Chaak gods**, **new-year ceremonies**, and a closing page showing a **flood** pouring from the sky and from the mouth of a celestial caiman. It has been read as a world-destruction scene, and that reading is debated.

The German librarian **Ernst Förstemann** worked out in the **1880s** how the codex's numbers and calendars ran, and identified the Venus table, decades before the glyphs themselves could be read. The decipherment of Maya writing as a **logo-syllabic** script, largely after the 1950s, now lets most of its texts be read.

## The fear in the table

The Venus pages do more than count. Each first rising of Venus as morning star is paired with a picture of an **armed god spearing a victim**, such as a jaguar or the maize god. Maya belief held that the planet's heliacal rising was **dangerous**, and the table told priests **when** to expect the danger and **whom** it would strike. It is **astronomy in the service of divination**, as it was across the ancient world.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Bar, dot and shell.** Maya numbers are written with **dots for one**, **bars for five** and a **shell for zero**, stacked in places of increasing value. In calendar counts the third place is worth **360** instead of 400, to approach the solar year. The Maya had a true positional **zero** centuries before it reached Europe. The interactive object uses this notation.

**Colour as meaning.** The scribes wrote in red and black. Among the Aztecs the phrase *in tlilli, in tlapalli*, "the black, the red", meant writing and wisdom, and the Maya pairing of the two inks is often compared with it. Blue-green, the colour of water, jade and maize, marks the rain gods.

**Day-signs and gods.** Each day of the 260-day count combines a **number (1–13)** with one of **20 named day-signs**, and each carries its patron gods. The almanacs are grids of these combinations.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **pre-conquest Maya screenfold** in Dresden since **1739**, damaged but surviving in **1945**.
- It contains a **Venus table** built on a **584-day** cycle and **eclipse-season tables**, both of real astronomical accuracy.

**What is not supported**

- Claims that the codex **predicts the end of the world** in 2012. The "2012" idea came from a Long Count date on monuments, not from this codex, and Maya texts do not predict the world's end then.
- Claims that its astronomy needed **outside** (ancient-alien or Old World) knowledge. It is the product of centuries of recorded Maya observation.

**What's genuinely open**

- **When and where** the codex was painted (proposals include Chichén Itzá and a date around 1200–1250), and how much of it is copied from older books.
- **How it reached Europe.**
- The meaning of the **flood page**.
