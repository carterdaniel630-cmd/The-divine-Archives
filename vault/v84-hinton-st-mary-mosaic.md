# The Hinton St Mary Mosaic: A Face Before the Chi-Rho

*Vault entry V84 · Relics & Artifacts · Hinton St Mary, Dorset, 4th century CE · British Museum, London · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **1963**, in the Dorset village of **Hinton St Mary**, the blacksmith **Walter White** was digging a post-hole when his spade struck a floor of small coloured stones. Excavation uncovered the mosaic pavement of two rooms of a Roman building. In the middle of the larger room was a roundel: the head and shoulders of a clean-shaven, fair-haired man in a cloak, and behind his head the monogram **☧**, the **Chi-Rho**, the first two Greek letters of *Christos*. On either side of him hangs a **pomegranate**. If the face is Christ's, as most scholars think, it is one of the earliest known pictures of him anywhere, and the earliest from Britain.

## The object

- A mosaic floor from a Roman villa or building, dated on grounds of style to the **fourth century CE**, and attributed to a workshop based at **Durnovaria** (Dorchester).
- Lifted in 1965 and acquired by the **British Museum**. For years only the **central medallion** has been on display; the rest was stored, and the museum has since led new fieldwork at the site.
- It covered two rooms joined by an opening: a smaller one with **Bellerophon on Pegasus killing the Chimera**, and a larger one with the Chi-Rho bust, surrounded by hunting scenes and four corner busts, sometimes read as the **winds** or the **four evangelists**.

## Who is the man?

- The usual reading: **Christ**, with the Chi-Rho as his halo-like emblem.
- An alternative: the emperor **Constantine** or another Christian ruler, the Chi-Rho being Constantine's battle standard.
- Some have also asked why an image of Christ would be placed on a **floor**, to be walked on; later Christian law (427 CE) forbade putting the sign of Christ on pavements.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The Chi-Rho.** Two letters, Χ and Ρ, superimposed: a name turned into an emblem, which Constantine used after 312 and which became one of the commonest Christian signs of the fourth century.

**Pomegranates.** In Greek myth the fruit of **Persephone** and return from the dead; for Christians it could signal **eternal life** and resurrection.

**Bellerophon.** A pagan hero killing a monster; in a Christian house it may stand for **good defeating evil**. The mosaic mixes the two vocabularies in the way fourth-century Christians often did.

## Connections

Early Christianity (ch16), with Roman Mystery Cults (ch18) and Rome (ch13) for the pagan imagery it borrows. Compare other early images claimed to show Christ: the Shroud of Turin (V04), the Mandylion (V57) and the Veil of Veronica (V25).

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- The mosaic was found in **1963** at Hinton St Mary and is held by the **British Museum**.
- It is fourth-century, shows a bust in front of a **Chi-Rho** flanked by **pomegranates**, and a separate **Bellerophon and Chimera** panel.

**What is not supported**

- That the face records what Jesus looked like. It follows Roman portrait conventions of the fourth century, three centuries after his life.

**What's genuinely open**

- Whether the bust is **Christ** or an emperor such as **Constantine**.
- What the building was: a villa's dining room, a house church, or a room of mixed use.
