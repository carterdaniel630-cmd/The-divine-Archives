# The Tel Dan Stele: "House of David" in Stone

*Vault entry V23 · Texts & Tablets · The Israel Museum, Jerusalem · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **July 1993**, at the excavation of **Tel Dan** in far northern Israel, the surveyor **Gila Cook** noticed a piece of basalt built into a later wall. On it were letters in **Old Aramaic**. The excavation's director, **Avraham Biran**, published it within months. In **1994** two more fragments turned up. Together they form part of a **victory inscription** set up by a king of **Aram-Damascus**, almost certainly **Hazael**, in the **late ninth century BCE**. He boasts of killing kings of **Israel** and of **bytdwd**: in the reading most scholars accept, the "**House of David**". It was the first time the name **David** had been found **outside the Bible**, in an inscription less than two centuries after the time the Bible places him.

## The object and its text

- **Three basalt fragments** (A, B1 and B2) of a stele in **Old Aramaic**, written in the **Phoenician-derived alphabet**. They are dated on archaeological and palaeographic grounds to the **later ninth century BCE**.
- The surviving lines speak of the king's father's death, of a campaign in which the king **killed** a "[…]ram son of […] **king of Israel**" and a "[…]iah son of […] **of the House of David**". Scholars commonly restore these names as **Joram (Jehoram) of Israel** and **Ahaziah of Judah**. **These restorations are reconstructions.** The surviving letters are partial.
- **2 Kings 9** attributes the deaths of Joram and Ahaziah to the Israelite usurper **Jehu**, not to Hazael. **The discrepancy is itself debated.** Some suggest Jehu acted as Hazael's ally or agent. Others see two rival claims to the same killings.

## The "bytdwd" debate

- **Majority reading.** ***bytdwd*** means "**House of David**", the name of the **dynasty of Judah**, just as Assyrian texts call Israel "House of Omri".
- **Minority readings**, mostly from the 1990s. Because the word is written **without a word-divider** in the middle, some read it as a **place name**, as the name of a **temple** ("house of Dod", a deity or epithet), or as other words. Another minority view held that the fragments were **misassigned or forged**.
- **Where it stands.** Most epigraphers and historians now accept "House of David". This **does not prove** the biblical portrait of David. It shows that **a dynasty named after a David ruled Judah in the ninth century**.

## Symbology

**A dynasty named for its founder.** "House of X" is the standard ancient Near Eastern way of naming a **royal line**. When a foreign enemy uses that formula for Judah, it shows how the kingdom was **known abroad**.

**Victory in the gods' name.** Like the Mesha Stele (V22), the inscription credits a **god**, here **Hadad**, who "went before me". The **same theology of divine warfare** runs through the whole region.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- **Three Old Aramaic basalt fragments** found at **Tel Dan in 1993–94**, from a **late-ninth-century BCE** victory stele probably of **Hazael of Damascus**, mentioning a **king of Israel** and **bytdwd**.

**What is not supported**

- That the inscription **proves** the biblical account of **David's life and empire**. It attests a **dynasty's name**, not the stories.
- That the fragments are **forgeries**. They were excavated in a controlled dig.

**What's genuinely open**

- The **restored names** (Joram and Ahaziah), and **who killed them**: Hazael, as the stele claims, or Jehu, as 2 Kings claims.
- A small minority still disputes the "**House of David**" reading.
