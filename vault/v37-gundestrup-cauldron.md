# The Gundestrup Cauldron: Gods in Silver from a Danish Bog

*Vault entry V37 · Relics & Objects · National Museum of Denmark, Copenhagen · Recently added — pending full review.*

On **28 May 1891** peat-cutters in a small bog called **Rævemose**, near **Gundestrup** in the north of Jutland, struck metal. Under the peat lay a great **silver bowl**, and stacked inside it a set of **silver plates** crowded with **gods, beasts and warriors**. Someone had **taken the cauldron apart** before it was laid in the bog, placing the rim and the decorated plates in the bottom of the bowl. It is the **largest known piece of European Iron Age silverwork**.

## The object

- About **69 cm across** and **42 cm high**, weighing almost **9 kg**, of silver, **gilded** in places. It is made of **13 plates**: **five inner** plates, **seven outer** plates (an **eighth** is missing), and a **round base medallion**, fitted to a plain curved bowl.
- It was made by **several silversmiths**, working by **repoussé** (hammering from behind). The busts' eyes once held **glass**.
- Most specialists date it between about **150 BCE and the early first century CE**, the pre-Roman Iron Age.

## The images

**Inside** are the famous scenes. An **antlered figure** sits cross-legged holding a **torc** and a **ram-horned serpent**, among stags and other animals. A bearded god holds a **wheel**. A goddess appears between **wheels**, with **elephants** and **griffins**. **Three bulls** face men with swords. On the most discussed plate, **foot-soldiers** with long **carnyx** war-horns march in one direction while **horsemen** ride above them in the other, and a **giant figure** holds a man **head-down over a vat**.

**Outside**, great **busts of gods and goddesses** grasp smaller human figures and animals. **At the bottom** of the bowl, a **bull** collapses beneath a figure with a sword.

## Celtic, Thracian, or both?

Almost everything about the cauldron's origin is argued.

- **Celtic** elements: the **torcs**, the **carnyx** horns, the helmets and the shield types match finds from the Celtic world. The antlered figure closely resembles the god named **Cernunnos** on a Gaulish monument from Paris, the Pillar of the Boatmen. Scholars such as **Garrett Olmsted** read the scenes as Gaulish myth.
- **Thracian** workmanship: the **silversmithing techniques**, the style of the animals, and the **elephants** and griffins point to the **lower Danube**, in modern Bulgaria and Romania, where Thracian silverwork flourished. **Timothy Taylor** and others argued that Thracian smiths made it, perhaps for Celtic patrons.
- **How it reached Denmark** is unknown: as booty, a gift, a trade good, or carried by migrating peoples such as the **Cimbri**, who came from Jutland and raided southwards in the late second century BCE.

Most scholars now see it as **the product of contact**, a Thracian-style object bearing Celtic imagery, and do not think it has to be one or the other.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The antlered god.** The seated figure with antlers is the best-known image of what is called **Cernunnos**. The name comes from one damaged inscription, and whether the cauldron's figure bore it is **not known**. The torc and serpent recur with antlered figures in Gaulish art.

**The vat.** The scene of a man held over a vat has been read as **human sacrifice by drowning**, which a later Roman scholium on Lucan links to the god Teutates. It has also been read as a **cauldron of rebirth**, like the one in the Welsh *Mabinogion* that brings dead warriors back to life. Both are **modern readings** of an image without text.

**Deposition in water.** Deliberately **dismantling** the cauldron and **placing it in a wet place** matches a long northern European tradition of **offering valuable objects** to the powers of lakes and bogs. The same tradition lies behind the **bog bodies** of the region.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **gilded silver cauldron** of **13 plates**, found **dismantled** in the **Rævemose** bog in **1891**, made c. **150 BCE–1st century CE**, now in the **National Museum of Denmark**.
- Its imagery combines **Celtic** equipment and motifs with **Thracian-style** silverwork and **exotic animals**.

**What is not supported**

- That the plates show **named** gods. The cauldron carries **no inscription**. Names such as Cernunnos and Taranis are **modern identifications** by comparison.
- That it proves a **single myth** such as the Welsh cauldron of rebirth. Such links are interpretive.

**What's genuinely open**

- **Where** and **by whom** it was made, and for whom.
- **How** it came to Denmark, and why it was **dismantled** and **deposited**.
- The meaning of the **vat scene** and the order of the plates.
