# The Pilate Stone: The Prefect in Stone

*Vault entry V44 · Texts & Tablets · The Israel Museum, Jerusalem · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **June 1961** an Italian team led by **Antonio Frova** was excavating the Roman **theatre** at **Caesarea Maritima**, the port city Herod the Great had built on the coast of Judaea. The archaeologist **Maria Teresa Fortuna Canivet** turned over a limestone block that had been used as a **step** in a later rebuilding of the theatre. Its underside carried four broken lines of Latin, and in the second line was a name known from every Christian creed: **PILATVS**.

## The inscription

- A limestone block about **82 × 65 cm**, cut down when it was reused, so that the **left side** of every line is missing.
- The surviving text, with standard restorations in brackets:

> [ . . . ]S TIBERIEVM
> [ . . . PO]NTIVS PILATVS
> [ . . . PRAEF]ECTVS IVDA[EA]E
> [ . . . FE]CIT D[E . . . ]

- It records that **Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea**, built or dedicated a **Tiberieum**, a structure named in honour of the emperor **Tiberius**.
- Pilate governed Judaea from **26 to 36 CE**, so the stone dates from those years. The original is in the **Israel Museum**; a replica stands at Caesarea.

## Prefect, not procurator

The Roman historian **Tacitus**, writing around 116, calls Pilate a ***procurator***. So does most later tradition. The stone shows that in his own time Pilate's title was ***praefectus***, a military rank. The governors of Judaea became procurators under Claudius, after Pilate's day. Tacitus used the title of his own time. The Gospels simply call him "governor" (Greek *hēgemōn*).

## What was the Tiberieum?

The first line is the most uncertain. Proposals include:

- a **temple** for the cult of the emperor, which Pilate, as a loyal official, would promote;
- the epigrapher **Géza Alföldy's** restoration ***[Nautis] Tiberieum***, "a Tiberieum for sailors": a **lighthouse** or harbour structure at Caesarea's port.

Neither restoration can be proven from the surviving letters.

## Pilate beyond the stone

Pilate appears in the four **Gospels**, in **Josephus** and **Philo**, who describe him as provocative and harsh, and in **Tacitus**, who notes that Christ "suffered the extreme penalty" under him. The stone adds no new events. What it gives is **independent, contemporary confirmation** of his office and name. A **copper ring** found at Herodium, inscribed "of Pilatus" and read with new imaging in **2018**, may also be connected with him, although *Pilatus* was not a unique name.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Honouring the emperor.** Naming a building after Tiberius was a political and religious act. In the Roman world, loyalty to the emperor was expressed through **cult**. In Judaea this collided with Jewish law against images, and Josephus and Philo record Pilate provoking protests by bringing **military standards** and **gilded shields** into Jerusalem.

**Reuse as erasure.** Cutting a governor's dedication down to serve as a stair shows how quickly monuments to officials lost their meaning. It also shows that the stone survived only by accident.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **Latin dedication** found at **Caesarea** in **1961** naming **Pontius Pilate** as ***praefectus* of Judaea** and a **Tiberieum**. It is authentic, from an excavated context, and dates to **26–36 CE**.
- It corrects the later title ***procurator*** for Pilate's time.

**What is not supported**

- That it proves any **Gospel event**. It confirms Pilate's existence and office, which other ancient sources also attest.

**What's genuinely open**

- **What the Tiberieum was**: a temple, a lighthouse, or something else.
- The wording of the **first and last lines**.
