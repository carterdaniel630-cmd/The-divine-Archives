# The Mawangdui Silk Texts: The Laozi Before It Was Edited

*Vault entry V81 · Manuscripts · Tomb 3, Mawangdui, Changsha, sealed in 168 BCE · Hunan Museum, Changsha · Recently added — pending full review.*

In **December 1973**, archaeologists working at **Mawangdui**, on the edge of **Changsha** in Hunan province, opened the third of a group of Han dynasty tombs belonging to the family of the **Marquis of Dai**. The man buried there, generally identified as a son of the marquis **Li Cang**, had died in **168 BCE**. Inside a lacquered box lay rolls and folded sheets of **silk** covered in writing: some thirty texts on philosophy, medicine, astronomy, divination and war. Among them were **two copies of the *Laozi***, the book later called the ***Daodejing***, now known as **Laozi A** and **Laozi B**. They were, at the time, the oldest known manuscripts of the text by more than five hundred years.

## What the manuscripts showed

- **Order.** In both copies the section on **De ("virtue", "power")** comes **before** the section on **Dao ("the way")**. The received book begins with the Dao; the Mawangdui copies would be called, in that order, a *Dedaojing*.
- **The opening line.** The received text begins "*Dao ke dao, fei chang dao*", "the way that can be spoken of is not the constant way". The silk copies read "**道可道也，非恒道也**", *dao ke dao ye, fei **heng** dao ye*, with *heng*, "constant", where later editions have *chang*. The substitution is usually explained as avoidance of the personal name of **Emperor Wen of Han, Liu Heng** (r. 180–157 BCE), whose name could not be written; the added particles *ye* also change how the line can be read.
- **Two hands.** **Laozi A** is written in a script still close to the older seal forms and does not avoid the name of the dynasty's founder; **Laozi B** is in the **clerical script** of the Han and does avoid it, so it was copied later, probably under the first Han emperors.

## Not the oldest after all

In **1993** a tomb at **Guodian** in Hubei yielded bamboo slips of about **300 BCE** carrying portions of the *Laozi*, about a third of the received text in a different order. Mawangdui remains the oldest **complete** text of the book; Guodian shows that the *Laozi* was still being assembled in the late Warring States period.

## The other texts

The tomb also held the "**Four Classics of the Yellow Emperor**", a *Yijing* (Book of Changes) with commentaries, medical and sexual-cultivation manuals, star charts including a list of **comets**, maps, and the *Daoyin tu*, a painted chart of exercises. Tomb 1, of the marquis's wife **Xin Zhui**, held her remarkably preserved body and a famous painted silk banner showing the passage to the afterlife.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The taboo name.** The swap of *heng* for *chang* is a small case of a large Chinese practice: a ruler's personal name was sacred and could not be written, so texts were altered around it, and editors can date copies by which names they avoid.

**Heng: the constant.** The word the taboo hid, *heng*, is also the name of a hexagram of the *Yijing* ("Duration"), and the "constant Dao" is the unchanging source that cannot be named.

**Silk.** Writing on silk was costly and prestigious; burying such a library placed learning among the goods the dead would need.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- Tomb 3 at Mawangdui was sealed in **168 BCE** and excavated in **1973**; it held two silk manuscripts of the *Laozi*, **A** and **B**.
- In both, the **De** section precedes the **Dao** section, and they read ***heng*** where the received text has ***chang***.
- The **Guodian** slips (c. 300 BCE, found 1993) are older but partial.

**What is not supported**

- That the received *Daodejing* is a fixed text going back unchanged to a single author, Laozi. The manuscripts show it was arranged and edited over time.

**What's genuinely open**

- Whether the De-first order is original or a Han arrangement.
- Exactly when Laozi A was copied, and the identity of the man in Tomb 3.
