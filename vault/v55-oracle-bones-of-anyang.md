# The Oracle Bones of Anyang: Questions to the Ancestors

*Vault entry V55 · Texts & Tablets · Academia Sinica, Taipei; institutions in China and worldwide*

In Chinese pharmacies of the late nineteenth century one could buy **"dragon bones"**, fragments of old bone and shell ground into medicine. In **1899** the scholar **Wang Yirong** noticed that some of them bore **carved signs** that looked like a very ancient form of Chinese writing. Collectors traced the bones to farmland near **Anyang** in Henan. From **1928** archaeologists excavating there found the royal capital of the **late Shang dynasty**, called **Yinxu**, "the ruins of Yin". They found the palaces and tombs of kings, and pits holding tens of thousands of inscribed **turtle shells** and **cattle shoulder-blades**. They are the earliest substantial body of **Chinese writing**, and they are almost all records of **divination**.

## How divination worked

The interactive object above follows the steps:

1. A **turtle plastron** (the flat under-shell) or an ox **scapula** was cleaned, polished and cut with rows of **hollows** on the back.
2. The diviner spoke the **charge**, a statement put to the spirits, often in paired forms: "we will receive harvest" / "we may not receive harvest".
3. A hot brand was pressed into a hollow. The bone **cracked** on the front in a ⊢ shape, the origin of the character **卜** (*bu*), "to divine".
4. The king **read the cracks**.
5. Engravers carved the **date, diviner's name and charge** beside the crack, sometimes the king's **prognostication**, and occasionally the **verification**: what actually happened.

## What the kings asked

Around **150,000** pieces are known, most from the reigns of about nine kings, especially **Wu Ding** (c. 1250–1192 BCE). They asked about:

- **Sacrifices** to the royal **ancestors**, and to the high god **Di**: which ancestor, how many cattle, sheep or **human victims**.
- **Harvests, rain and weather.**
- **War**, hunting and travel.
- **Illness, dreams** and **childbirth**, including dozens of divinations about the consort **Fu Hao**, whose unlooted tomb was found at Yinxu in **1976**.

The world they reveal is one where the living king mediated between the people and powerful, sometimes dangerous **ancestors**, who had to be fed and consulted.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Writing born in ritual.** The oracle bones show Chinese writing already developed, with thousands of distinct characters, used in a **religious** setting: communication with the spirits. Many characters can be traced directly to their modern forms.

**The crack as answer.** The spirits answered not in words but in **cracks**. The character 卜 draws the crack itself, and 占 (*zhan*, "to prognosticate") adds a mouth: speaking the reading of the crack.

**Pairs and balance.** Posing each question in **positive and negative** forms reflects a way of thinking in complementary pairs, sometimes compared with later **yin–yang** thought. That link is **interpretive**.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- Inscribed **turtle plastrons and cattle scapulae** from **Yinxu** (Anyang), c. **1250–1050 BCE**, recording royal **pyromantic divination**, identified as writing in **1899** and excavated from **1928**.
- They are the **oldest large corpus of Chinese writing** and show a religion centred on **royal ancestors** and sacrifice, including **human sacrifice**.

**What is not supported**

- That Chinese writing **began** with the oracle bones. The script is already mature, implying an earlier development that has not survived (perhaps on perishable materials).

**What's genuinely open**

- **How writing developed** before the late Shang.
- How far the oracle-bone religion was shared **beyond the royal court**.
