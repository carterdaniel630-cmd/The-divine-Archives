# The Kumulipo: The Hawaiian Song of Creation

*Vault entry V69 · Texts & Tablets · Printed 1889; translated 1897*

In **1895**, after a failed rising to restore her to the throne, **Queen Liliʻuokalani**, the last monarch of the Hawaiian Kingdom, was held prisoner in a room of **ʻIolani Palace** in Honolulu. She spent part of her confinement translating a chant her brother, **King Kalākaua**, had printed in **1889**: the **Kumulipo**, the genealogy of her line from the beginning of the world. Her English version was published in **1897**. It begins in heat and darkness: *"At the time when the earth became hot, at the time when the heavens turned about..."*

## The chant

- A genealogical chant (*koʻihonua*) of **2,102 lines**, divided into **sixteen wā**, "ages" or "eras".
- It was composed in the **eighteenth century** in honour of the chief **Kalaninuiamamao** and handed down orally in his family, which led to Kalākaua and Liliʻuokalani.
- The first **seven wā** belong to *pō*, the night: out of darkness are born, in order, the **coral polyp**, the creatures of the sea, the plants of the land, insects, birds and animals, each paired with its counterpart.
- With the **eighth wā** comes *ao*, daylight: the first humans and the gods. The later wā trace generations of chiefs, ending with the line of Kalaninuiamamao.

## Why it mattered

Printing the Kumulipo was political. Kalākaua, whose family was not of the old royal line, published it to show his descent from the gods and the first chiefs, at a time when American business interests were pressing on his kingdom. Liliʻuokalani's translation, made in captivity after the monarchy was overthrown in **1893**, presented her people's learning to the outside world. Later translations include those of **Martha Beckwith** (1951) and the scholarship of Hawaiian writers since.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**Creation as genealogy.** In the Kumulipo the world is not made; it is **born**. Every creature is a relative, and the chief at the end of the chant is kin to the coral, the fish and the stars. Reciting the genealogy was a way of affirming that kinship and that rank.

**Night and day.** The division between *pō* (night) and *ao* (day) organises the whole chant: the long darkness of growth, then the light of gods and people.

**Paired births.** Each creature of the sea is born alongside a creature of the land that shares part of its name, for example sea and land plants. Scholars read this pairing as a way of ordering nature through language.

**Evolution?** Because the chant moves from simple sea life to complex land life, some writers have compared it with **evolution**. The comparison is a modern reading; the chant's own logic is genealogy.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A **2,102-line** genealogical chant in **sixteen wā**, printed by **Kalākaua** in **1889** and translated by **Liliʻuokalani**, published **1897**.
- Its association with the chief **Kalaninuiamamao** and the royal line.

**What is not supported**

- That the chant is an early statement of **scientific evolution**. The resemblance is a modern comparison.

**What's genuinely open**

- How much the chant changed in oral transmission before it was written down.
- The meaning of many of its names and lines, on which translators differ.
