# Chapter 51 — Sacrifice & the Scapegoat: The Gift of Blood

*Recently added — pending full review.*

To make something *sacred* — the word literally means "to make holy" — the ancient world gave it up: burned it, poured it out, killed it, drove it into the desert. Sacrifice is one of the most universal of all religious acts and one of the most troubling, and running through it is a single dark and durable idea: that the many can be spared by loading their guilt onto one, and sending that one away to die.

## Two goats and a scarlet thread

On the tenth day of the seventh month, the Day of Atonement, the high priest of Israel took two identical goats and cast lots over them. One lot was marked "for the LORD" — that goat was slaughtered on the altar, its blood carried into the Holy of Holies. The other lot was marked "for **Azazel**," and over its head the high priest confessed all the sins of the people, laying them onto the animal; then, according to the tradition preserved in the Mishnah, a band of **scarlet wool** was tied to it and it was led out into the wilderness and driven over a cliff, carrying the year's guilt away with it. This is the original **scapegoat** — the *goat that escapes* (in Tyndale's coinage), the ʿez ʾōzēl of Leviticus 16 — and it is the sharpest image of a logic found across the ancient world: that the impurity of the community can be gathered onto a single victim and expelled, and that in its going the people are made clean.

## The gift, the smoke, and the shared meal

Before the scapegoat there is the simpler and vaster fact of *offering*. Nearly every ancient religion gave gifts to its gods, and the words reveal what people thought they were doing. The Hebrew **korban** means "that which is brought near" — sacrifice as *approach*, a way to close the distance to God. The Greek **thysia** means "to make smoke" — the sweet savor rising to the Olympians. The Vedic **yajña** is the pouring of offerings into the sacred **fire**, whose god Agni carries them to heaven. And the old Latin tag names the transaction plainly: ***do ut des***, "I give so that you may give."

But sacrifice was rarely only a bribe. Two other readings run alongside the gift. In one — associated with the scholar **W. Robertson Smith** — the heart of sacrifice is the **shared meal**: the victim is consumed *together* by the god and the worshippers, and the eating binds them into one community, a *communion*. In another, the offering *feeds* the god or sustains the cosmos itself. The Greeks split the difference ritually: an **Olympian** sacrifice (*thysia*) burned the god's portion — the fat and thigh-bones, the smoke — and the people feasted on the meat; but a **chthonic** offering to the powers below (*sphagia*, the holocaust) was burned or buried *whole*, given up entirely, not shared. What you did with the body encoded what you believed the gods were.

## The blood on the altar

At the center of many sacrificial systems stood **blood**. In the Israelite Temple cult (ch07, ch10) the blood of the victim was dashed against the altar because, as Leviticus puts it, "the life of the flesh is in the blood... it is the blood that makes atonement." Blood was life, and life given back to its giver was the most potent offering of all. The whole apparatus of the Jerusalem Temple — the daily lambs, the sin-offerings and peace-offerings, the priestly courses — was an engine for the ordered, sanctioned shedding of blood, and its abrupt end with the Temple's destruction in 70 CE forced one of the great transformations in religious history (below).

## The ram in the thicket

One of the deepest currents in the history of sacrifice is the **substitution of an animal for a human being**. The Hebrew Bible dramatizes exactly this turn in the **Aqedah**, the Binding of Isaac (Genesis 22): Abraham is commanded to sacrifice his son, raises the knife — and is stopped, a **ram** caught in a thicket offered in the boy's place. Whatever else it is, the story marks a memory of, and a decisive turn away from, child sacrifice; the same logic runs through the **Passover lamb**, whose blood on the doorposts substitutes for the firstborn, and through the biblical law that the firstborn of Israel must be *redeemed* rather than offered. Across many cultures the trend of history runs the same way: from human victim, to animal, to the bloodless substitute of grain, money, or prayer.

## Human sacrifice, honestly

But the human victim was real, and honesty requires facing it. The best-documented case is the **Aztec** (ch29): human sacrifice was integral to their religion, understood as *repayment* — the gods had shed their own blood to create the world and to keep the sun moving, and humans owed blood in return to sustain the cosmos. The scale is debated — some Spanish colonial figures were clearly propaganda, and revisionist scholars stress the exaggeration — but the practice itself is beyond doubt, attested archaeologically (the great skull-rack, the *tzompantli*, has been excavated at Tenochtitlan). Other cases must be weighed more carefully: the **Carthaginian** *tophet* and the biblical horror of child sacrifice "to Molech" remain genuinely **contested**, with scholars still divided over whether the tophet cemeteries evidence ritual child-killing or the burial of children who died naturally; the **Celtic** "bog bodies" of northern Europe show signs of ritualized death but resist certain interpretation; and the **Shang** kings of early China (ch09) were buried with sacrificed retainers, documented in the oracle bones and the royal tombs. The archive's rule holds especially hard here: state what the evidence shows, mark what is disputed, and neither sensationalize nor explain away.

## The scapegoat mechanism

The Israelite scapegoat had a Greek cousin: the **pharmakos**, a human victim — often a slave, a criminal, or a beggar — who, in times of plague, famine, or crisis, was ritually expelled or killed to carry away the city's pollution. The pattern of the *loaded and banished victim* was widespread enough that two twentieth-century thinkers built sweeping theories on it. **René Girard** argued that human desire is **mimetic** — we want what others want — which breeds rivalry and escalating violence, until a community unconsciously discharges its tension by uniting against a single **scapegoat**, whose killing restores peace; religion, on this view, grew up to ritually *repeat* that founding murder, and (Girard controversially added) the Gospels expose and break the mechanism by siding with the innocent victim. The classicist **Walter Burkert**, in *Homo Necans* ("Man the Killer," 1972), rooted sacrifice instead in the **guilt and bonding of the hunt** — the communal kill, ritualized. Both are powerful, influential, and *contested*: critics, and each of the two about the other, doubt the claim to a single **universal** origin, and much of the evidence is read differently by others. They are theories about sacrifice, offered here as such, not settled findings.

## The sacrifice that ends sacrifice

Two of the world's largest traditions resolved the problem of blood by transforming it. **Christianity** (ch16) took the sacrificial system and declared it *fulfilled and finished*: Christ is the "**Lamb of God**," the one perfect, final sacrifice whose death ends the need for any other, and the **Eucharist** re-presents that one offering rather than adding new ones — sacrifice sublimated into a shared sacred meal that loops back to Robertson Smith's communion. **Judaism**, after the Temple's destruction left it with no altar, replaced sacrifice with **prayer, study, and deeds of loving-kindness** — "the service of the heart" (ch19). Elsewhere the bloodless turn took other forms: much of **Hinduism** shifted from Vedic animal sacrifice toward the offerings of **pūjā** and **bhakti** (ch30), though animal sacrifice persists in some traditions (notably to Kali); and **Jainism** and **Buddhism** (ch11, ch52) rejected Vedic blood-sacrifice outright in the name of ***ahiṃsā***, non-violence. And in the sacrificial cosmogonies of ch46 — the cosmic **Puruṣa** and the giant **Ymir**, whose dismembered bodies *become* the world — sacrifice and creation are revealed as the same act: the world itself as the first offering.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

- **The altar.** The raised stone or table, often with **fire** or a channel for **blood**, is the encoded meeting-point of the human and divine worlds — the place where a gift crosses over. Its very shape (the horned altar, the fire-pit, the pyramid-top) marks the boundary being crossed.
- **The rising smoke.** The "sweet savor" ascending to heaven encodes the offering's *transfer* — the burned portion made invisible, translated from the human realm to the divine (the meaning folded into the Greek *thysia*, "to make smoke," and the Vedic fire).
- **The substitute and the scarlet thread.** The ram for Isaac, the lamb for the firstborn, the scapegoat's red wool: the encoding of *transfer of guilt or debt* onto a stand-in — one life accepted in place of another, one body carrying away the sins of many.
- **The shared meal.** Bread and wine, the sacrificial feast, the portions divided among god and worshippers: eating the offering encodes *communion*, the making of one body out of many — the symbol that Christianity placed at its very center.

## Connections

Sacrifice runs through the whole archive. Its blood-cult is the heart of the **Israelite Temple** and its transformation in **Rabbinic Judaism** (ch07, ch10, ch19); its fire-offering is the **Vedic** yajña (ch05); its human victim is the **Aztec** sun-cult and the **Shang** royal tombs (ch29, ch09); its scapegoat is the Greek **pharmakos** (ch08). It is bound to **sacred kingship** and the dying king (ch49), to the **dying-and-returning god**, and to **creation by sacrifice** (ch46); and it reaches its most consequential reinterpretation in the **Christian** Lamb of God and Eucharist (ch16). To ask what a people were willing to give up, and to whom, is to ask what they held most sacred of all.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** That sacrifice — the offering of animals, food, drink, and objects to the divine — was near-universal in ancient religion, with well-attested systems: the Israelite Temple cult, the Greek thysia/sphagia, the Vedic yajña, and many more. The Leviticus 16 scapegoat ritual and the Greek pharmakos are documented; the substitution of animal for human (the Aqedah, the Passover lamb) is a clear textual pattern. Aztec human sacrifice is beyond reasonable doubt, attested archaeologically (the excavated Templo Mayor tzompantli). The post-70-CE transformations of Judaism and the Christian reinterpretation of sacrifice are historically clear.

**Not supported (as established fact).** The universal-origin theories of **Girard** (the scapegoat mechanism as the root of all religion and culture) and **Burkert** (sacrifice from the hunt) are influential but **contested**, and their claims to universality are widely doubted. The *scale* of Aztec sacrifice was exaggerated in some colonial sources. And whether the Carthaginian **tophet** evidences systematic child sacrifice remains genuinely disputed among specialists.

**Genuinely open.** *Why* sacrifice is so universal — gift, communion, feeding the gods, discharge of communal violence, or some braid of all of these — is unresolved; the competing theories each capture part of a phenomenon that may have no single cause. The interpretation of ambiguous physical evidence (bog bodies, some infant burials) remains open. The archive documents the act, the texts, and the debate, and does not pretend to have found the one key to the knife.
