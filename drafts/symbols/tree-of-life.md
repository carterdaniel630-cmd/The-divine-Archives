# Symbol — The Tree of Life

*The ten-fold diagram of the hidden God, and the ancient sacred tree it was, and was not, descended from.*

## The gate of light

The most famous picture of the Tree of Life is younger than most people assume. In **1516**, in Augsburg, a Latin book called **Portae Lucis** ("Gates of Light") — the Christian Hebraist Paulus Ricius's translation of Joseph Gikatilla's Kabbalistic *Sha'arei Orah* (c. 1290) — carried on its title page a woodcut of a bearded scholar holding a diagram of **ten linked medallions**. It is the **first appearance in print** of the *Ilan ha-Sefirot*, the "Tree of the Sefirot" — the schematic Tree of Life familiar today from a thousand posters and pendants. The image is medieval and early-modern, not ancient; the idea beneath it is older; and the older sacred trees it is often merged with are older still, and not clearly the same thing at all. Keeping those three layers apart is the whole discipline of reading this symbol honestly.

## What the symbol is

In its precise sense, the **Tree of Life** (Hebrew *Etz Chaim*) is the central diagram of **Kabbalah**: a map of the **ten sefirot**, the emanations through which the infinite, unknowable God (*Ein Sof*) brings the cosmos into being and sustains it. The ten are arranged as spheres joined by **paths** and grouped into **three pillars** (mercy, severity, and the balancing middle), and read as a ladder the soul can climb back toward the divine. It is at once a diagram of God's inner life, of the structure of creation, and of the human being made in that image.

## Origins and history

- **The ancient sacred tree.** A stylized tree tended by winged genii and kings appears across the ancient Near East, most strikingly on **Assyrian palace reliefs** of the 9th–7th centuries BCE. Scholars have read it as fertility, as cosmic order (roots below, branches above), or as the king himself — but, crucially, **there is no consensus** that this Assyrian "sacred tree" carried the later "tree of life" *meaning*, and whether the concept as such existed in Mesopotamia is genuinely debated. The visual motif is old; the equation with the Kabbalistic Tree is a modern inference, not a documented lineage.
- **The Eden tree.** The Hebrew Bible names a **Tree of Life** in the Garden of Eden (Genesis 2–3), guarded after the expulsion — and it is a **different tree** from the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil beside it. This scriptural tree of eternal life is a source the Kabbalists drew on, but the sefirotic diagram is not "in" Genesis.
- **The sefirot and the diagram.** The doctrine of ten sefirot took shape in medieval Jewish mysticism (the *Sefer Yetzirah*, the *Bahir*, and the 13th-century *Zohar* — see chapter 26). The **tree-shaped diagram** of ten circles on three columns is a **development of that tradition**, crystallizing in late-medieval manuscripts and fixed in the public imagination by the **1516 Portae Lucis** print; earlier diagrams exist, but the canonical wiring (ten spheres, twenty-two connecting paths) was still being standardized at that date.

## How it is read

The Tree encodes a whole theology in its layout. The **ten sefirot** run from **Keter** (crown) at the top down to **Malkhut** (kingdom) at the base, each a distinct attribute of the divine — wisdom, understanding, loving-kindness, severity, beauty, and so on. The **three pillars** balance expansive and restraining forces around a reconciling center. The sefirot are also mapped onto the **four worlds** of Kabbalistic cosmology and onto the body of the primordial human, *Adam Kadmon*. The connecting **paths** (later numbered twenty-two, one per Hebrew letter) make the Tree a diagram of descent — how the One becomes the many — and of ascent, the mystic's route back toward *Ein Sof*.

## The modern afterlife

From the Renaissance on, the Tree left its Jewish setting and was rebuilt by others. **Christian Kabbalah** (Ricius, Reuchlin) read it as a witness to Christian doctrine. The 19th–20th-century **occult revival** absorbed it wholesale: the **Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn** mapped the **twenty-two tarot trumps onto the twenty-two paths** of the Tree, turning a card game into an encoded diagram of the soul's journey (see chapter 37), and this **Hermetic Qabalah** is the form most Westerners now meet. **Theosophy** and countless New Age systems carried it further. These adaptations are real, living, and influential — but they are **modern reconstructions**, and the "ancient Egyptian" or primordial pedigrees sometimes claimed for the tarot-and-Tree system are not historical.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported.** The Kabbalistic Tree of Life as a documented medieval-and-later tradition — the ten sefirot, the *Etz Chaim* diagram, its texts and its 1516 first printing in *Portae Lucis*; the biblical Tree of Life of Genesis; the Assyrian sacred-tree *motif* on the palace reliefs; and the modern Golden Dawn / Hermetic-Qabalah adaptation mapping tarot onto the paths.

**Not supported (as history).** A single unbroken "Tree of Life" descending from Assyria (or Eden) into Kabbalah: the Assyrian tree's *meaning* is undetermined and its identity with the later symbol is a modern inference, not an attested line of transmission. Also unsupported: the claimed ancient/Egyptian antiquity of the tarot-on-the-Tree system, which is a late-19th-century construction.

**Genuinely open.** What the Assyrian sacred tree actually meant to those who carved it; how much the Kabbalistic diagram owes to earlier iconography versus to fresh medieval systematization; and the theological claims of the symbol itself, which are matters of faith, recorded here as belief, not adjudicated.

## Connections

The Tree of Life sits near, but should not be collapsed into, several other tree symbols this archive treats. A **world-tree** or cosmic axis — Norse **Yggdrasil** (chapter 23), the Vedic and Mesoamerican world-trees — is primarily a structural pillar holding up the cosmos, a different idea from a *life*-giving or emanational tree, even where the images rhyme. The **Egyptian** sacred sycamore, the **Buddhist** Bodhi tree, and the biblical Eden tree are further relatives, each with its own documented meaning. Popular culture flattens all of these into one "Tree of Life"; the honest account keeps them adjacent but distinct.
