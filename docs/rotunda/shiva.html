SHIVA

Dark Bronze · India · The Great God in Meditation

Built From a Real Scan — Murudeshwar

Drag to turn him. He turns; the plinth stands still. Pinch or scroll to come closer.

About this figure

Shiva, the great god of India — destroyer and renewer in one figure, the still point at the heart of change. Here he sits in deep meditation on a tiger skin, legs crossed, spine straight, four arms at rest: the upper right hand lifts his trident, the upper left his little drum, whose beat is the rhythm of the worlds being made and unmade. A cobra rides at his neck with its hood raised beside him. When the river of heaven fell toward earth with a force that would have shattered it, Shiva caught her in his matted hair and let her flow down gently — the river Ganga is in his locks still, and the crescent moon rides there beside her. The dark beads at his chest are rudraksha seeds, and the brass vessel at his side holds water for his worship.

His worshippers tell of the poison. When gods and demons churned the ocean of milk for the nectar of immortality, the first thing to rise was a venom strong enough to end the world. Shiva drank it — and held it in his throat, swallowing nothing, spitting nothing out, so that the world would live. In the story the poison stained his throat blue, and he is Neelkanth, the Blue-Throated, ever since. That blue lives in the story, not on this statue: the figure here is finished in dark bronze, as a temple bronze is.

This figure is not our carving. It is a scan of the great statue of Shiva at Murudeshwar, in Karnataka — a living temple monument of our own century, among the tallest images of Shiva on earth, where he sits in this same meditation above the Arabian Sea. The statue was photographed and measured into this model by Boris3D Studio, and is shared under a Creative Commons Attribution licence: “Statue of Shiva in the lotus position at Murudeshwar” by Boris3D Studio, CC BY 3.0, via Thingiverse. What is real here is the scan's own stone — his form, his face, his tiger skin, even the tiger's face carved on its front. What is ours is the finish: one single dark bronze over the whole figure, as a temple statue is cast, with nothing painted on — his ornaments, his cobra and his tiger skin read by their carved form alone, and we say so plainly.

In the museum every figure is covered, and Shiva keeps his covering here as the statue gives it: the tiger skin across his lap and waist, the sacred thread across his chest. Nothing has been added to him and nothing taken away; the monument's own form stands as it was scanned.
