The Rotunda Circle · Statue — Head From a Real Scan, Body Made for It

Socrates of Athens (469–399 BCE), the questioner of Athens, tried and condemned by the city he would not stop questioning. He wrote nothing and built nothing; he spent his days in the streets and marketplaces asking the powerful what justice is, what courage is, what a good life is — and showing, question by question, that they did not know. In 399 BCE Athens charged him with impiety and corrupting the young and sentenced him to die by hemlock. Offered the chance to escape, he refused to run and refused to stop; he drank the cup among his friends, and their accounts of him became the root of Western philosophy.

No likeness of Socrates from life exists. The head of this figure follows the portrait tradition carved after his death — the snub nose, broad brow and great beard that every ancient copy keeps — and comes from the scan of that portrait held by SMK, the National Gallery of Denmark in Copenhagen, made with Scan the World and shared openly; the file reached the Archives in a print-ready form prepared by Kvant_64ru (CC BY). The body is a regular robed philosopher’s figure, made for this statue: no image of Socrates’ own body survives, so his head stands on a plain period body — an Athenian citizen’s heavy himation, worn the ordinary way.
