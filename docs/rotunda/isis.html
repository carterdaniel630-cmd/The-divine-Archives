ISIS

Black Stone & Gold · Egypt & Rome · The Throne-Bearer

A Modelled Rendition — Built To The Tradition

Drag to turn her. She turns; the plinth stands still. Pinch or scroll to come closer.

About this figure

Isis, whose name is the throne — the seat of kingship itself made goddess. Wife of Osiris, mother of Horus, the magician who learned the secret name of Ra and the mourner whose tears were said to swell the Nile. Her worship outgrew Egypt until her temples stood from Britain to Mesopotamia, and of all the old gods she was the last still worshipped, at Philae, into the sixth century.

She is a modelled rendition, built in the museum's workshop to the form the tradition gives her — not a scan of an ancient statue, and not presented as one. The famous standing figures of Isis, at Naples and elsewhere, have no openly shared scan, and we do not take what is not shared, nor invent an original and call it real. What stands here is our building, honestly named.

Her face is modelled on a real thing: a Roman bronze bust of Isis, hollow-cast in the second century CE, in the Hadrianic age, and only four and a half inches high in the bronze (Mount Holyoke College Art Museum, Accession Number MH 1965.10.C.G; the museum's photogrammetry by Laura Shea; the digital model prepared by 3DWP and shared under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence). The face is that bronze's, studied and followed; the head is finished in the same black stone as the body, so the figure reads as one carved statue. Everything below the neck is the museum's own carving.

She is wrought as the inlaid temple statues were wrought: one black stone throughout, the gold set into it as separate worked pieces — the horned sun-disc above her brow, the broad collar across her shoulders, her skirt, the knot of Isis at her breast. In her right hand she lifts the sistrum, the rattle whose sound was her presence arriving; in her left she carries the situla, the bucket of Nile water and milk offered in her temples.
