The James Ossuary: A Bone Box and a Disputed Line

Recently added · pending review

Held
Returned to its private owner after a criminal trial
Date evidence
Box: 1st century BCE – 1st century CE type; inscription disputed
Recently added · pending full review. This entry is live but has not yet completed the keeper’s review pass. It is sourced to the project’s standard, but wording and detail may still change. The tag is removed once the entry is cleared.

In October 2002, at a press conference in Washington, D.C., a limestone box about 50 centimetres long was presented to the world. It was an ossuary, a chest for bones of the kind Jews in and around Jerusalem used for secondary burial in the first century BCE and first century CE. Along one side ran a short Aramaic inscription: Ya'akov bar Yosef akhui di Yeshua, "James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus." If genuine, and if it referred to the Jesus of the Gospels, it would be the earliest physical reference to Jesus ever found. Within a year the Israel Antiquities Authority declared the inscription a forgery. Within two years the box's owner was on trial. The trial ended in 2012 with an acquittal that settled nothing about the inscription.

The inscription

Cut into the side of the limestone box, right to left. Touch a word for its meaning, and see which words are disputed.

יעקוב בר יוסף אחוי דישוע

James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus

    The object and the inscription

    • A limestone ossuary of the standard first-century type. The box itself is accepted as ancient.
    • The inscription, in Aramaic, carved into the side:

    יעקוב בר יוסף אחוי דישוע
    Ya'akov bar Yosef akhui di-Yeshua: James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus

    • The dispute centres on the second half, "brother of Jesus". Critics argued that it was added to a genuine but ordinary inscription ("James son of Joseph") and that a fake patina was applied to disguise the fresh cuts.

    What happened

    • 2002. The ossuary was published by the epigrapher André Lemaire in Biblical Archaeology Review and exhibited at the Royal Ontario Museum, where it cracked in transit.
    • 2003. An Israel Antiquities Authority committee concluded that the inscription was forged, citing its study of the patina.
    • 2004–2012. The owner, the collector Oded Golan, and others were tried in Jerusalem on forgery charges covering this and other artefacts. In March 2012 the judge acquitted him of forging the ossuary inscription. The judge found that the prosecution had not proved forgery beyond reasonable doubt, and stated explicitly that the verdict did not prove the inscription authentic. Golan was convicted of lesser charges relating to trading in antiquities.
    • Afterwards. The ossuary was returned to Golan. Scholarly opinion remains divided. Some epigraphers and geologists defend the whole inscription, and others maintain that the second half is modern.

    The statistical question

    Even if the whole inscription is ancient, James, Joseph and Jesus were all very common names in first-century Judaea. The identification with the family of Jesus of Nazareth therefore depends on probability arguments. One is that the unusual mention of a brother suggests the brother was notable. Those arguments are debated too.

    Symbology

    An inscription as a claim of identity. Ossuary inscriptions usually record name and father. Naming a brother is rare, and that rarity is exactly what makes this line either remarkable or suspicious. The text's significance lies entirely in one short phrase. The object shows how much weight a single line of writing can be made to carry.

    ✦ The evidence, honestly

    What's well supported by the evidence

    • A first-century-style limestone ossuary bearing the Aramaic inscription Ya'akov bar Yosef akhui di Yeshua. The IAA declared the inscription forged (2003). The owner was acquitted of forgery in 2012, with the court stating that the verdict did not establish authenticity.

    What is not supported

    • That the ossuary is proven to be that of James the brother of Jesus of Nazareth. It is equally unproven that the whole inscription is proven fake; the trial did not establish that.

    What's genuinely open

    • Whether the phrase "brother of Jesus" is ancient or modern, and, if ancient, whether it refers to Jesus of Nazareth.
