In a Vodou temple (ounfò) in Haiti, before the drums begin, the priest (oungan) or priestess (manbo) kneels on the earthen floor of the dance space, the peristil, beside the painted centre-post, the poto mitan. Taking a handful of cornmeal, they let it trickle between thumb and fingers, tracing an intricate design on the ground: a cross with a walking stick, serpents around a pole, a heart filled with lattice. Each design is a vèvè, the sign of a particular lwa (spirit). Offerings are placed on it, songs are sung to it, and as the ceremony goes on the dancers' feet blur it into the dust.

Traced in cornmeal

Choose a lwa and watch its vèvè drawn on the earthen floor.

  • Legba (keeper of the gate): Legba's vèvè is drawn first, to open the way between the living and the lwa. Its cross marks the crossroads; the cane is the old man's crutch.
  • Damballa (the serpent): Damballa, the great serpent of the sky and waters, often paired with his wife Ayida-Wedo, the rainbow. His vèvè shows serpents rising around a pole or an egg.
  • Ezili Freda (love and luxury): Ezili Freda's vèvè is a heart, often filled with a lattice. The fierce Ezili Dantor's heart is pierced by a knife.
  • Baron Samedi (lord of the cemetery): The Baron's vèvè raises a cross on a stepped tomb: he is the first man buried in every cemetery and head of the Gede spirits of the dead.

    What vèvè are

    • Ritual ground drawings made in cornmeal, flour, ash, coffee grounds or powdered brick, each belonging to one lwa.
    • Drawn at the start of a service, to call the lwa and mark where offerings are laid. Legba's vèvè is usually drawn first, since he opens the gate between the human world and the spirits.
    • Vèvè vary between houses and priests. Collections of them have been published, notably Milo Rigaud's Vè-Vè: Diagrammes rituels du Voudou (1974).
    • They are also painted on temple walls, flags and, since the mid-twentieth century, in Haitian art.

    Four lwa and their signs

    The interactive floor above traces schematic vèvè for four lwa:

    • Legba, the old man at the crossroads, keeper of the gate: a cross and a cane.
    • Damballa, the white serpent of the sky and waters, with his wife Ayida-Wedo, the rainbow: serpents rising around a pole or an egg.
    • Ezili Freda, lwa of love, beauty and luxury: a heart, often latticed. Her fierce counterpart Ezili Dantor's heart is pierced by a knife.
    • Baron Samedi, head of the Gede, the spirits of the dead: a cross on a tomb.

    Where they come from

    Older writers traced vèvè to the Taíno, the Indigenous people of Hispaniola. More recent scholarship, following Robert Farris Thompson's Flash of the Spirit (1983), emphasises African roots: the Kongo cosmogram, a cross within a circle marking the boundary between the living and the dead, and the ground drawings and sign systems of the Fon and neighbouring peoples of West Africa, from whom much of Vodou's liturgy descends. Catholic imagery, and Masonic and ceremonial designs, have also shaped some vèvè. The mix is still debated.

    Symbology and sacred encoding

    A drawing as a door. The vèvè is not a picture of the lwa but a point of contact: offerings placed on it reach the spirit, and the lwa may come to "mount" a devotee in possession.

    The crossroads. Many vèvè are built on a cross, the crossroads where the human and spirit worlds meet, echoing the Kongo cosmogram and Legba's role.

    Made to be erased. A vèvè exists only for the ceremony; its effacement by dancing feet is part of the rite.

    ✦ The evidence, honestly

    What's well supported by the evidence

    • Vèvè are ritual ground drawings of Haitian Vodou, each belonging to a lwa, traced in cornmeal or other powders at ceremonies and effaced during them.
    • They are documented in ethnographies and collections of the twentieth century, such as Rigaud's.

    What is not supported

    • That there is one correct form of each vèvè; practice varies widely.

    What's genuinely open

    • The origins of the vèvè: how far they descend from Kongo and Fon sign systems, and what, if anything, comes from the Taíno.
    • How old particular designs are, since records before the twentieth century are few.
