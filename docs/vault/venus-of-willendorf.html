The Venus of Willendorf: A Face Nobody Carved

On 7 August 1908, at a railway cutting beside the Danube near the village of Willendorf in Austria, a workman named Josef Veram was digging in an excavation directed by Josef Szombathy, Hugo Obermaier and Josef Bayer. From the loess he lifted a small stone figure, about 11 centimetres tall: a woman with heavy breasts, a rounded belly and wide hips, her thin arms resting on her breasts. Her head has no face. Instead it is covered with rows of carved bands, perhaps braided hair or a woven cap. She has become the most famous image of the Ice Age.

What was she?

Each card is one reading of the figure. Turn it to see the evidence for and against.

  • A fertility goddess (the classic reading): Widely proposed. The figure stresses breasts, belly and vulva. But no evidence tells us she was a goddess, or worshipped at all.
  • A 'Mother Goddess' cult (20th-century theory): Linked to a supposed universal prehistoric goddess religion. Most archaeologists now reject a single Ice Age goddess cult (see The Great Goddess, ch48).
  • A self-portrait (LeRoy McDermott, 1996): Proposed that women carved their own bodies seen from above, explaining the foreshortening. Suggestive; untestable.
  • An amulet or charm (for pregnancy or health): Small enough to carry. Possible, and consistent with its wear, but not provable.
  • An ideal of plenty (survival in the Ice Age): Read as celebrating body fat and abundance in a hard climate. An interpretation, not evidence.
  • The name 'Venus' (a modern label): Early archaeologists nicknamed such figures after the Roman goddess of love, partly in jest. The name says nothing about their meaning.

The figure

  • Carved from oolitic limestone, a stone not found at Willendorf, and once coloured with red ochre, traces of which survive.
  • Dated to the Gravettian period, roughly 30,000–25,000 years ago; a revised study of the site in 2015 placed the find layer at around 30,000 years.
  • It is one of more than a hundred small female figures made across Europe and into Siberia in the Upper Palaeolithic. The earliest known, the Venus of Hohle Fels, is about 40,000 years old.

Where the stone came from

In 2022 a team at the Natural History Museum Vienna and the University of Vienna (Weber, Lukeneder, Harzhauser and colleagues) scanned the figure by micro-CT, comparing the grains and tiny fossils inside the stone with samples from across Europe. The closest match came from near Lake Garda in northern Italy, some 700 km away. If this is correct, the stone, or the figure, travelled a long way. The authors note that a match from eastern Ukraine could not be excluded, and the conclusion is probable, not certain.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The name. Nineteenth-century archaeologists called such figures "Venus" figures after the Roman goddess of love, partly as a joke. The name carries no evidence about their meaning.

A body without a face. The carver took great care over the body and the hair but gave no features to the face. Readings include anonymity, a type rather than a person, or a figure meant to be held and seen from above. None can be tested.

Red ochre. Red pigment appears in many Palaeolithic burials and objects, and is often read as blood or life. Again, this is interpretation.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • An 11 cm limestone figure of a woman, found at Willendorf in 1908, made about 30,000 years ago, with traces of red ochre.
  • Its stone is not local; the 2022 study points to northern Italy.

What is not supported

  • That it represents a goddess, or that a single "Mother Goddess" religion spanned Ice Age Europe.

What's genuinely open

  • What it meant to the people who made it, and how it was used.
  • Whether the stone's origin near Lake Garda is confirmed by further work.
