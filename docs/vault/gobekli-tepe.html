Göbekli Tepe's Pillars: Stone Beings Before Farming

In the archive

Held
In place: Göbekli Tepe, Şanlıurfa Province, Turkey (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
Date evidence
c. 9500–8000 BCE (Pre-Pottery Neolithic)
Era
Era I · Prehistory
In the archive
The Neolithic · The Paleolithic

On a limestone ridge above the Harran plain in south-eastern Turkey, excavators have uncovered circles of great stone pillars, each shaped like a T, standing inside rough stone walls. The largest are over 5 metres tall. On their sides are carved foxes, boars, snakes, vultures, scorpions and cranes, and on two central pillars, arms that reach around from the back and hands that meet above a belt: the pillars are people, or beings. They were raised some 11,500 years ago, by people who hunted and gathered, before pottery, before metal and, it seemed at first, before farming.

An enclosure, seen from above

A schematic plan of Enclosure D: a ring of pillars set in a wall, around two great central pillars. Touch a pillar.

  • Pillar 18: About 5.5 m tall. The T-shaped top is a head: carved arms reach round the sides, the hands meet above a belt, and a fox pelt hangs from the belt. The pillars are stylised human or superhuman beings.
  • Pillar 31: The twin of Pillar 18, also with arms, hands and belt. The paired central figures have been read as ancestors, spirits or gods; the builders left no text.
  • Pillar 43, 'the Vulture Stone': Carved with a vulture holding a disc, a scorpion, birds and other animals. A 2017 paper read it as a star map recording a comet strike; the excavators and most archaeologists reject that reading.

    Discovery and excavation

    • The mound was recorded in a survey in 1963, but its broken slabs were taken for a medieval cemetery.
    • From 1995 Klaus Schmidt of the German Archaeological Institute (DAI) excavated it with the Şanlıurfa Museum, until his death in 2014. Excavation continues.
    • Four large enclosures (A–D) belong to the oldest layer, dated to about 9500–8000 BCE, the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. Geophysical survey shows many more still buried.
    • It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018.

    A temple? The debate

    Schmidt argued that Göbekli Tepe was a sanctuary, "the first temple", visited by hunter-gatherers who gathered there for feasts and ritual, and that the need to feed these gatherings helped drive the invention of farming. He also held that the enclosures were deliberately buried.

    More recent work by the excavation team has revised this picture. They have found cisterns, huge numbers of grinding stones, and domestic buildings, which suggest that people lived at the site. The enclosures may have been filled by slope slides rather than deliberate burial. Similar sites nearby, such as Karahan Tepe, show that Göbekli Tepe was part of a wider regional culture. Most archaeologists still see the enclosures as special, probably ritual buildings, but "temple" is now used with more caution.

    Symbology and sacred encoding

    Pillars as persons. The T-shaped top is a head seen from the side; carved arms, hands, belts and loincloths make the pillars stylised human or superhuman beings. The two great central pillars of each enclosure may have been ancestors, spirits or gods. Nobody knows.

    Dangerous animals. The animals carved are overwhelmingly wild and often dangerous: predators, venomous creatures and carrion birds. Many readings link them to death or to the spirit world, especially the vultures.

    Carved skulls. Fragments of human skulls with carved grooves were found at the site (Gresky and colleagues, 2017), suggesting a skull cult, as known from other Neolithic sites in the region.

    The "Vulture Stone" and the comet. In 2017 two engineers, Martin Sweatman and Dimitrios Tsikritsis, read Pillar 43 as a star map recording a comet impact around 10,950 BCE. The excavators rejected this, pointing out that the carvings do not match the proposed constellations and that the pillar is later than the supposed event. Claims that Göbekli Tepe was built by a lost advanced civilisation have no archaeological support.

    ✦ The evidence, honestly

    What's well supported by the evidence

    • Monumental enclosures of carved T-shaped pillars, built about 9500–8000 BCE by communities who did not yet farm fully.
    • Pillars carved as stylised human figures, and many reliefs of wild animals.

    What is not supported

    • That Pillar 43 records a comet strike, or that the site was built by a lost civilisation.
    • That the site was only a temple with no one living there, as first proposed.

    What's genuinely open

    • What the enclosures were used for, and what or whom the pillars represent.
    • How the building of such sites relates to the beginnings of farming.
