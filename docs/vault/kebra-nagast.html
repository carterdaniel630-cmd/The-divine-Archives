In August 1872, the British Museum sent a manuscript back to Ethiopia. Four years earlier, a British army under Sir Robert Napier had stormed the mountain fortress of Magdala, where the Emperor Tewodros II took his own life, and carried away hundreds of manuscripts from his library and the churches. The new emperor, Yohannes IV, wrote to the British government asking for one book in particular: the Kebra Nagast, the "Glory of the Kings". Without it, he wrote, his people would not obey him. A copy was returned. The book told how Ethiopia's kings descended from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, and how the Ark of the Covenant came to Ethiopia.

The title. ክብረ ነገሥት Kəbrä Nägäśt, 'the Glory of the Kings', in Ge'ez.

Makeda. The Queen of Sheba, Makeda, travels to Jerusalem to learn Solomon's wisdom, and turns to the God of Israel. Expanding 1 Kings 10.

Menelik. She bears Solomon's son, Menelik, who grows up in Ethiopia and returns to Jerusalem to meet his father. The founder of the dynasty.

The Ark. Menelik returns home with the firstborn sons of Israel's nobles, who bring the Ark of the Covenant with them. The glory of Zion passes to Ethiopia. Ethiopian tradition holds that the Ark is at Aksum today (see V07).

c. 1314–1322. Compiled in Ge'ez by Yeshaq, a churchman of Aksum, soon after the Solomonic dynasty took power in 1270. The colophon says it was translated from Arabic, itself from Coptic; that claim is debated.

1868 · 1872. British troops take manuscripts from the fortress of Magdala. Emperor Yohannes IV asks for this book back, and one copy is returned in 1872. A rare early restitution.

    The book

    • Written in Ge'ez, the classical language of Ethiopia, in 117 chapters.
    • According to its colophon, it was translated from Arabic (itself from Coptic) by a churchman named Yeshaq of Aksum, in the reign of the Solomonic dynasty's early kings; it is usually dated to about 1314–1322. Many scholars think it was largely composed in Ethiopia, drawing on older material, and that the translation claim adds authority. This is debated.
    • It is framed as a discussion among the 318 Orthodox fathers of the Council of Nicaea about where the true glory of kings lies.

    The story

    • Makeda, Queen of Sheba, visits Solomon in Jerusalem, learns his wisdom and turns to the God of Israel (expanding 1 Kings 10).
    • She bears Solomon's son, Menelik, who grows up in Ethiopia and later visits his father.
    • Menelik returns with the firstborn sons of Israel's nobles, who secretly take the Ark of the Covenant with them. The glory of Zion passes from Israel to Ethiopia, the new chosen people.

    Why it mattered

    In 1270 a new dynasty took the Ethiopian throne claiming descent from Solomon. The Kebra Nagast gave this Solomonic dynasty its charter, and it ruled until 1974. Haile Selassie's 1955 constitution declared the emperor a descendant of Menelik. In the twentieth century the Kebra Nagast became important to Rastafari, who revere Haile Selassie as a descendant of Solomon and, for many, as divine.

    Symbology and sacred encoding

    The Ark and the tabot. Ethiopian tradition holds that the Ark rests in the chapel of St Mary of Zion at Aksum, guarded by a single monk and shown to no one (see V07). Every Ethiopian Orthodox church keeps a tabot, a consecrated tablet representing the Ark, without which a church cannot be consecrated. At the feast of Timkat the tabots are carried in procession, wrapped in cloth.

    Zion. The book calls the Ark "Zion", and treats Ethiopia as the new Israel, a theme still central to Ethiopian Orthodox identity.

    Sheba. The Queen of Sheba appears in the Bible, the Qur'an (as the Queen of Saba, later named Bilqis) and Jewish legend. The Kebra Nagast gives her a name and a son. Where Sheba was, in Arabia or Africa, is debated.

    Connections

    Eastern Orthodoxy (ch60), for the Ethiopian Orthodox Church; Diaspora Religions (ch40), for Rastafari; Sacred Kingship (ch49). Compare the Ark of the Covenant (V07), and the Benin Bronzes (V64) for another restitution.

    ✦ The evidence, honestly

    What's well supported by the evidence

    • A Ge'ez work compiled in the early fourteenth century, telling the story of Makeda, Solomon and Menelik and the Ark's journey to Ethiopia.
    • Its use as the charter of the Solomonic dynasty (1270–1974), and the return of a copy from London in 1872.

    What is not supported

    • That the Ark of the Covenant is proven to be at Aksum. No outsider has been allowed to examine it.
    • That Ethiopia's dynasty is historically descended from Solomon. This is a religious and political tradition.

    What's genuinely open

    • How much of the book was translated and how much composed in Ethiopia, and the age of its sources.
    • The location of the kingdom of Sheba.
