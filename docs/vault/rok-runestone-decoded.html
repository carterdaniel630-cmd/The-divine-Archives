The Rök Runestone: A Father's Riddles
Recently added · pending full review. This page is live but has not yet completed the keeper’s review pass. It is sourced to the project’s standard, but wording and detail may still change. The tag is removed once the page is cleared.

On a granite slab beside the church at Rök, in Östergötland, a father named Varinn carved some 760 characters in 28 lines over five faces of the stone, around the year 800, for his dead son Vámóðr. It is the longest runic inscription in stone. Scholars agree, unusually well, on what the runes are and on how the cipher passages decode. They disagree, just as unusually, on the order in which to read them, where the words divide, and what the text means. This page walks through all three: what is carved, how the ciphers work, and which answers are agreed, contested or still unknown.

Photographs of the five faces will be added here once rights-clean images have been sourced and logged. Until then, the stone’s faces are described in words below.

1. The stone itself

The stone is catalogued as Ög 136 (Östergötland no. 136) in the Scandinavian Runic-text Database. It stands about 2.4 to 2.5 metres above the ground; with the part buried below ground it is 3.82 metres long in all. Sources give slightly different character counts (more than 700; about 750; about 760); c. 760 is the majority figure. It was documented in the 17th century built into a building beside the church, taken out in the 19th century when carving was found on all its faces, and now stands free under a protective roof by Rök church.

The opening two lines, large and plain on the front, are the one part of the text every reading agrees on:

aft uamuþ stonta runaR þaR. (i)n uarin faþi faþiR aft faikion sunu.

“In memory of Vámóðr stand these runes. And Varinn coloured them, the father, in memory of his dead son.” (Rundata)

faþi means “coloured” or “painted” (runes were painted in antiquity), so “coloured them” is the literal sense of what is often rendered “made” or “carved”. faikion (Old Norse feigr) means “death-doomed, fated to die”, not simply “dead”: whether that is just alliteration (with faþi, faþiR) or a claim that the son was marked for death is itself part of the argument. Varinn is both the sponsor and the runemaster. The inscription calls him only “the father”; “kinglet” or “chieftain” is a modern gloss.

2. How to read it

  • Five faces. Every face except the buried base is carved. By the usual lettering: A the front; B the first narrow side; C the back; D the second narrow side; E the top. These letters say where text sits, not the order it is read in.
  • 28 lines: twenty vertical and eight more or less horizontal.
  • Three scripts. Most of the text is in the short-twig runes of the 16-rune Younger Futhark. Two lines use runes of the older 24-rune Elder Futhark, already archaic by 800. Other passages are in cipher runes.
  • No word spaces and no doubled runes. The text runs on without breaks, and the same rune is never carved twice in a row, even where one word ends with it and the next begins with it. The 16-rune row also has to cover many more sounds than it has runes (one rune for both k and g, one for t and d). So every word division is an editorial decision, and this is the biggest single reason translations differ.
  • Reading order. Everyone starts with the two large lines on the front. Beyond the front, the reading order is not agreed: more than fifteen orders have been proposed.

3. The standard translation, in reading order

This is the standard translation of the Scandinavian Runic-text Database (Rundata), the same reading given on the Swedish National Heritage Board’s information board at the stone. It follows the traditional reading order, section by section. The repeated formula sakum mukmini is rendered “I say the folktale / to the young men”: the slash marks two possible word divisions (“folk-memory, tale” or “young men”).

Dedication · front (A), lines 1–2In memory of Vámóðr stand these runes. And Varinn coloured them, the father, in memory of his dead son.

First question · AI say the folktale / to the young men, which the two war-booties were, which twelve times were taken as war-booty, both together from various men.

Second question and the Theodoric stanza · A into BI say this second, who nine generations ago lost his life with the Hreidgoths; and died with them for his guilt. Þjóðríkr the bold, chief of sea-warriors, ruled over the shores of the Hreiðsea. Now he sits armed on his Goth(ic horse), his shield strapped, the prince of the Mærings.

The twelfth · back (C)I say this the twelfth, where the horse of Gunnr sees fodder on the battlefield, where twenty kings lie.

The thirteenth and the list · CThis I say as thirteenth, which twenty kings sat on Sjólund for four winters, of four names, born of four brothers: five Valkis, sons of Ráðulfr, five Hreiðulfrs, sons of Rugulfr, five Háisl, sons of Hôrðr, five Gunnmundrs/Kynmundrs, sons of Bjôrn.

A damaged line · CNow I say the tales in full. Someone …

The Ingold passage, in Elder Futhark · CI say the folktale / to the young men, which of the line of Ingold was repaid by a wife’s sacrifice.

The Vélinn passage, partly in shift cipher · CI say the folktale / to the young men, to whom is born a relative, to a valiant man. It is Vélinn. He could crush a giant. It is Vélinn …

Thor · second narrow side (D), coordinate cipherI say the folktale / to the young men: Þórr.

Sibbi · top (E) and the end of the back (C), cross cipherSibbi of Vé, nonagenarian, begot (a son).

Part of one line on the back is damaged and cannot be read in full; every translation marks it “Someone …”. The opening is in the Eddic metre ljóðaháttr and the Theodoric stanza in fornyrðislag.

4. The ciphers, worked

The decipherments themselves are agreed: Sophus Bugge read the short-twig text and solved the shift cipher in the 1870s, and Otto von Friesen (1920) read the Elder Futhark lines and the numerical cipher. What the decoded words mean is a separate question. Sources count the cipher systems as two or as four, depending on whether the Elder Futhark passage and the two forms of coordinate cipher are counted separately; the inventory itself is not in dispute.

a. The shift cipher: each rune is the next in the row

The Younger Futhark row runs f u þ a r k · h n i a s · t b m l R. In this passage every carved rune stands for the rune after it in the row. The information board at the stone gives the example: the carved runes read AIRFBFRBNHN; move each one place along and they read SAKUM UKMINI, the same formula that opens the plain-text questions.

Carvedairfbfrbnhn
Next in rowsakumukmini

Further along, the shifted groups resolve in the same way (finb → uaim, rhfþ → knua, rhis → knat), giving, in the standard reading, “… to whom is born a relative … It is Vélinn. He could crush a giant.” Parts of these lines are in plain runes, so the passage is only partly enciphered.

b. The coordinate cipher: family and place

The 16 runes fall into three families (ættir). A coordinate sign does not picture a rune; it gives two numbers: which family and which rune within it, shown by counts of twigs on either side of a stem (or in the parts of a cross). Sources write each sign as a pair, family:place. The family numbers are as the sources print them, and they are not counted in the left-to-right order of the row above: in these pairs f u þ a r k is family 3 and t b m l R is family 1. On the second narrow side (D) the signs read, pair by pair, exactly as the published worked example prints them:

Together: sakum ukmini þur, standardly “I say the folktale / to the young men: Þórr [Thor]”. For the last word the information board describes a single sign repeated in groups, normal then mirrored, whose counts give the family and place. Only pairs printed in the published worked examples are shown here; this page does not build a full table of the cipher from them.

c. The cross ciphers and the final nit

Three slanted crosses on the back and three on the top work the same way: each cross is split into upper and lower parts, each part gives a family:place pair, the cross is turned to read its second half, and plain runes carved beside the crosses complete the words. The published resolutions:

In reading order: sibi uia uari ul niruþR, standardly “Sibbi of Vé [guardian of the sanctuary], nonagenarian, begot (a son)”. The last group on the stone is also in coordinate cipher, 2:2 2:3 1:1, which reads nit. In the standard reading nit is left uninterpreted.

d. The Elder Futhark passage: the Ingold lines

Two lines on the back use runes of the older futhark: familiar-looking shapes that carry their older sound values. Transliterated with capitals to mark those values, they read saGwM MOGMEni (þ)aD HOaR iGOlD(i)Ga OaRi GOlDin D GOonaR HOsli. Normalised, the standard reading is sakum ukmini þat, huaR Inguldinga uari goldinn at kuanaR husli: “I say the folktale / to the young men, which of the line of Ingold was repaid by a wife’s sacrifice.” One sign inside the passage is itself a coordinate cipher, 3:3 = þ. Why the older script is used here is not known; deliberate archaism is the traditional assumption.

5. The questions the stone asks

The text is built from prompts (“I say…”) followed by questions. In the standard reading only four carry numbers: an unnumbered first, then the second, the twelfth and the thirteenth. How the count jumps from two to twelve is one of the stone’s long-standing puzzles. Each question below is marked Answer carved where the stone itself states the answer, Contested where a proposed answer is disputed, and Unsolved where no answer is agreed.

The two war-booties

Unsolved “Which the two war-booties were, which twelve times were taken as war-booty”. The stone gives no answer. The common guess is two famous weapons (a sword and a shield, or a helmet) passed from man to man in lost heroic poems; the story itself is lost.

Who died nine generations ago: Theodoric

Answer carvedIdentification contested The stone answers its own question with the stanza about Þjóðríkr, “prince of the Mærings”. Since 1879 (Guðbrandur Vigfússon) he has been identified with Theodoric the Great, the Ostrogothic king who ruled from Ravenna (493–526): nine generations before c. 800 falls in his lifetime; Mærings is his family name in the Old English poem Deor; and “now he sits armed on his horse” is usually explained by his equestrian statue, moved from Ravenna to Aachen by Charlemagne in 801. This was the standard reading of the 20th century, though why Theodoric would appear on a Swedish memorial was never settled. It is now actively challenged: Bo Ralph (2007) and Holmberg and colleagues (2020) divide the same runes as “Ride the horse did the bold champion”, with no Theodoric named at all. This page presents Theodoric as the traditional reading, now challenged.

The twelfth: where twenty kings lie

Unsolved “Where the horse of Gunnr sees fodder on the battlefield, where twenty kings lie.” Gunnr is a valkyrie; a valkyrie’s “horse” is a wolf, and its fodder on the battlefield is the dead. The kenning is understood; the battlefield is not. Herman Lindqvist suggested the legendary Battle of Brávellir; that link is unverified.

The thirteenth: the twenty kings

Answer carvedMeaning unsolved The stone answers with the list itself: four sets of five brothers, each set sharing one name, their four fathers themselves brothers. Who they were has been lost. Sjólund may be Zealand (Sjælland) in Denmark, or a Swedish place; Joseph Harris suggested an initiatory warband whose shared names outlive its members. Whether these are the same twenty kings who “lie” on the battlefield in the twelfth question is assumed, not shown.

The line of Ingold and the wife’s sacrifice

Unsolved “Which of the line of Ingold was repaid by a wife’s sacrifice.” No proposed answer has wide support; Holmberg calls this passage “never interpreted satisfactorily”. Åke Ohlmarks (1979) read husl as “baptism” rather than “sacrifice”; that is a minority hypothesis. A common mistake to correct: the stone does not speak of “the sons of Ingvald”. It asks about one of the line of Ingold (Inguldinga). The sons in the text are the twenty kings and, at the end, the son Sibbi begot at ninety.

To whom a kinsman is born: Vélinn

Answer carvedMeaning unsolved The stone answers “It is Vélinn. He could crush a giant.” Who Vélinn is (a god, a hero, the dead son himself) is unknown, and Harris divides the same runes into a quite different sentence. The final word nit remains uninterpreted.

Thor, and Sibbi at ninety

Unsolved These close the inscription as statements, not questions: an invocation of Thor, and Sibbi, “of Vé” (guardian of a sanctuary), who begot a son at ninety. Sibbi is unidentified, and even the order of these last lines is an editorial choice.

A contested proposal, set apart

6. The 2020 climate reading (Holmberg, Gräslund, Sundqvist and Williams)

Everything in this box is one team’s proposal. It is not the standard reading and is not used anywhere else on this page.

In “The Rök Runestone and the End of the World” (Futhark 9–10, published 2020), Per Holmberg, Bo Gräslund, Olof Sundqvist and Henrik Williams re-divide the same agreed runes into nine riddling questions. They answer five of them with the sun and four with matters concerning Odin, drawing parallels from the Eddic poem Vafþrúðnismál. In their words, the inscription “deals with an anxiety triggered by a son’s death and the fear of a new climate crisis similar to the catastrophic one after 536 CE”, when volcanic eruptions brought years of cold summers and failed harvests. Nine generations back from c. 800 lands near 536, the same arithmetic the traditional reading used for Theodoric.

Their reading depends on re-dividing several key strings:

  • sakum ukmini becomes “Let us say (this as) a memory for Yggr [Odin]”;
  • the Theodoric line becomes “Ride the horse did the bold champion” (after Ralph 2007), so no Theodoric;
  • Inguldinga becomes “because of a howler [a wolf]”, so no Ingold;
  • “It is Vélinn” becomes “It is not a lie”, so no Vélinn; Sibbi, Thor and the nonagenarian disappear the same way; the final nit becomes “Clash!”.

The authors’ own caveats: “we are not so arrogant as to believe that we have now solved every problem in the Rök text”; “the reading order that we propose may also be questioned”; one link is “admittedly a weaker link in our chain of reasoning”; and of two of the questions, “our proposal is just one possibility, we hope better than previous ones”.

How it has been received: it was widely publicised, and seriously argued, but it has not become the standard reading. Colleagues have cautioned that the climate reading may reflect our own worries about global warming; Holmberg has replied that every era has its interpretive frameworks. In 2021 alone, the National Heritage Board’s runologist Magnus Källström counted three weighty new interpretations: a further article by Holmberg, a book by Williams, and a two-volume alternative by Bo Ralph that reads the stone differently again. The Rundata translation above remains the reference text. No point-by-point published rebuttal was located for this page; that is not the same as saying none exists.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • The stone (Ög 136) was carved c. 800 CE / early 9th century by Varinn for his dead son Vámóðr; the dedication on the front is agreed by every reading.
  • The identification of the runes and the decipherment of all the cipher passages (shift, coordinate, cross and Elder Futhark) are agreed.
  • The text has no word spaces and no doubled runes, so word division is editorial; it is a deliberately difficult composition in verse and prose using kennings.

What's not supported by the evidence

  • “The sons of Ingvald”: the stone asks about one of the line of Ingold, not his sons.
  • Any claim that the riddles have been solved. Neither the traditional reading nor the 2020 climate reading is agreed as the meaning of the text.
  • A full cipher table built from the few published coordinate pairs; the pairs on this page are only those printed in the sources.

What's genuinely open

  • The reading order beyond the front (more than fifteen proposals) and the word division of key strings such as sakum ukmini.
  • Whether Þjóðríkr is Theodoric the Great (the traditional reading, now challenged).
  • The two war-booties, the battlefield of the twenty kings, the line of Ingold, Vélinn, Sibbi and the final nit.
  • The 2020 climate reading: seriously argued, actively contested, not adopted.
