The Cyrus Cylinder: A Conqueror's Proclamation, and a Modern Myth

Recently added · pending review

Date evidence
After 539 BCE

In 1879 the archaeologist Hormuzd Rassam, digging for the British Museum at Babylon, recovered a barrel-shaped cylinder of baked clay about 23 centimetres long, broken and incomplete. It was covered in Babylonian cuneiform. It came from the ruins of Marduk's great temple, the Esagila, and was made as a foundation deposit: Mesopotamian kings buried building inscriptions for the gods and for future rulers to find. Its speaker was Cyrus the Great, the Persian king who had taken Babylon in 539 BCE.

“I am Cyrus”

The opening of line 20, in transliterated Akkadian. Touch a word.

anāku Kuraš šar kiššati šarru rabû šarru dannu šar Bābili šar māt Šumeri u Akkadî šar kibrāt erbetti

I am Cyrus, king of the universe, the great king, the mighty king, king of Babylon, king of Sumer and Akkad, king of the four quarters.

    What the cylinder says

    The text is written in Akkadian in the traditional style of Babylonian royal inscriptions, by Babylonian scribes and for a Babylonian audience.

    • The fall of Nabonidus. It opens by condemning Nabonidus, the last native king of Babylon, for neglecting the worship of Marduk, the city's god, and for oppressing its people.
    • Marduk chooses Cyrus. Marduk searches the lands for a just ruler, takes Cyrus by the hand, calls his name, and sends him to Babylon, which he enters "without battle".
    • "I am Cyrus." In line 20 Cyrus speaks in the first person, with the traditional titles of Mesopotamian kingship: king of the universe, king of Babylon, king of Sumer and Akkad, king of the four quarters. He traces his descent from his forefathers, kings of Anshan.
    • The return of gods and peoples. Cyrus says he returned the statues of gods to their sanctuaries in several cities, and gathered their people and returned them to their homes.
    • Building work. It ends with Cyrus's repair of Babylon's walls and a mention that he saw an inscription of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal, an earlier restorer.

    It is not unique. In 2010 Irving Finkel of the British Museum identified two fragments of clay tablets in the museum's collection carrying parts of the same text. The proclamation was copied and circulated, not only buried.

    Cyrus and the Bible

    The Hebrew Bible remembers Cyrus warmly. Ezra 1:1–4 and 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 quote a decree allowing the exiled Judeans to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the Temple. Isaiah 44:28–45:1 calls Cyrus the Lord's "anointed" (mashiach), the only non-Israelite given that title.

    The cylinder does not mention Judah, Jerusalem or the Jews. What it shows is a policy: a new king presenting himself as the restorer of local gods and displaced peoples. The biblical decree fits that policy, and many historians think the return of Judean exiles was part of it. The cylinder does not confirm the biblical decree as written, and it does not contradict it.

    The "first charter of human rights"

    In 1971 the Shah of Iran, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi, celebrated 2,500 years of Persian monarchy and promoted the cylinder as the first declaration of human rights. A replica was presented to the United Nations, where it stands at the headquarters in New York. Invented "translations" circulate online, with articles on freedom of religion, abolition of slavery and choice of occupation that appear nowhere on the cylinder.

    Historians agree that the cylinder is royal propaganda in a Mesopotamian tradition. Its claims of restoration are the claims earlier Assyrian and Babylonian kings made about themselves. Calling it a human-rights charter is anachronistic: the concept did not exist. The cylinder remains a real witness to a conqueror who chose legitimacy through tolerance of local cults over forced conformity, and for many Iranians it is a treasured national symbol.

    Symbology and sacred encoding

    Chosen by the god. The cylinder's central claim is divine election: Marduk chooses Cyrus. Isaiah's "anointed" makes the same kind of claim from the other side, with Israel's God choosing him.

    A foundation deposit. Buried as a building record, the cylinder was addressed as much to the gods and to future kings as to living readers. Cyrus says he saw Ashurbanipal's inscription, and in turn expects his own to be found.

    The form of cuneiform kingship. Its structure, which goes from the failings of the previous king to the god's choice of the new one, his titles and genealogy, his restorations and his building works, follows centuries-old Babylonian models. That is why it reads like a Babylonian text.

    ✦ The evidence, honestly

    What's well supported by the evidence

    • A clay foundation cylinder found at Babylon in 1879, inscribed in Akkadian after Cyrus's conquest of 539 BCE. It condemns Nabonidus, presents Cyrus as Marduk's choice, and records the return of gods and peoples to their homes.
    • Parts of the same text survive on tablet fragments identified in 2010.

    What is not supported

    • That it is a charter of human rights. That description dates from the twentieth century.
    • That it mentions the Jews or confirms the wording of the decree in Ezra.
    • The widely shared "full translations" listing modern rights. They are fabrications.

    What's genuinely open

    • How far the policy the cylinder describes applied across the empire, and how the return of the Judean exiles fitted into it.
