The story comes before the text. A seeker, called Balīnūs in the Arabic sources (the name stands for Apollonius of Tyana, the first-century wonder-worker), goes down into a vault beneath a statue of Hermes. There he finds an old man seated on a golden throne, holding a tablet of green stone. On it, in a script the seeker can read, is written a short, strange text about the "one thing" from which all things come. That is the frame in which the Emerald Tablet (Tabula Smaragdina) first appears in writing. It is a tablet inside a story. No archaeologist has ever found it. No museum holds it. Every "photograph" of it is an illustration.

What does exist is the text: about fourteen short lines, translated from Arabic into Latin in the twelfth century. It became perhaps the most quoted scripture of European alchemy. Isaac Newton translated it, and its second sentence, "that which is below is like that which is above", is now repeated as the slogan "as above, so below". This entry traces the text's documented history, gives the standard Latin with our own line-by-line translation, and separates the tablet of legend from the texts that are real, and from a twentieth-century book that borrowed its name.

Where the text actually comes from

The earliest datable version is Arabic. The oldest source that can be dated is the Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa (Book of the Secret of Creation), attributed to Balīnūs. It is a compilation usually placed between about 750 and 850 CE, and some scholars tie it to the reign of the caliph al-Ma'mūn (813–833). That date is not certain. A second early Arabic version appears in a work attributed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, the Kitāb Usṭuqus al-Uss al-Thānī (Second Book of the Element of the Foundation). Jābir is the legendary figure behind a large Arabic alchemical corpus whose own dating and authorship are debated.

Earlier origins are hypothesis, not evidence. The Tablet is attributed to Hermes Trismegistus ("thrice-greatest Hermes"), the Greek-Egyptian figure behind the Hermetic literature of late antiquity. No Greek, Coptic, Syriac or Egyptian original has ever been found. Some scholars have argued for a lost Greek or Syriac source behind the Arabic. Others think it was composed in Arabic in the form we have. This is open.

Latin Europe received it in the twelfth century. The Spanish translator Hugo of Santalla rendered the Sirr al-khalīqa into Latin, Tablet included. Another Latin version travelled with the enormously popular Secretum secretorum. From these came the version now called the "vulgate", the form printed in Nuremberg in 1541 (in the edition of Chrysogonus Polydorus) that became standard.

The legend kept growing. Later European retellings moved the discovery to other finders, among them Alexander the Great and even Sarah, the wife of Abraham, in Hermes' tomb. These are legends about the text, not history.

The text: Latin vulgate and a working translation

The Latin below is the standard vulgate text, public domain. The English is this archive's own line-by-line working translation, kept close to the Latin so that its obscurities are not smoothed over. Where a word is genuinely ambiguous, the alternatives are given in brackets.

The Tablet

The standard Latin text, cut into green stone as the legend describes it. Touch any line to raise its meaning.

Verum, sine mendacio, certum et verissimum:
True, without falsehood, certain and most true:

Quod est inferius est sicut quod est superius, et quod est superius est sicut quod est inferius, ad perpetranda miracula rei unius.
That which is below is like that which is above, and that which is above is like that which is below, for accomplishing the wonders of the one thing.

Et sicut res omnes fuerunt ab uno, meditatione unius, sic omnes res natae fuerunt ab hac una re, adaptatione.
And as all things came from the One, by the meditation [thought / mediation] of the One, so all things were born from this one thing, by adaptation.

Pater eius est Sol, mater eius Luna; portavit illud Ventus in ventre suo; nutrix eius Terra est.
Its father is the Sun, its mother the Moon; the Wind carried it in its belly; its nurse is the Earth.

Pater omnis telesmi totius mundi est hic.
Here is the father of all the telesma [perfection / consecration / talismanic power] of the whole world.

Vis eius integra est si versa fuerit in terram.
Its power is whole if it is turned into earth.

Separabis terram ab igne, subtile ab spisso, suaviter, magno cum ingenio.
You shall separate the earth from the fire, the subtle from the gross, gently, with great skill.

Ascendit a terra in coelum, iterumque descendit in terram, et recipit vim superiorum et inferiorum.
It rises from earth to heaven and descends again to earth, and receives the power of the things above and the things below.

Sic habebis gloriam totius mundi. Ideo fugiet a te omnis obscuritas.
Thus you will have the glory of the whole world. Therefore all obscurity will flee from you.

Hic est totius fortitudinis fortitudo fortis, quia vincet omnem rem subtilem, omnemque solidam penetrabit.
This is the strong strength of all strength, for it will overcome every subtle thing and penetrate every solid thing.

Sic mundus creatus est.
Thus the world was created.

Hinc erunt adaptationes mirabiles, quarum modus est hic.
From this will come wonderful adaptations, of which this is the means.

Itaque vocatus sum Hermes Trismegistus, habens tres partes philosophiae totius mundi.
Therefore I am called Hermes Trismegistus, having the three parts of the philosophy of the whole world.

Completum est quod dixi de operatione Solis.
What I have said concerning the operation of the Sun is complete.

Notes on the translation.

  • "Meditatione": Latin manuscripts vary. Some readers prefer "mediation", the One acting through something, over "meditation". The Arabic behind it is usually rendered as the One's design or governance. Genuinely ambiguous.
  • "Telesmi": the Latin borrows a Greek-derived word, telesma, related to "completion" and ritual consecration. The same root gives English "talisman" by way of Arabic ṭilasm. Translators render it "perfection", "talismanic power" or "consecration". No single meaning is certain.
  • "Operatio Solis": to alchemists, Sol meant gold as much as the sun, so the last line can be read as "the working of gold". Both readings are historically attested.
  • Newton's version. Isaac Newton's English translation survives in his alchemical papers, King's College, Cambridge, Keynes MS 28. It begins "Tis true without lying, certain & most true. That which is below is like that which is above & that which is above is like that which is below to do ye miracles of one only thing." It is a direct witness to how the vulgate was read in the late seventeenth century.

How it was read: the Tablet as an operating manual

Medieval and early modern alchemists read the Tablet as coded laboratory instruction. They did not read it as philosophy for its own sake. The standard readings, which are documented interpretations and not facts about the text's original meaning, include these:

  • "Father Sun, mother Moon": the two principles that unite in the Work. They were read as gold and silver, or later as sulphur and mercury.
  • "The Wind carried it in its belly": air, vapour, distillation. "Its nurse is the Earth": the vessel or the solid base.
  • "Separate the earth from the fire, the subtle from the gross": purification and sublimation.
  • "It rises from earth to heaven and descends again": repeated distillation or circulation, vapour rising and condensing back.
  • "Its power is whole if it is turned into earth": fixation, making the volatile solid.

Mystical and Christian-Hermetic readers read the same lines as a map of creation and of the soul's ascent and return. For them "the one thing" was the world-soul, the Spirit, or the philosopher's stone understood spiritually. Both kinds of reading are historically real. Neither can be shown to be what the original author meant.

Symbology and sacred encoding

"As above, so below" is a paraphrase. The famous slogan compresses the Tablet's second sentence. In its modern form it is best known from The Kybalion (1908), a New Thought book published under the name "Three Initiates" (usually attributed to William Walker Atkinson). It presents a "Principle of Correspondence" as ancient Hermetic teaching. The Kybalion is a twentieth-century work. It is part of the Tablet's later life, not evidence for its origin.

The Tablet as an image. Because no object existed, artists invented one. The best-known picture is an engraving in Heinrich Khunrath's Amphitheatrum Sapientiae Aeternae (Hanau, 1609). It shows the Latin text carved into a rock face, the rock standing for the philosophers' stone. Later alchemical emblem books did the same. Their images build a visual code: sun and moon, the eagle of the volatile, the lion of the fixed, the descending and ascending bird.

The number three. "Trismegistus" was explained by the Tablet's own last lines as possessing the three parts of the philosophy of the whole world. Later writers attached many triads to the phrase. Those are interpretations built onto the text.

An acronym often linked with it. The alchemical motto V.I.T.R.I.O.L. (Visita Interiora Terrae Rectificando Invenies Occultum Lapidem, "Visit the interior of the earth; by rectifying you will find the hidden stone") is a genuine, intentional acrostic of early modern alchemy. It spells the Latin for vitriol (sulphuric acid and its salts). It is often printed with the Tablet but is a separate, later text, usually traced to seventeenth-century printed sources attributed to "Basil Valentine".

Not the same thing: "The Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean"

A different book is sold today under a similar name: The Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean, fifteen "tablets" of verse. Its author was Maurice Doreal (born Claude Doggins, 1898–1963), who founded the Brotherhood of the White Temple in Denver in 1930. He said he had found and translated tablets in the Great Pyramid in the 1920s. The book first circulated in the 1930s, and editions from 1939 survive. Its content has no connection with the medieval Emerald Tablet beyond the name. It draws on Theosophy and on pulp Atlantis literature. Scholars treat it as a modern esoteric composition, not a translation. This archive covers it as part of twentieth-century new religious movements (see ch35 and ch37). It is not an ancient source.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • The Tablet is a text, first attested in Arabic in the Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa (c. 750–850, date debated) and in the Jābirian corpus. It was translated into Latin in the twelfth century (Hugo of Santalla and the Secretum secretorum tradition), and the standard Latin form was printed at Nuremberg in 1541.
  • It was central to European alchemy. Isaac Newton translated it (Keynes MS 28), and Khunrath's 1609 engraving is among many visual renderings.
  • Doreal's "Emerald Tablets of Thoth" is a separate twentieth-century composition.

What is not supported

  • That a physical emerald tablet ever existed, was found in a tomb or vault, or can be seen anywhere today. The discovery stories are literary frames and later legends.
  • That the text is ancient Egyptian or was written by a historical Hermes. No pre-Islamic original has been found.
  • That Doreal's book translates an ancient artefact.

What's genuinely open

  • Whether the Arabic text translates a lost Greek or Syriac original or was composed in Arabic.
  • The exact date of the Sirr al-khalīqa and of the Jābirian texts.
  • What the author meant by "the one thing" and by telesma. Alchemical, cosmological and mystical readings all have long histories behind them.
