The Nebra Sky Disc: A Bronze Age Sky

Recently added · pending review

Held
State Museum of Prehistory, Halle (Saale), Germany
Date evidence
Buried c. 1600 BCE (Early Bronze Age); a later date was proposed in 2020 and rejected by most specialists

In July 1999 two men with metal detectors, working illegally, dug into the summit of the Mittelberg, a hill near Nebra in Saxony-Anhalt, Germany. They found a hoard: two bronze swords, two axes, a chisel, fragments of spiral bracelets, and a round green plate of bronze inlaid with gold. They sold it on, damaging it in the process. In February 2002, in a hotel in Basel, a buyer who said he represented a museum met the dealers. He was Harald Meller, the state archaeologist, working with the Swiss police. The disc was seized. The looters were later convicted, and one led archaeologists to the find spot.

The disc, stage by stage

The disc was changed several times before it was buried. Step through the stages archaeologists have reconstructed.

  1. Stage 1: sun, moon and stars: A large gold disc (sun or full moon), a crescent moon and 32 gold stars. Seven stars form a tight group usually identified as the Pleiades.
  2. Stage 2: the horizon arcs: Two gold arcs are added at the edges; one star is covered and two are moved. Each arc spans about 82°, close to the angle between the midsummer and midwinter sunsets on the horizon at this latitude.
  3. Stage 3: the boat: A curved gold band with fine strokes is added at the bottom, often read as a sun-boat, a motif known from Egypt and later Nordic art. The reading is an interpretation.
  4. Stage 4: the rim pierced: About 39 small holes are punched around the edge, perhaps to fix the disc to something, such as a standard or a textile.
  5. Stage 5: loss and burial: One horizon arc has gone. The disc is buried on the Mittelberg hilltop with two swords, two axes, a chisel and spiral bracelets.

    The disc

    • A bronze disc about 30 cm across and about 2 kg, now green with corrosion, originally perhaps a dark brown or black-violet.
    • Inlaid with gold: a large circle (sun or full moon), a crescent, and 32 small circles read as stars, among them a tight group of seven, usually identified as the Pleiades.
    • Analysis of the metals suggests the copper came from the eastern Alps and the gold, at least in part, from Cornwall. That provenance has been debated but is widely accepted.
    • It went on the UNESCO Memory of the World register in 2013.

    A disc that was changed

    Close study shows the disc was altered several times. The interactive object steps through the stages:

    1. Sun, moon and 32 stars.
    2. Two horizon arcs are added at the edges; to fit them, one star is covered and two are moved. Each arc spans about 82°, close to the angle between the solstice sunsets (and sunrises) on the horizon at this latitude.
    3. A curved band is added at the bottom, often read as a sun-boat.
    4. About 39 holes are punched around the rim.
    5. One arc is lost, and the disc is buried with the weapons and tools, around 1600 BCE.

    The dating dispute

    The hoard's swords and axes date the burial to the end of the Early Bronze Age, c. 1600 BCE, in the Únětice culture. In 2020, Rupert Gebhard and Rüdiger Krause argued that the looters' account of the find spot was unreliable, that the disc might not belong with the hoard, and that its style fits the Iron Age, a thousand years later. In 2021 Ernst Pernicka, Harald Meller and colleagues replied with soil, corrosion and metal analyses linking the disc to the hoard and the site. Most specialists accept the Bronze Age date, and the Iron Age proposal has few supporters.

    What was it for?

    • A calendar? The horizon arcs track the sun between midsummer and midwinter. It has been proposed (notably by Rahlf Hansen) that the crescent and Pleiades together encode a rule for adding a leap month to keep lunar and solar years aligned. Such a rule is known from much later Babylonian texts. The idea is striking and debated.
    • A religious object? The boat, the sky and the burial with weapons suggest a sacred object, perhaps used by a ruler or priest, then deposited as an offering.
    • Both. In the Bronze Age, reading the sky and serving the gods were not separate.

    Symbology and sacred encoding

    The sun-boat. The idea that the sun crosses the sky or the underworld in a boat is known from Egypt and from later Nordic Bronze Age rock carvings and razors. The band on the disc may be its earliest appearance in central Europe.

    Changing the sky. The alterations show that the disc's meaning was updated over generations: from sky, to calendar, to religious emblem.

    The Pleiades. The seven stars, visible to the naked eye, marked farming seasons across many cultures. Their setting and rising in spring and autumn were signs to sow and to harvest.

    ✦ The evidence, honestly

    What's well supported by the evidence

    • A bronze and gold disc with sun or moon, crescent and 32 stars, recovered in 2002 from a hoard looted in 1999 on the Mittelberg, and altered in several stages.
    • Its burial with the hoard c. 1600 BCE is accepted by most specialists after the 2021 analyses.

    What is not supported

    • Claims that it is a fake. Its corrosion and metallurgy have been extensively tested.
    • Claims of precise astronomical knowledge beyond what the horizon arcs and star group show.

    What's genuinely open

    • Whether it encodes a leap-month rule.
    • The meaning of the boat and the purpose of the holes.
    • Whether the 2020 Iron Age challenge has been fully answered, which some critics still dispute.
