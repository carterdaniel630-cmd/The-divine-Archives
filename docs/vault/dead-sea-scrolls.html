The Dead Sea Scrolls: A Library from the Caves

In the winter of 1946–47 a Bedouin shepherd of the Ta'amireh tribe threw a stone into a cave in the cliffs above the north-western shore of the Dead Sea, heard pottery break, and later climbed in. Inside were tall clay jars. In the jars were leather scrolls wrapped in linen. One of them, taken out of that cave (now called Qumran Cave 1), was a copy of the Book of Isaiah seven metres long, complete from its first chapter to its sixty-sixth. It was about a thousand years older than any Hebrew manuscript of Isaiah scholars had held before. Over the next decade, Bedouin searchers and archaeologists found eleven caves around the ruin of Khirbet Qumran. Between them they held the remains of roughly 900–1,000 manuscripts, most of them broken into tens of thousands of fragments.

This entry covers what the scrolls are, what they contain, how they have been read, and how they have been faked. It sets the documented archaeology apart from the theories built on top of it, the most debated being the question of who collected them.

Unroll the scroll

The words the entry discusses, set out as a scroll carries them: consonants only, hanging from ruled lines, sheets sewn edge to edge, read from the right.

Isaiah 40:3

קול קורא במדבר פנו דרך יהוה

ישרו בערבה מסלה לאלהינו

A voice calls out: “In the wilderness clear the way of the LORD; make straight in the desert a highway for our God.”

The verse as in the Masoretic Text, in consonants only. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) preserves the whole chapter. Where the pause falls (‘a voice calls: in the wilderness…’ or ‘a voice calling in the wilderness’) differs between the Hebrew accents and the Greek, and the Gospels follow the Greek.

Community Rule · 1QS VIII 13–14

…they shall separate themselves from the dwelling of the men of injustice,

to go into the wilderness, to prepare there the way of הואהא

as it is written:

“In the wilderness prepare the way of ••••

make straight in the desert a highway for our God.”

This archive's working translation. The community read Isaiah 40:3 as its own command to withdraw into the desert, and would not write the Name even when quoting it.

The Name, set apart

יהוה · 𐤉𐤄𐤅𐤄

In several scrolls written in the square script, the scribe switches to the old palaeo-Hebrew letters for this one word.

4Q521 · the one who is to come

…he will heal the wounded, give life to the dead,

and bring good news to the poor…

Compare Luke 7:22 and Matthew 11:5.

The collection: what "the Dead Sea Scrolls" actually means

Strictly, the name means the manuscripts from the eleven Qumran caves. It is often stretched to cover finds from other Judaean Desert sites, including Masada, Wadi Murabba'at and Nahal Hever. Those finds are real and important, but they are separate deposits with different histories. Many are later, from the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132–135 CE. This entry keeps them apart.

  • Materials. Most scrolls are parchment (animal skin). A minority are papyrus. One, the Copper Scroll (3Q15), is text hammered into sheets of copper.
  • Languages and scripts. The great majority are in Hebrew, a substantial number in Aramaic, and a few in Greek. Most are written in the square "Jewish" script, some in the older palaeo-Hebrew script, and a handful in cryptic scripts (see Symbology below).
  • Date range. On palaeographic and radiocarbon grounds, the scrolls run from about the third century BCE to the first century CE. The caves are generally thought to have been sealed or abandoned around the Roman destruction of the Qumran site in c. 68 CE.

What the scrolls contain

The oldest biblical manuscripts. About a quarter of the Qumran manuscripts are copies of books later collected in the Hebrew Bible. Fragments have been identified for every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther. Some books survive only as scraps, and the identification of some fragments is debated. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ), about 7.3 m long, 54 columns on 17 sheets, is the only book preserved complete. It is usually dated to roughly 150–100 BCE on palaeography, with radiocarbon ranges that overlap and extend earlier.

A plural text, not one fixed Bible. The scrolls show that in the late Second Temple period the text of scripture was not yet fixed to one form. Many copies stand close to the later Masoretic Text. Others agree with the Greek Septuagint or with the Samaritan Pentateuch against it. 4QJerᵇ has the shorter Jeremiah known from the Septuagint. 4QSamᵃ preserves a passage about the Ammonite king Nahash at 1 Samuel 11 that is missing from the Masoretic Text. Several modern Bible translations now print it.

Books that did not become "Bible". The caves held many texts that Jewish and most Christian canons later left out:

  • The oldest known copies of parts of 1 Enoch, in Aramaic. There are no copies of the "Similitudes" (chapters 37–71), a notable absence often discussed in debates about that section's date.
  • Jubilees, Tobit (in Aramaic and Hebrew), and fragments of Ben Sira (Sirach). A larger Ben Sira scroll comes from Masada, a separate find.
  • The Great Psalms Scroll (11QPsᵃ), which mixes canonical psalms with Psalm 151 and compositions found in no Bible.

The "sectarian" texts. A body of writings describes a disciplined community with its own laws, calendar, leaders and hopes for the end-time:

  • The Community Rule (1QS): admission, ranks, penalties, and the dualism of the "sons of light" and "sons of darkness".
  • The Damascus Document, also known from medieval copies found in the Cairo Genizah in the 1890s, long before Qumran.
  • The War Scroll (1QM), a battle-liturgy for the final war.
  • The pesharim, commentaries such as Pesher Habakkuk (1QpHab) that read prophecy as coded references to the community's own history: a "Teacher of Righteousness" opposed by a "Wicked Priest" and a "Man of the Lie". These are unnamed figures whose historical identities are disputed.
  • A 364-day solar calendar, in conflict with the lunar-solar calendar of the Jerusalem Temple.
  • The Temple Scroll, the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice (angelic worship), and texts such as 4Q521, which expects a figure under whom God will "heal the wounded, give life to the dead" and bring good news to the poor. The phrasing closely parallels a Gospel saying (Luke 7:22 and Matthew 11:5).

The Copper Scroll (3Q15). A list of about 64 hiding places of gold, silver and vessels, in a Hebrew close to later Mishnaic usage. No treasure from it has ever been found. Whether it records real Temple treasure, another hoard, or folklore is unresolved.

Who collected them? The central debate

The best-known interpretation, the Qumran–Essene hypothesis, was set out by the excavator Roland de Vaux and by Eleazar Sukenik. It holds that Khirbet Qumran was home to a community of Essenes, the Jewish movement described by Josephus, Philo and Pliny the Elder, and that they copied or collected the scrolls and hid them in the caves before the Romans came. Many specialists still hold a modified version of it. Its weaker points are documented, and the challenges to it are serious:

  • Norman Golb argued that the scrolls came from Jerusalem libraries hidden during the Roman siege, with no special tie to the Qumran site.
  • Yizhar Hirschfeld read Qumran as a fortified manor or estate. Yitzhak Magen and Yuval Peleg read it as a military outpost and then a pottery works.
  • Many scholars now separate the questions. The caves' contents may be larger and more varied than one site's community library, and "Essene" may be one label among several for a wider movement.

This entry does not decide the question. The link between the caves and the site is supported by pottery and by the caves' closeness to it. The identification of the community with Essenes is a reasonable but contested inference.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The divine name in an older script. In a number of scrolls written in the square script, the four-letter name of God (the Tetragrammaton, YHWH) is written in palaeo-Hebrew letters. The effect is that the name looks different from the text around it. The Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Nahal Hever does the same inside Greek text. This is a real, visible, intentional device: a way of marking holiness in the script itself.

Four dots for the Name. In the Community Rule (1QS VIII 13–14) the writer quotes Isaiah 40:3 to explain why the community goes into the wilderness, and in the quotation the divine name is replaced by four dots. Our working translation from the published Hebrew:

"…they shall separate themselves from the dwelling of the men of injustice, to go into the wilderness to prepare there the way of HIM, as it is written: 'In the wilderness prepare the way of ••••, make straight in the desert a highway for our God.'"

The word for "HIM" here is itself written in an unusual extended form (הואהא). It is usually understood as a substitute for the Name, though its exact reading is discussed. This is a documented case of a community encoding reverence in spelling.

Cryptic scripts. A few texts are written wholly or partly in invented alphabets that scholars call Cryptic A and Cryptic B. One of them, 4Q298, is addressed to the "sons of dawn". These alphabets have been deciphered, because they are simple letter-for-letter substitutions for Hebrew. They show that secrecy in script was practised here. They are not evidence of hidden messages in the biblical texts.

Mirror and mixed writing. A small astrological-physiognomic text, 4Q186, is written from left to right (reversing normal Hebrew direction) and mixes letters from several alphabets. Scholars read this as deliberate concealment of esoteric material.

Marginal signs. The Great Isaiah Scroll carries marginal marks, some resembling cryptic-script letters, whose purpose is unexplained. The Copper Scroll adds clusters of Greek letters after several of its entries. Their function is unknown, and they have prompted many treasure-hunting theories. None has been confirmed.

What is not here. Popular claims that the scrolls hold coded proof of Jesus, suppressed Gospels, or a Vatican-hidden truth have no basis in the published texts. The scrolls do not mention Jesus, John the Baptist or any Christian figure. The long delay in publishing the Cave 4 fragments fed conspiracy theories. That delay ended in 1991, when photographs were released and the full official edition, Discoveries in the Judaean Desert, followed.

Reading the scrolls with machines

Two recent studies, both published openly, show what computer methods can and cannot do here:

  • Popović, Dhali and Schomaker (2021) used pattern recognition on the ink traces of the Great Isaiah Scroll. They found statistical evidence for two main scribes whose hands are very similar. This work helps with who wrote, and it does not change what the text says.
  • Popović and colleagues (2025) trained a model called "Enoch" on handwriting from scrolls with radiocarbon dates, then predicted dates for 135 undated scrolls. Many came out older than the traditional palaeographic dates. The model is new, its training set is small, and its results are under discussion, not settled.

Forgeries: fragments that are not ancient

From the early 2000s a flow of "new" Dead Sea Scroll fragments reached the antiquities market. In 2020 an independent investigation led by Colette Loll (Art Fraud Insights), commissioned by the Museum of the Bible in Washington, D.C., concluded that all 16 of that museum's "Dead Sea Scroll" fragments were modern forgeries, written on old leather rather than on scroll parchment. Most post-2002 market fragments are now presumed suspect unless their provenance and material are proven. Any image or "fragment" presented outside an established collection deserves that caution.

How to study the images here

The Israel Antiquities Authority holds the great majority of the fragments. Through the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library it publishes high-resolution colour and multispectral images, including infrared that brings out ink on darkened skin. The Israel Museum publishes the Cave 1 scrolls, including the whole Great Isaiah Scroll, in its Digital Dead Sea Scrolls viewer. Both sets of images are copyrighted by those institutions and may not be republished here. So this entry sends you to them directly rather than copying them. The Vault's study viewer can open any other collection's scans that are published in the IIIF format.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • The scrolls are genuine ancient manuscripts, c. 3rd century BCE–1st century CE, from eleven caves near Qumran. The dating rests on palaeography and on multiple radiocarbon studies.
  • They include the oldest surviving manuscripts of the Hebrew Bible (every book except Esther is attested), and they show a pluriform biblical text (Masoretic-like, Septuagint-like and Samaritan-like forms side by side).
  • They include non-canonical Jewish scriptures (Enoch, Jubilees and others) and a set of sectarian texts describing a community with a distinctive calendar and theology. Cryptic scripts and palaeo-Hebrew divine names are documented scribal practices.

What is not supported

  • Claims that the scrolls contain hidden Christian texts, coded prophecies about modern events, or suppressed material about Jesus.
  • The authenticity of the post-2002 market fragments: the Museum of the Bible's sixteen were shown to be forgeries (2020).
  • Any claim that the Copper Scroll's treasure has been found.

What's genuinely open

  • Who collected the scrolls: a Qumran Essene community (the majority view, usually in modified form), Jerusalem libraries, or a wider movement with several centres.
  • The identities of the Teacher of Righteousness and the Wicked Priest, the meaning of the Copper Scroll, and the exact chronology of many scrolls, which new AI-assisted dating now challenges.
