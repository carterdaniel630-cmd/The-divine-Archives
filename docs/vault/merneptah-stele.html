The Merneptah Stele: The First Mention of Israel

Recently added · pending review

Held
Egyptian Museum, Cairo (JE 31408)
Date evidence
c. 1208 BCE
Recently added · pending full review. This entry is live but has not yet completed the keeper’s review pass. It is sourced to the project’s standard, but wording and detail may still change. The tag is removed once the entry is cleared.

In 1896 Flinders Petrie was digging in the ruined mortuary temple of the pharaoh Merneptah at Thebes. He found a black granite stele over three metres tall, lying face-down. One side carried an inscription of the earlier king Amenhotep III. Merneptah, the thirteenth son and successor of Ramesses II, had taken the stone and carved his own text on the back. An epigrapher, Wilhelm Spiegelberg, read it for Petrie on the spot. Near the end he came to a name he did not expect. Petrie is said to have told his colleagues that evening that this stele would be better known than anything else he had found. The name was Israel.

The Canaan lines

The closing lines (27–28), in English. Touch each name to see how the scribe classified it.

Canaan is plundered with every evil; Ashkelon is carried off; Gezer is seized; Yenoam is made as though it never was; Israel is laid waste, his seed is not; Kharu has become a widow for Egypt.

The only name in the list classed as a people rather than a land is Israel.

    The text

    • 28 lines in hieroglyphs, dated to Merneptah's fifth year, about 1208 BCE.
    • Almost all of it celebrates his victory over the Libyans and their allies, the "Sea Peoples", who invaded Egypt's western Delta.
    • The last lines turn to Canaan: "Canaan is plundered… Ashkelon is carried off; Gezer is seized; Yenoam is made as though it never was; Israel is laid waste, his seed is not; Kharu has become a widow for Egypt."
    • "His seed is not" is standard Egyptian boasting. It may mean offspring or grain, and it claims destruction, not a fact of extinction.

    What the determinatives say

    Egyptian writing adds determinatives: silent signs that say what kind of thing a word is. Canaan, Ashkelon, Gezer and Yenoam are written with the sign for a foreign land or city. Israel is written instead with the signs for a people: a throw-stick, a man and a woman, and plural strokes.

    Most scholars read this to mean that around 1208 BCE "Israel" was a group of people in Canaan, significant enough for Pharaoh to name, but not a state with a fixed territory or city. This fits the archaeology of the central highlands, where small villages multiply from about 1200 BCE. How far the scribes' use of determinatives can be pressed is itself debated.

    Why it matters, and what it doesn't show

    It is the earliest certain mention of Israel in any source. It shows a people called Israel in Canaan before the monarchy described in the Bible, and it predates the Mesha (V22) and Tel Dan (V23) stelae by over three centuries.

    It says nothing about the Exodus, Moses, or where Israel came from. It is sometimes used on both sides of the Exodus debate, but it only shows Israel already present in Canaan by c. 1208.

    A minority of scholars have questioned the reading "Israel" (for instance proposing Jezreel), but the majority reading stands. Some scholars have also connected the stele with battle reliefs at Karnak that may show Merneptah's Canaan campaign, including a group sometimes identified as Israel; that identification is disputed.

    Symbology and sacred encoding

    The king who restores order. Egyptian victory texts present the king as upholding Ma'at, cosmic order, against chaos. Foreign enemies are chaos personified, and their destruction is a ritual necessity as much as a military claim.

    The Nine Bows. Egypt's traditional enemies were symbolised as nine bows under the king's feet. The stele names them as crushed. The formula made every victory an act in a cosmic drama.

    Reused stone. Carving over an earlier king's stele was common. It borrowed the monument's sanctity while claiming it for the new king.

    ✦ The evidence, honestly

    What's well supported by the evidence

    • A granite stele of Merneptah, c. 1208 BCE, found by Petrie in 1896, whose Canaan section names Israel.
    • Israel is marked with a people determinative, unlike the cities and lands around it.

    What is not supported

    • That the stele confirms or refutes the Exodus. It is silent on Israel's origins.
    • That "his seed is not" describes a real annihilation. It is a conventional boast.

    What's genuinely open

    • What kind of group "Israel" was in c. 1208: its size, organisation and location.
    • Whether the Karnak reliefs depict the same campaign and people.
