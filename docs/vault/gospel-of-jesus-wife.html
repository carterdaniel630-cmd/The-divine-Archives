The "Gospel of Jesus's Wife": Anatomy of a Forgery

Recently added · pending review

Held
A papyrus fragment now recognised as a modern forgery
Date evidence
Papyrus old; writing modern (forgery)
Recently added · pending full review. This entry is live but has not yet completed the keeper’s review pass. It is sourced to the project’s standard, but wording and detail may still change. The tag is removed once the entry is cleared.

In September 2012, at an international congress of Coptic studies in Rome, the Harvard historian Karen L. King presented a scrap of papyrus smaller than a business card. It had eight broken lines of Coptic on one side. In one of them Jesus says, "my wife…". King was careful. She said the fragment did not prove that Jesus was married, only that some early Christians may have discussed it. She provisionally titled it The Gospel of Jesus's Wife. The news travelled around the world within hours. Four years later, the fragment had become a textbook case of how forgeries are exposed, and King herself agreed that it was almost certainly fake.

This entry is in the Vault because the method matters as much as the object. The standards that exposed it are the same standards this archive applies everywhere.

How a forgery was caught

The fragment's two famous phrases, then the evidence as it arrived.

“Jesus said to them, ‘My wife…’” “…she will be able to be my disciple…”

  • 2012 Announced in Rome: Karen King presents the fragment at the International Congress of Coptic Studies, cautiously.
  • 2012 A patchwork text: Francis Watson shows nearly every phrase comes from the Coptic Gospel of Thomas, cut up and reassembled.
  • 2014 Old papyrus: Tests date the papyrus to antiquity, but old blank papyrus is available to forgers, so the date does not fix the writing.
  • 2014 The companion fake: A Coptic John fragment from the same owner is shown to be copied line by line from a 1924 edition.
  • 2015 The modern typo: The fragment repeats an error from Michael Grondin's 2002 online edition of Thomas.
  • 2016 The owner found: Ariel Sabar traces the fragment to Walter Fritz. The provenance documents collapse.
  • 2016 King concedes: “The evidence now presses in the direction of forgery.”

The fragment

  • About 4 × 8 cm of papyrus, with eight incomplete lines in Sahidic Coptic on one side.
  • Its key phrases, in translation: "Jesus said to them, 'My wife…'" and "…she will be able to be my disciple…".
  • Provenance was supplied by the anonymous owner. It came with a typed contract and a note said to date from the 1980s, claiming the fragment came from a German collector. Both later proved false.

How it was exposed

  1. The text was patched together. Within days, the New Testament scholar Francis Watson showed that almost every phrase could be found in the Coptic Gospel of Thomas from Nag Hammadi (V08), cut up and reassembled.
  2. A modern typo. The fragment reproduced a typographical error found in a 2002 online edition of the Gospel of Thomas by Michael Grondin. It was an error no ancient scribe could have copied.
  3. A companion fragment. A second papyrus from the same owner, a fragment of the Gospel of John in Coptic, turned out to be copied line by line from a published 1924 edition of a different manuscript, with the same line breaks.
  4. Material tests misread. Radiocarbon tests dated the papyrus to antiquity, and ink analysis did not rule out an ancient origin. But old blank papyrus is available to forgers, so an old material does not date the writing. That is the same point as the "parchment versus writing" question in the Voynich entry (V01).
  5. The provenance collapsed. In 2016 the journalist Ariel Sabar, writing in The Atlantic, identified the owner as Walter Fritz, a former Egyptology student turned businessman. Fritz's documents did not hold up. Fritz denied forging the papyrus, but most scholars regard him as the likely forger.
  6. King's response. King said that the evidence now "presses in the direction of forgery".

Symbology and lesson

Forgery as mirror. The fragment said what some modern readers wanted an ancient text to say, about women disciples and a married Jesus. A text that confirms a hope too neatly deserves the closest scrutiny. This is not because the hope is wrong, but because forgers write to hopes.

The evidence pattern. A forgery was exposed by several independent lines of evidence: textual dependence, a modern error, a companion fake and provenance failure. No single test was decisive, but together they were overwhelming. That is how this archive's "not supported" verdicts are meant to work.

Connections

The Nag Hammadi Codices (V08), the forged Dead Sea Scroll fragments (V02), the James Ossuary (V16), and Early Christianity (ch16).

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • A small Coptic papyrus fragment presented by Karen King in 2012. Its text depends on a 2002 online edition of the Gospel of Thomas, including a modern typo. Its companion John fragment was copied from a 1924 edition. Its provenance was traced to Walter Fritz and did not hold up (Sabar, 2016). King accepted that it is likely a forgery.

What is not supported

  • That the fragment is an ancient witness to any early Christian belief about Jesus's marriage.

What's genuinely open

  • Who made the fragment and how. Fritz is widely regarded as the forger but denies it.
  • The separate historical question of what early Christians believed about Jesus's marital status. The canonical and early texts do not say he was married, and this fragment offers no evidence either way.
