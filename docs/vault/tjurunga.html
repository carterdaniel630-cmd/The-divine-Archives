The Tjurunga of Central Australia — Not Shown

This entry is an absence, kept on purpose.

The tjurunga are sacred boards and stones of the Arrernte and neighbouring peoples of Central Australia, bound to the ancestral beings of the Dreaming. They are secret-sacred: held hidden, and shown only in ceremony, only to those entitled to see them. To see one without that right is a serious offence in Arrernte law — so this archive shows none. No picture, no drawing, no rendering.

What can be told is the history around them. In 1896, at the Alice Springs telegraph station, Arrernte men held ceremonies before two outsiders, the biologist Baldwin Spencer and the telegraph master Francis Gillen, who published descriptions and photographs in 1899. Those photographs are now treated as something that should never have been printed. Through the twentieth century, collectors took thousands of tjurunga into museums around the world. The largest collection, gathered by T. G. H. Strehlow, is held by the Strehlow Research Centre in Alice Springs under restricted access, and museums in Australia and overseas are returning secret-sacred objects to their communities.

This archive treats the refusal to show them as part of the record, not a gap in it. The fuller history of the tradition is in the chapter on Aboriginal Australian Dreaming.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • Tjurunga are secret-sacred objects of the Arrernte and neighbouring peoples, bound to ancestral beings and places.
  • They were published and collected from the 1890s on.
  • Many are now under restricted access or being returned to their communities.

What is not supported

  • That the published descriptions and photographs give a full or authorised account. They were made by outsiders, often against the wishes of later custodians.

What's genuinely open

  • How many collected tjurunga remain in museums and private hands worldwide, and where they came from.
  • How collections such as Strehlow's should be cared for, a question for their custodians to decide.
