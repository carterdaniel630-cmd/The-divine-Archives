The Holy Mandylion: The Face Not Made by Hands

In the archive

Held
Claimed by the Holy Face of Genoa (San Bartolomeo degli Armeni) and the Mandylion of the Vatican; the original's fate is unknown
Date evidence
Legend from the 4th century; image attested at Edessa from the 6th; in Constantinople 944–1204
Era
Era V · Late Antiquity
In the archive
Eastern Orthodoxy & Byzantium · Patristic Christianity

On 16 August 944, the city of Constantinople turned out for a procession. The Byzantine army had besieged Edessa (modern Şanlıurfa in Turkey), then under Muslim rule, and had lifted the siege in exchange for the city's most sacred treasure: a cloth said to bear the face of Christ, imprinted not by any artist but by Christ himself. The emperor received it and placed it in the palace chapel of the Pharos among the empire's holiest relics. The Byzantines called it the Mandylion, "the little cloth". Its story had begun centuries earlier, with a king who wrote to Jesus.

The image through time

The legend's letter first (touch each phrase), then the documented history.

Abgar, ruler of Edessa, to Jesus the good physician… come to me and heal my suffering.

  • c. 325 The letters: Eusebius records an exchange of letters between Abgar and Jesus, from the Edessa archives. No image.
  • c. 400 A portrait: The Syriac Doctrine of Addai adds a portrait of Jesus painted by the king's envoy.
  • 544 The siege: Writing c. 593, Evagrius says an image 'made by God' saved Edessa from the Persians in 544: the first record of the cloth as miraculous.
  • 944 To Constantinople: The Byzantine army takes the image from Edessa to Constantinople, where it is received with great ceremony on 16 August.
  • 1204 Lost from view: The Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople; the image's later fate is not documented.
  • Today Two claimants: The Holy Face of Genoa and the Mandylion of Rome (Vatican) are each said to be the original. Neither has been scientifically dated.
  • 1978 The Shroud theory: Ian Wilson proposes that the Mandylion was the Shroud of Turin folded to show only the face. Most historians find the case unproven.

The legend grows

  • c. 325. The church historian Eusebius says that he found in the archives of Edessa letters between King Abgar V, who was ill, and Jesus, who promised to send a disciple to heal him. No image is mentioned.
  • c. 400. The Syriac Doctrine of Addai adds that Abgar's envoy painted a portrait of Jesus and brought it home.
  • c. 593. Evagrius Scholasticus writes that during the Persian siege of Edessa in 544, an image "made by God" (theoteuktos) saved the city. This is the first record of the image as miraculous.
  • Later legend. Jesus pressed a cloth to his wet face, leaving his likeness, and sent it to Abgar. The image was said to have been hidden in the city wall, rediscovered, and even to have printed copies of itself on tiles.

After 1204

When crusaders sacked Constantinople in 1204, the Pharos chapel's relics were scattered; some reached Paris, where the Crown of Thorns (V06) went. The Mandylion's fate is undocumented. Two images each claim to be it:

  • The Holy Face of Genoa, kept in the church of San Bartolomeo degli Armeni, said to have come to Genoa in the fourteenth century.
  • The Mandylion of Rome, long kept at San Silvestro in Capite and now in the Vatican.

Both are faces painted on cloth mounted on board in later frames. Neither has been scientifically dated, and art historians generally date both to the Middle Ages.

The Shroud theory

In 1978 the writer Ian Wilson argued that the Mandylion was actually the Shroud of Turin (V04), folded so that only the face showed. This would give the Shroud a history before its first appearance in France around 1354. The theory is attractive to Shroud supporters, but most historians find it unproven: the Mandylion was consistently described as a face on a small cloth, and the 1988 radiocarbon dating of the Shroud (itself contested) places it in the Middle Ages.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Acheiropoieta. The Mandylion is the model of the acheiropoieton, an image "not made by hands". Such images answered a theological question: if Christ himself made his image, then images of him could be holy, not idols. This mattered greatly in the Iconoclast controversy of the eighth and ninth centuries.

The icon of the Holy Face. In Orthodox art the Mandylion became a standard icon: Christ's face on a white cloth, often held by angels, placed over church doors and on battle standards.

A city's protector. At Edessa and later at Constantinople the image was a palladium, a sacred object that protected the city, like the Ark of the Covenant (V07) in Israel's wars.

Connections

Eastern Orthodoxy & Byzantium (ch60) and Patristic Christianity (ch22). Compare the Shroud of Turin (V04), the Veil of Veronica (V25), the Sudarium of Oviedo (V24) and the Tilma of Guadalupe (V13), other images claimed as not made by hands.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • The Abgar letters are recorded by Eusebius (c. 325), the portrait by the Doctrine of Addai (c. 400) and the miraculous image at Edessa by Evagrius (c. 593).
  • An image regarded as the Mandylion was brought to Constantinople in 944 and venerated there until 1204.

What is not supported

  • That the Abgar–Jesus letters are authentic. They are regarded by historians as later compositions.
  • That the Genoa or Vatican images are proven to be the Edessa cloth.
  • That the Mandylion was the Shroud. This remains a hypothesis.

What's genuinely open

  • What became of the Mandylion after 1204.
  • The dates of the Genoa and Vatican images, which have not been scientifically tested.
