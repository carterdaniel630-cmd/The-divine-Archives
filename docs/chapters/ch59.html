Slavic & Baltic Paganism

In the archive

In the year 988, on the orders of Prince Vladimir of Kiev, men tied the great idol of the thunder-god Perun to a horse's tail, dragged it down through the city while twelve men beat it with sticks, and threw it into the Dnieper — and Rus became Christian. Far to the north, the old gods held out for four more centuries: Lithuania did not accept baptism until 1387, the last pagan state in Europe. This is the story of the continent's final polytheists — and of the hard fact that we know them mostly through the words of the Christians who ended them.

The last holdouts of pagan Europe

The Slavs and the Balts were the last peoples of Europe to keep the old gods. The Slavic lands were Christianized between the 9th and 12th centuries — Kievan Rus under Vladimir the Great in 988, choosing Byzantine Orthodoxy (ch60); the western Slavs under German and Latin pressure. The Balts — Lithuanians, Latvians, and the now-vanished Old Prussians — held out longest of all, their conversion dragged out by the crusading Teutonic Knights until Lithuania's rulers finally accepted Christianity at the close of the 14th century, and folk practice persisting for centuries after. These were living European paganisms surviving into the high medieval age, contemporaries of the cathedrals and the scholastics — and precisely because they were the last, their suppression is unusually well recorded, if only by their enemies.

The Slavic gods

Our clearest snapshot is political: in 980, Prince Vladimir, seeking to unify his realm, set up six idols on a hill above Kiev — Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh — a state pantheon meant to mirror the hierarchy of princes. At its head stood Perun, the thunder-god, lord of lightning and the oak, the warrior-god of the ruling druzhina. His great counterpart was conspicuously absent from the hill: Veles (Volos), god of cattle, wealth, the underworld, music, and magic — the "serpent" god, whose shrine stood below, in the merchants' quarter by the river. The opposition of these two — the sky-thunderer above and the wooly, watery serpent below — is thought to preserve the great Slavic myth of Perun versus Veles, the storm-god's battle with the serpent (a chaoskampf, ch46). Alongside them stood Svarog (sky, fire, the divine smith), Dazhbog (the sun, "giving-god"), Stribog (wind), and Mokosh — "moist Mother Earth," the one goddess of the pantheon, spinner and protector of women. Among the western Slavs the greatest sanctuary was the temple of the four-headed Svetovid at Arkona on the island of Rügen, whose god looked to the four quarters of the world — the last great pagan Slavic shrine, destroyed by the Danes in 1168.

The Baltic gods

The Baltic pantheon, cognate but distinct, was headed by Dievas, the sky-god and supreme creator, and by Perkūnas (Latvian Pērkons), the thunder-god whose very name comes from a Baltic word for the oak — and who is a direct cousin of the Slavic Perun, both descended from the same Indo-European thunder-god. Most beloved of all was Saulė, the Sun-goddess, who appears more often than any other figure in the folk songs; with her belong Mėnuo/Mēness (the Moon), Žemyna, the earth-mother who receives the dead, and Velnias, a chthonic god of the underworld (a Baltic cousin of Veles). Baltic worship centered on sacred groves and oaks, and on the eternal fire kept perpetually burning by priests and priestesses — the felling of a sacred tree was punishable by death. Medieval sources speak of a central sanctuary, Romuva, whose undying flame was revered across the Baltic lands.

The double faith and the folk survival

Conversion did not erase the old gods; it drove them underground and into the folk. Scholars speak of dvoeverie, "double faith" — the long coexistence of Christian and pagan practice, in which the peasant kept the church's calendar and the field's older rites. Perun's thunder passed to the Christian St. Elijah; the household spirits (domovoi), the water- and forest-nymphs (rusalki, Baltic laumės), and the ambiguous witch Baba Yaga lived on in tale and custom for a thousand years. And in the Baltic lands the old religion survived, above all, in song: the dainos, the traditional Lithuanian and Latvian folk songs — more than two hundred thousand variants collected from the 1820s onward by scholars such as Krišjānis Barons — preserve Saulė, Perkūnas, and the whole sacred world of the pre-Christian Balts more fully than any chronicle.

The problem of the sources

No Slav or Balt wrote down their own theology while they still believed it. Everything we have comes from outside or after: the Christian Primary Chronicle of Nestor and other hostile clerical accounts; the reports of Arab travelers and German missionaries; the crusaders' records; and the folklore and dainos collected only in the modern era. This makes the reconstruction of Slavic and Baltic religion genuinely uncertain — the neat "pantheons" in popular books often owe as much to Romantic-era imagination and modern systematizing as to the fragmentary evidence. Some celebrated "sources," notably the Book of Veles, are outright modern forgeries. The honest picture is of a religion of the oak, the fire, the thunder, and the ancestors, richly alive but recoverable now only in pieces and through the distorting lens of those who destroyed or long outlived it.

The old gods return

Slavic and Baltic paganism has had one of the most vigorous modern revivals of any dead European religion. Rodnovery, the Slavic Native Faith, has grown across Eastern Europe since the late 20th century; and in the Baltic, Romuva in Lithuania and Dievturība in Latvia — reconstructing the old religion largely from the dainos and folklore — are among the most successful pagan revivals anywhere, with Romuva recognized (after a long campaign) as a traditional Lithuanian religion. Like modern Wicca and Paganism (ch38), these movements consciously rebuild an ancient faith from surviving fragments, and face the same honest question of how much is recovery and how much is new creation.

Symbology and sacred encoding

  • The oak and the thunder-mark. The oak — the tree most struck by lightning — is the sacred tree of both Perun and Perkūnas, and the thunder-god's power was encoded in "thunder-marks" (six-spoke wheels and rosettes) carved on houses for protection, and in the "thunder-stones" (prehistoric axes and fossils) believed to be his fallen bolts.
  • The eternal fire. The perpetually tended flame of the Baltic sanctuaries encoded the presence and continuity of the sacred and of the community itself — its extinction an omen of catastrophe.
  • The four-faced idol. The Svetovid of Arkona, carved with four heads facing the four directions, encoded a god who sees all quarters of the world at once — a striking image of divine omniscience in wood.
  • The sun and the song. In the Baltic dainos the Sun (Saulė) is encoded through recurring images — the sun's daughters, the sun's copper boat, the celestial wedding — so that the song itself became the vessel of the sacred, the way a people with little monumental architecture kept its gods alive on the breath.

Connections

Slavic and Baltic paganism belong to the wider Indo-European family: their thunder-gods Perun and Perkūnas are cousins of the Norse Thor (ch23) and, further off, the Vedic Indra (ch05), all wielding the storm against a serpent (ch46). Their conversions tie them to the two Christendoms that absorbed them — Byzantine Orthodoxy for the Rus (ch60), Latin Christianity for the Balts. Their long folk survival parallels the vernacular religion beneath every official faith in this archive, and their contested reconstruction echoes the Great Goddess debate (ch48); their modern revival stands beside Wicca and modern Paganism (ch38) as the deliberate rebuilding of the old gods.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The late Christianization of the Slavs (Vladimir's conversion of Kievan Rus in 988) and of the Balts (Lithuania in 1387, the last in Europe); Vladimir's pantheon of 980 and the throwing-down of Perun; the destruction of the Arkona temple of Svetovid in 1168; the reality of the chief deities (Perun/Perkūnas, Veles/Velnias, Mokosh, Dievas, Saulė) attested in chronicles and folklore; the practices of sacred groves, oaks, and the eternal fire; the phenomenon of dvoeverie and the rich survival of the dainos; and the modern revivals (Rodnovery, Romuva, Dievturība).

Not supported (as established fact)

Much of the systematic Slavic and Baltic mythology found in popular sources is reconstruction, built from fragmentary, hostile, and late evidence and often inflated by Romantic-era and modern invention. Specific elaborate genealogies and myths should be treated with caution. The Book of Veles and similar "recovered scriptures" are modern forgeries. The exact form of the Perun–Veles myth is a scholarly reconstruction, not a preserved text.

Genuinely open

The true shape of pre-Christian Slavic and Baltic theology behind the chronicles; how much of the folklore preserves genuine pagan belief versus later Christian-era development; and how much the modern revivals recover versus create. The archive reports what is documented (the gods' names, the conversions, the sacred oak and fire, the songs) and marks the large reconstructed portion honestly as such.
