Early Modern · Oceania

Oceania

In the archive

On a treeless island in the remotest corner of the Pacific stand hundreds of colossal stone figures, most with their backs to the sea, gazing inward over the land. They are the moai of Rapa Nui (Easter Island) — carved images of the ancestors, set up to face the living villages and to pour the ancestors' sacred power over them. They are the most famous monuments of Oceania, and they open onto a religious world built on two ideas that the English language borrowed and never gave back: mana and taboo.

The peopling of the sea

The settlement of the Pacific was one of the greatest feats in human history: over thousands of years, seafaring peoples crossed a third of the globe by canoe, navigating by the stars, the swells, and the birds to find and settle islands scattered across an ocean vaster than all the continents combined. Their religions divide, broadly, into two great regions (the third, Aboriginal Australia, has its own chapter, ch61): Polynesia, the great triangle from Hawai'i to New Zealand to Rapa Nui, with its grand gods and sacred chiefs; and Melanesia, the islands north and east of Australia, with its ancestor-spirits and secret societies. In both, the knowledge of navigation itself was sacred — a lore of the sky and sea held by master wayfinders.

Mana and tapu

Two concepts govern all of Polynesian (and much of Oceanic) religion. Mana is sacred power — a spiritual potency and efficacy that dwells in gods, in chiefs, in objects, in words, and in successful action; to have mana is to be charged with the sacred and effective in the world. Tapu (the source, via Captain Cook, of the English word taboo) is the sacred-forbidden: the state of being so charged with the sacred that contact is dangerous and restricted. The two are inseparable — what has great mana is tapu — and together they ordered all of life, from the chief's person to the preparation of food; the violation of an important tapu could be punishable by death. Society was ranked by mana: the ariki (chiefs) held it by descent from the gods, and the tohunga (Hawaiian kahuna), the priest-experts, knew how to wield and contain it.

The gods and the world-parents

Polynesian creation begins, in the classic Māori account, with Rangi, the Sky Father, and Papa, the Earth Mother, locked in embrace in the darkness, until their children force them apart to let in the light and make the world (the world-parent cosmogony of ch46). From them come the great gods, worshipped across Polynesia under related names: Tangaroa (Hawaiian Kanaloa), god of the sea; Tāne (Kāne), god of forests, birds, and light; Tū (Kū), god of war; and Rongo (Lono), god of agriculture and peace. Beloved above all is Māui, the trickster-hero who fished up the islands from the sea, snared the sun to slow it, and died seeking immortality for humankind. Regional deities loom large — the Hawaiian volcano goddess Pele, beautiful and destroying. Worship centered on the open sacred precinct, the marae (Hawaiian heiau), with offerings, chant, feasting, and — in some societies — human sacrifice to the war-god.

Rapa Nui: the ancestors in stone

Rapa Nui developed a self-contained Polynesian religious world of extraordinary intensity. Its people carved and raised nearly a thousand moai, ancestor-images set on stone platforms (ahu) facing the villages, channeling the ancestors' mana to protect and nourish the living. In a later age of upheaval the moai were toppled, and a new cult arose: the birdman (tangata manu) cult of the creator-god Makemake, in which each year the young men competed to retrieve the first sooty-tern egg from an offshore islet, the winner becoming the sacred birdman for the year. (The old story that the Rapa Nui simply destroyed their own environment and society in a self-inflicted "collapse" is now seriously contested by recent archaeology, which finds a more resilient and complex history.)

Melanesia: ancestors, masks, and cargo

Melanesian religion followed a different pattern — generally without Polynesia's grand pantheon of high gods, and centered instead on ancestor spirits and local powers, elaborate masks and carvings, and initiatory secret societies. Leadership was often the achievement of the "big man," whose status was earned through feasting and exchange rather than inherited by sacred descent, though mana operated here too. And Melanesia produced one of the most studied religious phenomena of the modern age: the cargo cults — millenarian movements, hundreds of them, in which prophets announced the imminent return of ancestors or heroes bringing "cargo," the manufactured wealth of the outsiders. The most famous, the John Frum movement on Tanna (from the late 1930s), still awaits its promised deliverer. Far from being merely "confused," these movements are logical within a cosmology in which wealth flows from the spirit world and ancestors provide for their descendants: if that is so, then performing the right rituals to obtain the cargo makes perfect sense — a genuine apocalyptic hope (ch50) born of colonial encounter.

Symbology and sacred encoding

  • The ancestor image. The moai and the smaller carved tiki encode the ancestor made present and the mana he channels to the living — the megalithic ancestor-cult of the Pacific, kin to the standing stones of the Neolithic (ch42).
  • The tatau. Polynesian tattoo (tatau — the origin of the English word) is sacred encoding written on the body: its motifs record genealogy, rank, milestones, and protection, so that a person's history and mana are inscribed in their skin.
  • Mana and tapu are themselves a symbolic system — an invisible economy of sacred power and prohibition mapped onto every person, object, and act, making the whole social world a diagram of the sacred.
  • The star-path. The wayfinder's knowledge — the rising and setting points of the stars, the swells, the flight of birds — is an encoded cosmology of navigation, the sky read as a sacred map by which a people found their islands.

Connections

Oceanic religion contributes two of the most important concepts in the entire comparative study of religion: mana and taboo, which anthropology took as keys to the nature of the sacred itself — cousins of the Native American orenda and Wakan Tanka (ch62). Its world-parents Rangi and Papa belong with the creation cosmogonies of ch46; its moai ancestor-cult with the megaliths (ch42) and the reverence for the dead throughout this archive; its cargo cults with the apocalyptic and millenarian movements of ch50 and the new religious movements of ch35. It stands with the Aboriginal (ch61) and Native North American (ch62) traditions as one of the world's great living oral and Indigenous religious worlds.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The Austronesian settlement of the Pacific and the reality of sacred wayfinding navigation; the master-concepts of mana and tapu/taboo; the Polynesian pantheon (Tangaroa/Kanaloa, Tāne/Kāne, Tū/Kū, Rongo/Lono, Māui, Pele) and the Rangi-and-Papa creation; the marae/heiau cult (including human sacrifice in some societies); the Rapa Nui moai as ancestor images and the later birdman cult of Makemake; the Melanesian ancestor cults and secret societies; and the historical cargo cults (John Frum and many others). The Polynesian origin of the word "tattoo" (via Cook) is documented.

Not supported (as established fact) / contested

The popular "ecocide/collapse" narrative of Rapa Nui — that the islanders thoughtlessly destroyed their environment and civilization — is now seriously disputed by recent archaeology and should not be told as settled fact. The term "cargo cult" is an outsider label, and the condescending caricature of the movements as mere confusion is rejected here in favor of their own internal logic. Much of what is recorded about pre-contact Oceanic religion comes through missionary and colonial sources and carries their distortions.

Genuinely open

The precise pre-contact forms of many island religions (often reconstructed from post-contact records); the full history and meaning of the moai and the reasons for their toppling; and, as with the other living traditions, the archive reports the documented and public and leaves what is held privately to its custodians.
