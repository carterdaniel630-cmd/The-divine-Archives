Early Modern · Sikhism

Sikhism

In the archive

The faith born in the Punjab five centuries ago from the meeting of Hindu devotion and Muslim mysticism — a religion of one formless God, honest work, and radical human equality, shaped by ten Gurus and a scripture that became a living master — told with care for its documented history and for the founding story it tells about itself.

"There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim"

One morning around 1499, near the town of Sultanpur in the Punjab, a man named Nanak went down to the river Bein to bathe and did not come back. His clothes were found on the bank; his family gave him up for drowned. Three days later he reappeared — and his first recorded words were: "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim." He did not mean that the two faiths were false, but that before the one God the labels dissolved: what mattered was not the community one was born into but the truth one lived. From that river Guru Nanak (1469–1539) emerged as the founder of a new religion — Sikhism, the way of the sikhs, the "disciples" of the one God.

What Sikhism is

Sikhism is a monotheistic religion founded in the Punjab in the late fifteenth century, today the faith of some twenty-five to thirty million people and the fifth-largest in the world. Its center is devotion to a single, formless, creator God — named in the opening words of its scripture as Ik Onkar, "One God" — approached not through ritual, image, priest, or pilgrimage but through three plain disciplines: naam simran (loving remembrance of the divine Name), kirat karni (earning an honest living), and vand chakna (sharing with others). It rejects, root and branch, the caste system, idol worship, ritualism, superstition, and the domination of priests — and insists on the absolute equality of all human beings, women and men, of every birth.

Guru Nanak and the first community

Born in 1469 near Lahore, Nanak spent decades on long journeys — the udasis — carrying his message across South Asia and beyond, before settling at Kartarpur on the Ravi, where he founded the first Sikh community. There he built its enduring institutions: the sangat, the congregation in which all sat as equals, and the langar, the free communal kitchen where people of every caste and creed ate the same food from the same floor — a deliberate, daily assault on the barriers of caste. His teaching was carried in hymns, sung, that fused the fervor of the Bhakti saints and the Sufi mystics into something new.

The ten Gurus

Nanak was the first of ten Gurus, each adding to the work of the last across two centuries. Angad shaped the Gurmukhi script for the community's tongue; Amar Das entrenched the langar and the standing of women; Ram Das founded the city of Amritsar; Arjan compiled the first scripture and built the Harmandir Sahib, the Golden Temple — and in 1606 became the first martyr, executed under the Mughals. Persecution then forged the community: Hargobind took up the sword and the doctrine of miri-piri, the union of temporal and spiritual power; Tegh Bahadur was beheaded in 1675 for defending the religious freedom of Hindus not even of his own faith. The tenth, Gobind Singh, completed the line — and, in 1708, declared that there would be no more human Gurus: the scripture itself would be the eternal Guru.

The Guru Granth Sahib

That scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, is unlike any other. Compiled by Guru Arjan (1604) and sealed by Guru Gobind Singh, written in Gurmukhi, it contains the hymns of the Sikh Gurus — and, remarkably, also the verses of Hindu and Muslim saints, including the Bhakti poet Kabir, the low-caste Ravidas, and the Sufi Baba Farid. It is perhaps the only major scripture that enshrines the words of other faiths within its own. And it is treated not as a book but as a living Guru: enthroned under a canopy, ceremonially put to rest at night, and consulted as the present voice of the divine teacher.

The Khalsa and the Five Ks

In 1699, at Anandpur, Guru Gobind Singh called the community together and founded the Khalsa, the order of the "pure": the initiated brotherhood-and-sisterhood of the faith, baptized with sweetened water stirred by a double-edged sword. Its members take the names Singh ("lion") and Kaur ("princess"), and wear the Five Ks — kesh (uncut hair), kangha (a comb), kara (a steel bracelet), kachera (a cotton undergarment), and kirpan (a sword) — the visible uniform of a people who would never again be invisible or defenceless. In the Khalsa the Sikh ideal took its lasting form: the sant-sipahi, the saint-soldier, devoted to God and sworn to defend the oppressed.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Sikhism carries its theology in a handful of potent signs.

  • Ik Onkar (ੴ) — the opening glyph of the scripture, joining the numeral one to Onkar, the primal sound of God — is itself the creed in a single symbol: reality is One.
  • The Khanda, the Sikh emblem, encodes the whole faith in steel: the central double-edged sword for the one truth and the power of God, the chakkar (a ring) for the eternity and unity of the divine, and the two flanking kirpans of miri and piri — worldly and spiritual authority held in balance.
  • The Five Ks are theology worn on the body: uncut hair as acceptance of God's will, the sword as the duty to justice — a faith one cannot take off.
  • And at the heart of it all is the Shabad, the divine Word, and the Naam, the Name: to sing the scripture (kirtan) and repeat the Name is to encode God in sound — the Sikh member of the same family as the Sufi dhikr, the Bhakti nama-japa, and the mantra.

Connections

Sikhism grew directly from the meeting this book has been tracing: the nirguna Bhakti of Kabir and the Sufism of medieval India, whose love of a formless God beyond Hindu and Muslim became, in Nanak, a wholly new faith — and whose poets Sikhism honored by placing their verses in its own scripture. It rose in the same century as the Reformation half a world away, and rhymes with it uncannily: a vernacular scripture, a priesthood rejected, a return to the one God over ritual and hierarchy. From a devotional movement it was hammered by Mughal persecution into a distinct and martial nation of faith, and it stands today as one of the world's great living religions — and the youngest of the traditions born before the modern age.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The historical Gurus and their dates, from Nanak (1469–1539) to Gobind Singh (d. 1708); the founding of Kartarpur, Amritsar, and the langar; the compilation of the Guru Granth Sahib (1604) and its inclusion of non-Sikh saints; the martyrdoms of Arjan (1606) and Tegh Bahadur (1675); and the founding of the Khalsa (1699). These rest on solid historical record.

Not supported (as history)

Nanak's revelation and the miracle-tales of his life — his three days in the river, the wonders of the janamsakhi birth-narratives — are matters of faith and devotional legend, not documented history. The theological claims of the tradition are recorded here as belief.

Genuinely open — and handled with care

The precise biography of Guru Nanak is uncertain, since the janamsakhis are later, devotional, and layered. And there is a real, sensitive scholarly discussion about how far Sikhism is a synthesis of the Sant/Bhakti and Sufi currents around it and how far a wholly original revelation — a question on which the tradition's own self-understanding and academic history do not always agree. This chapter records the debate; it does not presume to settle a living faith's account of itself.
