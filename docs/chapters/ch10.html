Axial Age · Second Temple Judaism

Second Temple Judaism

In the archive

In 1947 a shepherd threw a stone into a cliff-cave by the Dead Sea and heard pottery break. The scrolls inside demolished a comfortable assumption — that the Judaism of this age was a single settled religion. It was a ferment of many Judaisms.

Jars in a cliff

In 1947 a Bedouin shepherd, tossing stones into a cave above the Dead Sea at Qumran, heard pottery shatter. Inside were tall clay jars, and inside them scrolls that had lain in the dark for nearly two thousand years. Eleven caves eventually gave up some 900 manuscripts: the oldest copies of the Hebrew Bible by a thousand years, and the private library of a Jewish community no one had known existed — with its own rule book, calendar, hymns, and conviction that it alone were the “sons of light” awaiting God’s war against the “sons of darkness.”

The Dead Sea Scrolls demolished a comfortable assumption. Before them, the Judaism of this age was imagined as a single, settled, law-bound religion — the tidy backdrop to the New Testament. The scrolls revealed a Judaism of astonishing diversity, and scholars now speak, in the plural, of the Judaisms of the Second Temple period. This chapter is about that ferment — the roughly six centuries (c. 516 BCE to 70 CE) in which the religion of pre-exilic Israel (Chapter 7) was transformed into the ancestor of Judaism, Christianity, and, later, Islam.

The exile that made a religion

The transformation began with catastrophe. In 586 BCE the Babylonians burned Jerusalem, destroyed Solomon’s Temple, and carried the Judean elite into exile. For a people whose theology was bound to a land, a temple, and a king, this should have been the end — in the ancient logic, a defeated nation’s god had been defeated too.

Instead, the exile forged a new faith. Rather than concede that Babylon’s gods had won, the exilic prophets concluded that Yahweh had used Babylon to punish his own people — and, pushing monolatry across the final line into monotheism, the prophet scholars call Second Isaiah declared the other gods simply nothing at all. The arrow reversed in Chapter 7 reaches its point here. The exile also forced two innovations that would outlast every temple: worship began to detach from sacrifice, turning to prayer, study, and the reading of texts (the seed of the synagogue); and the texts themselves moved to the center, so that a people came to define itself by a book. When Cyrus of Persia permitted the return in 538 BCE (the Second Temple was finished c. 516 BCE), the exiles came back the inventors of a religion that could survive the loss of everything external.

An age of empires

Second Temple Judaism was lived under a succession of foreign empires: the Persian period (539–332 BCE), two centuries under Zoroastrian-ruled Achaemenid rule, when Ezra and Nehemiah reestablished Torah as the community’s constitution; the Hellenistic period (332–63 BCE), when Alexander’s conquest flooded in Greek culture and the Seleucid Antiochus IV’s attempt to suppress Judaism sparked the Maccabean Revolt (from 167 BCE; the Temple’s rededication is still celebrated as Hanukkah) and the independent Hasmonean state; and the Roman period (from 63 BCE), when Herod rebuilt the Temple colossally and tensions built to the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. The struggle running through it all is the encounter with Hellenism: how much Greek thought could a Torah-centered people absorb before it stopped being itself?

New heavens and new fears

A whole apparatus of ideas that is thin or absent in the older Hebrew Scriptures appears, developed, in this period — and because this is where the Zoroastrian question of Chapter 6 comes due, it must be laid out carefully:

  • Satan. In the oldest texts ha-satan (“the accuser”) is a prosecutor in God’s court (as in Job); here he hardens into a proper name and a cosmic adversary — Belial, the prince of darkness.
  • Angels and demons. The vague “sons of God” become a ranked hierarchy of named archangels (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael) and, in 1 Enoch, a mythology of fallen angels (the Watchers).
  • Resurrection and judgment. The shadowy old afterlife gives way, first clearly in Daniel 12:2, to the resurrection of the dead and a final division of righteous and wicked.
  • Apocalyptic. A new genre — apocalypse (“unveiling”) — shows a seer the hidden shape of history and its imminent, catastrophic end (Daniel, 1 Enoch; the light-vs-darkness dualism of Qumran).

The Zoroastrian question, honestly. For two centuries the Jews of Judea lived inside a Persian empire whose religion (Chapter 6) already taught a cosmic struggle of good and evil, a hierarchy of divine beings, individual judgment, resurrection, and a final renovation — and it is precisely in and after that window that these ideas blossom in Judaism. The timing and parallels are real, and many scholars see genuine influence, especially on apocalyptic dualism and angelology. But direct borrowing is unprovable, and the honest word is “resonance” rather than demonstrated dependence: the Zoroastrian texts were written down late, the parallels are partial, and Jewish thinkers were also responding to their own scriptures and to Hellenism. Real, important, widely argued, and not settled — this project reports the debate, it does not close it.

Many Judaisms: the sects

Our main witness, the historian Josephus, describes several “schools”:

  • The Pharisees — a lay movement devoted to Torah and an Oral Law, believing in resurrection and angels; marginal to the Temple but closest to the people, and the ancestors of the rabbis who carry Judaism forward after 70 CE.
  • The Sadducees — the priestly, aristocratic elite of the Temple, accepting only the written Torah and denying the resurrection; they vanished when the Temple fell.
  • The Essenes — a strict, communal, often celibate movement awaiting God’s end; most scholars link them to the Qumran community of the Scrolls, though this is debated.
  • The Zealots and Sicarii — revolutionaries against Rome; the Samaritans — a parallel people with their own Torah and temple on Mount Gerizim; and the vast diaspora, above all Alexandria, whose thinker Philo fused Torah with Greek philosophy by reading scripture allegorically.

And near the period’s end, a small Jewish messianic movement around a Galilean teacher named Jesus — at this stage simply one more Judaism, whose chapter belongs to the next era.

The library: scripture and its rivals

There was, as yet, no single fixed “Bible.” What existed was a contested library: the emerging Hebrew Tanakh (the Torah settled first, the Writings still fluid — the old “Council of Jamnia” that supposedly fixed the canon is now largely discredited); the Septuagint, the Greek translation revered by diaspora Jews and the early Church; the Apocrypha/Deuterocanon (Tobit, Judith, Ben Sira, Wisdom, 1–2 Maccabees); the Pseudepigrapha (1 Enoch, Jubilees, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch), carrying much of the age’s apocalyptic imagination; and the Dead Sea Scrolls’ sectarian writings — the Community Rule, the War Scroll, the pesharim, the enigmatic Copper Scroll.

Law, ritual, and the two centers

At the heart stood the Torah and its interpretation (halakhah), and under imperial pressure the identity-markers intensified: circumcision, the Sabbath, the dietary laws, and ritual purity became the boundaries that kept a people distinct — the very things the Maccabean crisis was fought over. Practice had two centers. The first was the Temple, rebuilt gloriously by Herod, where a hereditary priesthood offered sacrifice, where Israel streamed for the pilgrimage festivals, and where once a year on Yom Kippur the high priest entered the Holy of Holies (now empty — the Ark long lost). The second was the emerging synagogue — a house of prayer and Torah-reading needing no altar or priest. When Rome destroyed the Temple in 70 CE, it was the synagogue and Pharisaic Torah-scholarship that let Judaism survive the loss.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The unspeakable Name and the rise of number. Reverence for the divine Name deepened until YHWH was no longer pronounced, replaced in reading by Adonai. Contact with Greek isopsephy helped Jewish gematria (letter-number values) take clearer shape, though its flowering lies later. Honesty note: the deliberate number-symbolism of the ancient apocalyptic writers is a genuine literary device; the modern “Bible code” claims (hidden messages found by equidistant-letter-sequence searches) are a twentieth-century, statistically discredited phenomenon and must not be confused with it.

Apocalyptic as code. The apocalypses are literally encoded political theology: Daniel’s four beasts are a cipher for four empires, his “seventy weeks” encode a chronology of the end, his “time, times, and half a time” hides a span under hostile rulers. To read an apocalypse rightly was to decode current history as a divine script — the same impulse the Qumran pesharim turned on the prophets.

The Temple as a model of the cosmos. Philo and Josephus read the Temple as an encoded universe: its three zones as heaven, earth, and sea; the veil of four colors as the four elements; the high priest’s vestments as a wearable diagram of the cosmos, so that entering God’s presence he carried all creation with him.

Scripture on the body and the door. The instinct first met in the Ketef Hinnom amulets (Chapter 7) matured into the tefillin (small boxes of scripture bound on arm and head, found at Qumran) and the mezuzah on the doorpost — the sacred text made a physical, protective presence, encoding the covenant onto the surfaces of daily life.

Connections

This chapter is a great junction: the direct sequel to pre-exilic Israel (Chapter 7), completing the journey to monotheism; where the fingerprints of Zoroaster (Chapter 6) are most hotly sought; and played out in the Babylon and Persia of Chapters 3 and 6. Sideways, it is locked in dialogue with Hellenism (the Septuagint, Philo), tying it to Chapter 8. Forward, it is the seedbed of the next era’s giants: Christianity, which begins as one of these Second Temple Judaisms; Rabbinic Judaism, the Pharisees’ heirs; and, more distantly, Islam, Gnosticism, and Kabbalah.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The Babylonian exile (586 BCE) was decisive: it is in and after it that monolatry becomes explicit monotheism (Second Isaiah), Torah moves to the center, and text-centered worship (the seed of the synagogue) emerges.
  • Second Temple Judaism was strikingly diverse (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, Zealots, Samaritans, diaspora Jews), as the Dead Sea Scrolls confirmed.
  • A large, layered literature existed with no single fixed canon: the emerging Tanakh, the Septuagint, the Apocrypha, and the Pseudepigrapha.
  • New developments — a personalized Satan, ranked angels/demons, resurrection and judgment, and the apocalyptic genre — appear developed here; the historical arc (Persian→Hellenistic→Roman; the Maccabees; 70 CE) is well established.

What’s not supported

  • A single, monolithic “Judaism” in this era — it was plural.
  • A fixed, early canon settled by a “Council of Jamnia” (discredited); canonization was gradual and still open.
  • That Zoroastrian influence on Jewish angelology, dualism, and eschatology is a proven fact — the parallels and window are real, but direct borrowing is unprovable (“resonance,” not demonstrated dependence).

What’s genuinely open

  • The extent and direction of influence — Zoroastrian and Hellenistic — on Second Temple theology.
  • The identity of the Qumran community (the Essene link is majority but debated) and its “Teacher of Righteousness.”
  • The dating and provenance of many pseudepigrapha.
  • How the diverse messianic expectations related — taken up directly in the Christianity chapter.
