High Medieval · Sufism

Sufism

In the archive

The mystical heart of Islam — a path of love, remembrance, and the annihilation of the self in God, carried by wandering ascetics, world-shaping philosophers, and the greatest devotional poets who ever lived — told with care for what the tradition teaches, what history can trace, and the ways the modern West has remade it.

The turning

A man stands in a circle in a white robe, a tall camel-hair hat on his head, arms crossed over his chest. Music begins — reed flute, drum, the chanted name of God — and he begins to turn. His arms open: the right palm lifts toward heaven, the left tips down toward the earth, and grace, the tradition says, pours through him from the one to the other. He spins, and keeps spinning, the robe flaring into a white disc, until the self that watches and counts and fears seems to dissolve into the turning. The hat is his ego’s tombstone; the robe, its shroud. This is the sema, the ceremony of the Mevlevi order — the “whirling dervishes” — a prayer danced rather than spoken, the soul’s journey out of separation into union with God.

The dervishes turn in memory of one man: Jalal ad-Din Rumi, whose verses are, eight hundred years later, among the best-selling poetry in the world. That fame is part of this chapter’s problem — because the Rumi the modern West adores has often been stripped of the thing he lived and breathed. Sufism is the inward, mystical dimension of Islam, and to describe it honestly is to keep it whole.

What Sufism is

Sufism — in Arabic tasawwuf — is the mysticism of Islam: the pursuit of direct, experiential nearness to the one God, beyond mere law and doctrine, through love (mahabba) and the ceaseless remembrance of God (dhikr). The name most likely comes from suf, the coarse wool of the early ascetics. Its practitioners walk a path (tariqa) under a master, ascending through stations (maqamat) and passing through God-given states (ahwal) toward two linked goals: fana, the annihilation of the ego-self, and baqa, subsistence in God.

Origins

Sufism arose early, among ascetics who recoiled from the sudden wealth of the Umayyad empire and turned to poverty, prayer, and the love of God. The great early figure is Hasan al-Basri (d. 728), the preacher remembered as a founder of the science of purifying the heart. A generation later Rabia al-Adawiyya (d. 801) — a freed slave woman of Basra — gave Sufism its most radical doctrine: love of God for God’s own sake alone. The tradition remembers her walking the streets with a torch and a bucket, meaning to burn down paradise and quench the fires of hell, so that people would love God neither for reward nor from terror, but purely.

How this piety became the vast mystical system of later centuries is genuinely debated. The old account of a smooth “transition from zuhd (asceticism) to tasawwuf” has been questioned, and scholars still argue how much Sufism drew on Neoplatonism, Christian monasticism, or Indian ideas versus growing from seeds in the Qur’an itself. Sufis insist on the latter: that theirs is the inner meaning of Islam, traced through an unbroken chain (silsila) of masters back to Muhammad.

Al-Ghazali: the great reconciliation

The hinge of Sufi history is Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 1111), the most celebrated theologian of his age. At the height of his fame, holding the foremost chair at the Nizamiyya academy in Baghdad, he suffered a shattering crisis — recounted in his autobiography, Deliverance from Error — abandoned his post, and wandered for years. Out of it came the Ihya Ulum al-Din (“The Revival of the Religious Sciences”), which wove Sufi inwardness together with orthodox Sunni law and theology and argued that the mystical path was the living core of the faith. More than any other act, al-Ghazali’s synthesis made Sufism respectable — the beating heart of mainstream Islam for the next seven centuries.

The great flowering

  • Ibn Arabi (1165–1240), the Andalusian “Greatest Master” (al-Shaykh al-Akbar), poured out over three hundred works, above all the Fusus al-Hikam and the immense Meccan Openings. His doctrine of wahdat al-wujud, the “unity of being,” holds that only God truly exists and all creation is the self-disclosure of the divine — a vision debated and condemned ever since.
  • Rumi (1207–1273), writing in Persian in Konya, composed the Mathnawi, some 25,000 couplets later Sufis called “the Qur’an in Persian.” After the loss of his companion Shams of Tabriz, his grief became a fountain of verse on divine love.
  • Attar of Nishapur, whose Conference of the Birds allegorizes the soul’s journey to God, and Suhrawardi, the philosopher of illumination, stand beside them.

The orders

This era’s other great development is institutional: Sufism organized into tariqas — orders, each a lineage descending through a silsila from a founding saint to the Prophet. The Qadiriyya grew around Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (d. 1166); the Chishtiyya carried Sufism deep into India; the Mevlevi formed around Rumi (c. 1250); the Shadhiliyya, Rifa’iyya, and many more followed. Each gathered in a lodge — khanqah, zawiya, or tekke — around a living shaykh to whom the disciple (murid) submitted utterly, as (the saying went) a corpse in the hands of the washer. Through these orders Sufism became the everyday religion of millions and the chief engine of Islam’s spread across Africa, Anatolia, Central Asia, and the Indian subcontinent.

Practice

The cornerstone of Sufi life is dhikr, the remembrance of God — rhythmic repetition of his names or short formulas, aloud or silent, alone or in swaying, chanting assemblies. Beyond it lie muraqaba (silent meditation), tafakkur (contemplation), breath-work, and sama, the “spiritual audition” of music and sacred dance. The seeker climbs the stations — repentance (tawba), patience (sabr), gratitude (shukr), trust (tawakkul) — toward love (mahabba) and finally ma’rifa, the tasted knowledge of God no book can give. Through all of it the work is to wear down the nafs, the grasping ego-self, until only God remains.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Sufism hides its deepest teaching, like the traditions beside it, inside an encoded language — and its symbols are among the most beautiful in any faith.

  • The Ninety-Nine Beautiful Names of God (al-asma al-husna) are the substance of dhikr: each name a facet of the divine, told on beads until the reciter is saturated in the quality he names.
  • The wine imagery of Sufi poetry is a complete symbolic system, not a celebration of drink. The wine is divine love; drunkenness is fana; the cup is the body and mind; the tavern of ruin is the state beyond the ego; the Cupbearer (Saqi) and the Beloved are God. What reads on the surface as forbidden and erotic is, to the initiate, the most sacred thing there is — a deliberate veil over the mystery.
  • The sema is enacted cosmology: the dervish becomes an axis between heaven and earth, the right hand receiving grace from above and the left passing it to the world, turning as the planets turn around the sun of God.
  • The heart (qalb) is the true organ of knowing — not the reasoning mind but the polished heart, in which, cleansed of rust by dhikr, the divine is reflected.

A necessary honesty: the Rumi quoted on a million calendars is frequently a modern invention. His most popular English “translators” worked from other translations, not the Persian, and quietly stripped away the Qur’an, the Prophet, and the Islam that saturate every page of the original, remaking a devout Muslim jurist and mystic into a creed-free guru. The real Rumi cannot be understood outside the faith he served.

Connections

Sufism is the mystical inner path of the Islam of the earlier era, and the exact counterpart of the Kabbalah of the neighboring chapter — two initiatory, love-drunk mysticisms rising inside two Abrahamic faiths in the same centuries, each with its coded poetry, its chain of masters, and its ladder to a hidden God. Its philosophical wing, especially Ibn Arabi, ran parallel to the great scholastic systems of the age and drew, like them, on Neoplatonism. It shaped the devotional life of half the Muslim world, and in the modern age became the most exported and most transformed of all Islamic traditions.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The rise of Sufism from early Islamic asceticism; the historical figures of Hasan al-Basri, Rabia, al-Ghazali, Ibn Arabi, and Rumi, and the dates and content of their major works.
  • The formation of the tariqa orders (Qadiriyya, Chishtiyya, Mevlevi, and the rest) in the high medieval period.
  • The core practices of dhikr, sama, and the master–disciple path.

What’s not supported (as history)

  • The miracles (karamat) of the Sufi saints are matters of faith, not historical finding.
  • Ibn Arabi’s wahdat al-wujud is a theological vision, described here, neither proven nor refuted.
  • The popular modern image of a universal, de-Islamized Rumi is a documented distortion of a poet who was a practicing Muslim through and through.

What’s genuinely open — and contested

  • The origins debate: how much Sufism owes to Neoplatonic, Christian-monastic, or Indian influence versus purely Qur’anic roots, and whether the “zuhd-to-tasawwuf” model holds.
  • The authenticity of some sayings and works attributed to the early masters.
  • A live intra-Islamic dispute this book does not settle: revivalist movements (Wahhabi, Salafi) condemn saint-tomb veneration and organized dhikr as bid’a, while most premodern Muslims embraced them.
