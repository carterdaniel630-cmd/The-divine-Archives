Creation & the First Order

In the archive

How the ordered world came to be — the same raw materials of water and darkness, made into very different worlds. Four shapes recur (order from a watery chaos, order won by combat, the world built from a slain body, the world spoken into being) and, against them all, one ancient hymn that looks at the beginning and admits it cannot be sure.

A word that remembers a monster

The second verse of the Hebrew Bible names a strange old thing: tehom, “the deep,” the dark formless water over which the wind of God hovers before anything is made. The word is a fossil. Tehom is linguistically kin to Tiamat — the salt-sea monster whom the Babylonian god Marduk hacks in two to build the world. Genesis keeps the ancient watery chaos but drains it of menace: tehom is no goddess and no dragon, and God does not fight it — he speaks, and it obeys. In that one demythologized word lies the drama of this comparative chapter: nearly every people who tried to say how the world began started from water, darkness, and a formless first state — and then made very different things of them.

Order out of the waters

The oldest cosmogonies begin wet. Babylon’s Enuma Elish starts as a mingling of Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (the salt sea). Egypt’s world rises from Nun, the limitless primordial ocean, on a first mound of dry earth. Genesis’s spirit of God moves over the face of tehom. Even the Greeks remembered Okeanos, the world-encircling river Homer calls “the origin of all.” To people who watched land emerge each year from the receding flood of the Nile or the Euphrates, creation looked like dry order lifting out of dark water. For Egypt that ocean is not evil but a reservoir of potential; for the Mesopotamian and Canaanite it can be a hostile chaos to be defeated — the second great shape.

The world won by combat: the chaoskampf

Scholars call it the chaoskampf, the “struggle against chaos”: a warrior-god defeats a monstrous, often aquatic power and builds the ordered world from the victory. Marduk slays Tiamat, splits her carcass “like a shellfish” into sky and earth, and sets the stars and the calendar in order. In Canaan the storm-god Baal battles Yamm (“Sea”) and the dragon Lotan. The Hebrew Bible keeps the same imagery in its poetry — God crushes Rahab, cuts the dragon, shatters the heads of Leviathan (Psalm 74, Isaiah 51, Job 26) — even though Genesis 1 omits it. And in Greece Zeus blasts the hundred-headed Typhon. Genesis 1 is the pointed exception: sharing the combat myth’s vocabulary (tehom/Tiamat, the great sea-monsters of the fifth day), it refuses the fight — a deliberate demythologizing to insist the one God met no rival.

The world from a body: the cosmic giant

A stranger shape builds the world from the dismembered body of a primordial being. The Norse gods Odin, Vili, and Vé slay the frost-giant Ymir and make the earth from his flesh, the sea from his blood, the mountains from his bones, the sky-dome from his skull. The Rigveda (10.90, the Purusha Sukta) sacrifices the cosmic Man Purusha: the moon from his mind, the sun from his eye — and, in a socially momentous verse, the four classes of society from his mouth, arms, thighs, and feet. Babylon’s world is built from Tiamat’s split body; China’s from the giant Pangu. The philologist Bruce Lincoln argued the Norse and Vedic versions are close enough — even in the paired correspondences (bone/rock, hair/plant, blood/water) — to descend from a shared Proto-Indo-European myth. A striking parallel — and, as the honesty note says, a reconstruction, since the image also appears where no Indo-European link exists.

The world spoken, and the question of nothing

A fourth shape needs neither water nor battle: the world is spoken into being. In Egypt’s Memphite Theology, Ptah conceives the world in his heart and brings it forth with his tongue. Genesis shares the structure exactly: God says “Let there be light.” That divine word runs on into personified Wisdom, the Logos, and “In the beginning was the Word.” And here a familiar belief needs honesty: many assume Genesis teaches creation out of nothing (creatio ex nihilo). But its Hebrew more likely describes God ordering a pre-existing chaos. The first verse can be read “In the beginning God created” (Septuagint, KJV) or, following Rashi and the JPS/NRSV, “When God began to create… the earth being formless and void” — on which reading the formless waters are already there. The full doctrine of creation from nothing crystallized only later, in the early Christian centuries, sharpened against the Gnostic systems of Chapters 17 and 45.

The hymn that would not answer

Against all this confident world-building stands the Nasadiya Sukta, the “Hymn of Creation,” Rigveda 10.129. It opens by refusing every category: “Then there was neither non-existence nor existence… neither death nor immortality… no sign of night or day.” Something breathed, windless, by its own power. And then it does what no other ancient creation text dares — it admits it does not know: “Who really knows? Who here will proclaim it? Whence this creation? The gods came afterwards… Who then knows whence it has arisen?” It ends by wondering whether even “the overseer of this world in the highest heaven… knows — or perhaps he does not know.” Carl Sagan loved it for its “humility before the great cosmic mysteries.” Beside every people that told a confident story of the beginning stands at least one ancient voice that looked at the darkness and said, we cannot be sure.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The cosmic egg. A single seed hatches the cosmos: the Rigveda’s Hiranyagarbha (10.121), the “golden womb”; the Orphic silver world-egg of Greece; Chinese and Polynesian parallels — all encoding the intuition that all diversity was once folded into one. Sacred number. Egypt’s Hermopolis begins with the Ogdoad, eight primeval deities in pairs; Genesis orders creation into a seven-day week (as much a theological claim — the Sabbath built into reality — as a chronology). The dividing line. The most common creative act is separation: sky from earth, waters above from below, light from dark, male from female. Marduk splits Tiamat; Genesis divides the waters with a firmament; Shu lifts Nut off Geb. Creation, symbolically, is the drawing of distinctions. The creative word. Where the world is spoken, the word and name carry power (Ptah’s tongue, God’s “let there be,” the Vedic reverence for Vāc, divine Speech) — the taproot of all sacred encoding.

Connections

Creation is the companion of the Flood (Chapter 1): both are stories of water and world-order. Its traditions are treated in full in their own chapters — the Enuma Elish in Mesopotamia (Ch. 3), Nun and Ptah in Egypt (Ch. 2), Purusha and the Nasadiya hymn in the Early Vedic world (Ch. 5), Chaos and the Theogony in Early Greece (Ch. 8), tehom in pre-exilic Israel (Ch. 7), and Ymir in Norse paganism (Ch. 23). Forward, “creation by word” feeds the Wisdom and Logos of Chapter 10, and the argument over creation from nothing sharpens against the Gnostic cosmogonies of Chapters 17 and 45.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The texts and what they say: the Enuma Elish and Marduk’s splitting of Tiamat; the Egyptian cosmogonies of Heliopolis, Memphis, and Hermopolis; Genesis 1–2; the Rigveda’s Purusha, Hiranyagarbha, and Nasadiya hymns; Hesiod’s Theogony; the Norse Ymir cosmogony; the Ugaritic Baal cycle.
  • The chaoskampf as a real, widespread pattern (Marduk/Tiamat, Baal/Yamm, Zeus/Typhon), preserved in Hebrew poetry (Rahab, Leviathan) while Genesis 1 omits the battle; and the genuine cognate of tehom with Tiamat (unpersonified in Genesis).
  • The real, old translation question at Genesis 1:1, with creatio ex nihilo as a doctrine that developed later; and the Nasadiya Sukta’s note of agnostic doubt.

What’s not supported

  • That there was a single original creation myth from which the others descend — the shared images arise repeatedly, often independently.
  • Treating creatio ex nihilo as the plain original meaning of Genesis (it is a later reading) — the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous.
  • Reading the Purusha hymn’s four classes as a neutral cosmic fact: that verse is in the latest Rigvedic layer and encodes the varna social order.

What’s genuinely open

  • Whether the cosmic-giant cosmogony (Ymir, Purusha) is a true inherited Proto-Indo-European myth (Bruce Lincoln) or a striking parallel reinforced by non-Indo-European occurrences (Pangu). Influential, not proven.
  • How much of the Norse cosmogony, recorded only in Christian-era Iceland, is genuinely pre-Christian.
  • The direction and degree of borrowing among the Near Eastern cosmogonies — shared atmosphere, common inheritance, and direct influence are hard to disentangle.
