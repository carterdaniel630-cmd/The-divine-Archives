Late Antiquity · Gnosticism

Gnosticism

In the archive

The losing side of early Christianity, whose scriptures the victors condemned and, it was thought, erased — until a jar opened in the Egyptian sand in 1945 let them speak again. Their message: the God who made this world is a blind impostor, matter is a prison, and salvation comes through gnosis, a secret knowledge of who we truly are.

A jar buried in the Egyptian sand

In December 1945, near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt, a farmer digging for fertilizer struck a sealed clay jar. Hoping for gold, he broke it — and found thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices in Coptic, buried some sixteen hundred years. What survived is one of history’s most important manuscript finds: fifty-two texts that gave back, in their own words, the voices of the ancient Gnostics. Until that jar was opened they were known almost entirely through their enemies, the Church Fathers who denounced them. Now they could speak for themselves — and what they said was extraordinary.

What “Gnosticism” was — and a caution about the word

The name comes from Greek gnosis, “knowledge” — but a caution belongs at the start, for it is a live scholarly issue: “Gnosticism” may not name a single thing. The term is a modern coinage, and the movements it groups — flourishing mainly in the 2nd–3rd centuries CE around the eastern Mediterranean — were enormously varied and did not call themselves by one name; some scholars (Williams, King) argue the category should be abandoned or redefined. Best understood, then, as a family of related movements sharing family resemblances: rejection of the material world, a distinction between the true God and the world’s maker, and salvation through revealed knowledge.

The great myth: the fall of Sophia and the false creator

At the heart of the classic systems — told most fully in the Sethian Apocryphon (Secret Book) of John — lies a breathtaking myth. Beyond everything is the true God, an unknowable Monad. From it emanate, in radiant pairs, the divine Aeons, together the fullness of the divine realm, the Pleroma. All is harmony until the youngest Aeon, Sophia (“Wisdom”), oversteps in her yearning to know the unknowable Father, producing a misbegotten Demiurge named Yaldabaoth (a lion-headed serpent). Cast out and ignorant of the beings above him, this blind creator declares: “I am God, and there is no other beside me” — the very words of the God of the Hebrew Bible. That is the whole scandal: the creator God of the Old Testament is Yaldabaoth, the arrogant impostor, and his world is a botched prison of matter.

But a fragment of true divine light becomes trapped in creation — in humanity, as a hidden divine spark, asleep and forgetful in a material body ruled by the Demiurge and his planetary Archons. Salvation is not forgiveness of sin but awakening: the spark must be roused to remember its true origin and shown the way home. The knowledge that awakens it is gnosis — and a savior descends to bring it. In Christian Gnosticism that savior is Christ, come not to die for sin but to reveal the saving knowledge and wake the sleepers.

The world turned upside down

Notice what this does to the familiar story. The Gnostics read the Jewish scriptures but inverted them. In several systems the serpent of Eden becomes a hero, bringing the liberating knowledge the jealous creator wished to withhold (the Fathers called one group Ophites, from the Greek for serpent), and the God who forbids knowledge and floods the earth is not the Most High but the tyrant Yaldabaoth. This deliberate reversal — reading the sacred story against itself — is the most startling feature of the Gnostic imagination, and why the orthodox church thought them not merely mistaken but dangerous.

The schools and their teachers

Sethian Gnosticism centered on Seth as ancestor of a spiritual “immovable race,” preserving the great myth (the Apocryphon of John). Valentinianism, founded by Valentinus (c. 100–175 CE, who nearly became bishop of Rome), taught a Pleroma of thirty Aeons and divided humanity into three natures — hylics (material), psychics (soul-people, ordinary Christians, saved by faith), and pneumatics (spiritual, saved by gnosis); Valentinians often stayed within the mainstream churches, reading the same scriptures on a secret level. Basilides of Alexandria taught 365 heavens under the Archon Abraxas, and a docetic Christology — that Christ only seemed to have a body; Irenaeus reports he taught Simon of Cyrene was crucified in Jesus’s place.

The texts, and the enemies who preserved them

Before 1945 our knowledge came mainly from the heresiologists — Irenaeus (Against Heresies, c. 180 CE), Tertullian, Hippolytus, Epiphanius — who quoted the Gnostics in order to refute them. The Nag Hammadi library gave us their works directly: the Apocryphon of John, the Gospel of Truth, the Gospel of Philip, the poetic Thunder, Perfect Mind. The separately-recovered Gospel of Judas (2006) casts Judas not as traitor but as the one disciple who understood. A special case is the Gospel of Thomas: 114 secret sayings (“the hidden words that the living Jesus spoke”), not a narrative and not straightforwardly Gnostic — many class it a sayings gospel — whose date is fiercely debated, some arguing a few sayings preserve very early tradition.

The religion of Light: Manichaeism

The most successful of Late Antiquity’s dualist faiths was not, strictly, Gnostic at all, but its great cousin: Manichaeism, founded by the Persian prophet Mani (c. 216–274 CE), who declared himself the last in a line of messengers that included Adam, Zoroaster, the Buddha, and Jesus. Where the Gnostics traced evil to a mistake within the Pleroma, Mani made the dualism absolute: two coeternal principles, Light and Darkness, locked in cosmic war. Particles of Light became trapped in matter, and the whole point of existence — and of the ascetic life — is to liberate that scattered Light and return it to its source.

The faith was organized into two ranks: the Elect, who lived in strict purity (vegetarian, celibate, non-violent even toward plants, lest they harm the Light within), and the far larger body of Hearers, lay supporters who fed and served the Elect. Carried by Mani’s missionary drive and a beautiful illustrated scripture, it spread astonishingly far: west across the Roman Empire — where the young Augustine was a Hearer for nine years before his conversion — and east along the Silk Road, becoming the state religion of the Uighur empire and reaching China, where Manichaean communities survived into the fourteenth century. For a thousand years “the religion of Light” was a genuine world faith.

The Hermetic path: as above, so below

Running alongside Gnosticism in the same Greco-Roman Egypt was a gentler esoteric current, Hermeticism — the teachings ascribed to Hermes Trismegistus (“thrice-greatest Hermes”), a legendary sage fusing the Greek Hermes with the Egyptian Thoth. Its core texts, the Corpus Hermeticum (seventeen Greek treatises of the first to third centuries CE), blend Platonism, Egyptian religion, and Greek and Jewish mysticism. Where the Gnostics damned the cosmos, the Hermeticists revered it as a living whole shot through with the divine, and taught that the human being can, by purifying the mind and coming to know God, ascend through the planetary spheres, shedding a vice at each, until the soul is deified. The opening treatise, the Poimandres, records such a revelation; and the famous maxim “that which is below is like that which is above” became the classic statement of the correspondence between macrocosm and microcosm that underlies astrology, alchemy, and the later Western esoteric tradition. (Two honesty notes: the Emerald Tablet, where that formula is usually quoted, is a later medieval Arabic work, not Late-Antique; and the Kybalion of 1908 is a modern New Thought composition, not ancient Hermetic wisdom.)

Symbology and sacred encoding

Gnosticism is, in a sense, the tradition of sacred encoding itself — its whole premise is that the truth is hidden, available only to those with the spark. Abraxas: a name that is a number. The word ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ, great Archon of Basilides’s 365 heavens, is a masterpiece of Greek isopsephy (Chapter 15): its letters sum to exactly 365 — the number of the heavens and the days of the year — and it was carved on countless magical gems.

The serpent and the Ouroboros. The serpent, villain of orthodox Eden, is in Gnostic symbolism a figure of wisdom and liberation; the Ouroboros, the snake devouring its tail, encoded cosmic wholeness and appears in the Hermetic and alchemical currents that overlapped with Gnosticism (three Hermetic treatises were bound into the Nag Hammadi codices). The numbered cosmos — the thirty Aeons, the 365 heavens, the seven planetary Archons — turns cosmology into theology-by-enumeration, where to know each sphere’s number and name helps the soul ascend past its guardians. And the Demiurge Yaldabaoth, the lion-headed serpent, is itself an encoded image of the arrogant power ruling matter.

Connections

Gnosticism is the great sibling and rival of the early Christianity of Chapter 16 — sharing its Christ and scriptures but reading them against the grain — and draws deeply on the Platonism of Chapter 15 and on Jewish scripture and apocalyptic (Chapter 10), radically reinterpreted. It runs alongside the Hermeticism of Greco-Roman Egypt and the Roman mystery cults of the next chapter. Its family extends further: the Mandaeans of Iraq and Iran — who revere John the Baptist, reject Jesus, and survive as the last living Gnostics — and the dualist world-religion of Manichaeism, founded by Mani in third-century Persia, which carried a comparable vision from Rome to China. Forward, the Gnostic impulse resurfaces in the medieval Bogomils and Cathars, and in modern occult and psychological revivals from the Theosophists to Carl Jung.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • A family of movements in the 2nd–3rd centuries CE teaching a transcendent true God distinct from an inferior Demiurge, a trapped divine spark, and salvation by gnosis — attested by the hostile heresiologists and, since 1945, by the Gnostics’ own Nag Hammadi texts.
  • The major schools and teachers — Sethian, Valentinus, Basilides — and their doctrines (the Sophia myth, thirty Aeons, three human natures, docetism, the 365 heavens and Abraxas).
  • The Nag Hammadi discovery and its texts, the recovered Gospel of Judas, the Gospel of Thomas as 114 sayings; the survival of the Mandaeans and the spread of Manichaeism.
  • Manichaeism as a major Late-Antique world religion (Mani; the absolute Light/Darkness dualism; the Elect and Hearers; the spread from Rome to China; Augustine’s nine years as a Hearer), and Hermeticism (the Corpus Hermeticum; Hermes Trismegistus; the soul’s ascent; “as above, so below”).

What’s not supported

  • The one-sided picture from the heresiologists alone — including their lurid accusations of Gnostic libertine orgies, which the Gnostics’ own (mostly ascetic) writings do not bear out and which were likely slander.
  • That “Gnosticism” was a single, unified church with one doctrine — the sources show great diversity, and the category is contested.
  • Treating the Gospel of Thomas or Judas as reliable historical accounts of Jesus’s life rather than later theological/esoteric works.
  • Treating the Emerald Tablet as a Late-Antique Hermetic text (it is first attested in medieval Arabic sources) or the Kybalion (1908) as ancient Hermetic wisdom (it is modern New Thought).

What’s genuinely open

  • Whether “Gnosticism” is a usefully coherent category at all — a serious, unresolved debate.
  • The origins of the movements: from within Christianity, from Hellenistic Judaism, from Platonism, or a pre-Christian source — and how early.
  • The date and independence of the Gospel of Thomas, and the relationships among Gnosticism, Hermeticism, and Manichaeism.
