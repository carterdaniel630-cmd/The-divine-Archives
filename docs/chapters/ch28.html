High Medieval · Scholasticism

Scholasticism

In the archive

The great medieval effort to think the faith through to the bottom — to take the newly recovered logic of Aristotle and the whole inheritance of Christian doctrine and reconcile them, question by disputed question, in the cathedral schools and the first universities of Europe — and the honest limits of what reason, then and now, can settle.

The straw

On the sixth of December, 1273, in Naples, the greatest theologian of the Middle Ages stopped writing. Thomas Aquinas was at Mass, in the middle of composing the third part of his vast Summa Theologica, when something happened to him — a vision, an experience he never described. Afterward he set down his pen and would not take it up again. When his secretary begged him to finish the enormous work, Aquinas answered: “I cannot. Everything I have written seems to me like straw compared to what I have seen.” He died three months later, the Summa unfinished. His life’s project — to show that faith and reason, revelation and Aristotle, could be woven into one seamless understanding of God and the world — ended in a silence that reason could not fill.

That moment holds the whole of scholasticism in miniature: the boundless confidence that the human mind, rightly ordered, could climb toward God by argument — and the honest knowledge, at the summit, that argument is not the same as sight.

What scholasticism is

Scholasticism (from Latin scholasticus, “belonging to the school”) was not a doctrine but a method — the way of learning practiced by the “schoolmen” of the medieval universities from roughly 1100 to 1500. Its aim was to reconcile faith and reason: to bring the philosophy of the ancient pagans, above all Aristotle, into harmony with Christian theology through rigorous dialectic — the disciplined posing of a question, the marshalling of arguments for and against, and the reasoned resolution of the contradiction. At bottom it was the conviction that the truths of revelation and the truths of reason, both from God, could not finally conflict.

The recovery of Aristotle

Scholasticism was lit by a spark from outside Christendom. For centuries the Latin West had known only a fraction of Aristotle. Then, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, a wave of translations — many made in Toledo from Arabic versions preserved and expounded by Muslim philosophers — poured the whole Aristotelian corpus into Europe. It arrived wrapped in the great commentaries of Avicenna (Ibn Sina) and Averroes (Ibn Rushd), so that Christian thinkers met the Philosopher through Muslim eyes. At the same moment the universities were being born — Paris, Oxford, Bologna — giving the new learning a home and a method.

The method and the early masters

The scholastic classroom ran on the lectio (close reading), the quaestio (the question it raised), and above all the disputatio — the formal disputed question, arguing every side before determining the answer. Peter Abelard (1079–1142) set the tone with Sic et Non (“Yes and No”), juxtaposing questions on which the Fathers contradicted one another; Peter Lombard’s Four Books of Sentences (c. 1150) became the textbook every theologian was required to expound. Before the flood of Aristotle, Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109) had already given the movement its motto — fides quaerens intellectum, “faith seeking understanding” — and its most famous puzzle, the ontological argument for God’s existence. Abelard pressed the problem of universals — whether categories like “humanity” are real things or only names — that would divide the schoolmen for three centuries.

The synthesis: Thomas Aquinas

The summit is Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), pupil of Albert the Great, who undertook the full baptism of Aristotle into Christian thought. His Summa Theologica, built as thousands of disputed questions, sought to treat every point of doctrine in order. Near its beginning stand the Five Ways (quinque viae) — five arguments for God’s existence, from motion, causation, contingency, the degrees of perfection, and the design of nature, each concluding to a first mover or cause “which everyone calls God.” Reason unaided, for Aquinas, could climb this far; grace perfected nature rather than contradicting it.

It must be said plainly, in the spirit of this book: the Five Ways and the ontological argument are philosophical arguments, contested continuously from their own day to ours — not settled proofs that history can pronounce valid. They are among the most influential arguments ever made, and they remain arguments.

The later turn: Scotus, Ockham, and 1277

Aquinas’s synthesis did not go unopposed. In 1277 the Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemned 219 propositions drawn from the bolder Aristotelians, insisting on God’s absolute freedom — a shock some historians (following Pierre Duhem) have credited with loosening Aristotle’s grip and opening the road toward modern science. In its wake two Franciscans reshaped the field. John Duns Scotus (1266–1308), the “Subtle Doctor,” refined the universals problem with haecceity — the “thisness” that makes each individual unique. Then William of Ockham (1287–1347), a thoroughgoing nominalist, held that only individuals exist and universals are mere concepts, and wielded the parsimony principle ever after called Ockham’s razor. With Ockham the confident marriage of faith and reason began, quietly, to come apart.

Three faiths, one project

Scholasticism was not only Christian. The same recovered Aristotle produced parallel masterworks in the other Abrahamic faiths — above all Maimonides (1138–1204), the Jewish philosopher of Córdoba and Cairo, whose Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190) reconciled Aristotelian philosophy with the Torah, in direct dialogue with the Muslim philosophers Avicenna and Averroes. The three traditions read the same Greek, argued the same problems, and cited one another across the boundaries of faith — one of the great shared intellectual projects in history.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Scholasticism’s genius was for order made visible, and its symbolic systems are systems of structure.

  • Medieval reading rested on the four senses of Scripture: the literal, the allegorical (what to believe), the moral (how to act), and the anagogical (our final destiny) — fixed by a couplet: “The letter teaches deeds; allegory, what you believe; the moral, what to do; anagogy, where you are bound.” It is the Christian cousin of the Jewish PaRDeS of the neighboring chapter.
  • The Gothic cathedral, rising in the same cities and decades as the great summae, has been read (famously by Erwin Panofsky) as a “summa in stone” — built by the same habits of manifestatio (the clear articulation of every part) and concordantia (the reconciliation of opposing forces into a stable whole). Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis made its soaring light a theology in itself.
  • Medieval thought loved the diagram of order — the Porphyrian tree branching genus into species, the ranked angelic hierarchies of pseudo-Dionysius, and the great chain of being. To picture the cosmos was to picture a perfectly ordered argument.

Connections

Scholasticism grew from the patristic Christianity of the earlier era and from the recovered Aristotle carried into Europe by the philosophers of Islam. It ran in exact parallel with the Kabbalah and Sufism of its own age — each straining to order the whole of reality, though scholasticism reached for that order through logic where the mystics reached through love. Its later, nominalist turn helped clear the ground for the Reformation and the scientific revolution of the next era. Its central question — how far reason can reach God — has never gone away.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The scholastic movement, its method, and its institutions (Paris, Oxford, Bologna); the recovery of Aristotle through the Toledo translations and the Muslim commentators.
  • The historical figures and texts — Anselm, Abelard’s Sic et Non, Lombard’s Sentences, Aquinas’s Summa, Scotus, Ockham, and Maimonides’s Guide; and the Condemnations of 1277.

What’s not supported (as history)

  • The truth of the theological conclusions — that the Five Ways or the ontological argument succeed in proving God — is a matter of philosophy and faith, not a historical finding; the arguments remain genuinely contested.

A myth to correct, a claim to limit

  • The popular “Dark Ages” picture of an anti-rational, anti-scientific void is false: scholasticism was intensely rigorous and helped lay foundations the scientific revolution built on.
  • But the opposite overclaim — that scholasticism or the Condemnations of 1277 invented modern science — goes too far; the Duhem thesis is debated, and the truth is long, indirect groundwork, not a single birth.
