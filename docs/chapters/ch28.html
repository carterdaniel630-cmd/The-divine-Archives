High Medieval · Scholasticism

Scholasticism

In the archive

The great medieval effort to think the faith through to the bottom — to take the newly recovered logic of Aristotle, carried into Europe through the philosophers of Islam, and the whole inheritance of Christian doctrine, and reconcile them, question by disputed question, in the cathedral schools and the first universities — and the honest limits of what reason, then and now, can actually settle.

The straw

On the sixth of December, 1273, in Naples, the greatest theologian of the Middle Ages stopped writing. Thomas Aquinas was at Mass, in the middle of composing the third part of his vast Summa Theologica, when something happened to him — a vision, an experience he never described. Afterward he set down his pen and would not take it up again. When his secretary begged him to finish the enormous work, Aquinas answered: "I cannot. Everything I have written seems to me like straw compared to what I have seen." He died three months later, the Summa unfinished. His life's project — to show that faith and reason, revelation and Aristotle, could be woven into one seamless understanding of God and the world — ended in a silence that reason could not fill.

That moment holds the whole of scholasticism in miniature: the boundless confidence that the human mind, rightly ordered, could climb toward God by argument — and the honest knowledge, at the summit, that argument is not the same as sight.

What scholasticism is

Scholasticism (from Latin scholasticus, "belonging to the school") was not a doctrine but a method — the way of learning practiced by the "schoolmen" of the medieval universities from roughly 1100 to 1500. Its aim was to reconcile faith and reason: to bring the philosophy of the ancient pagans, above all Aristotle, into harmony with Christian theology, through rigorous dialectic — the disciplined posing of a question, the marshalling of arguments for and against, and the reasoned resolution of the contradiction. It was, at bottom, the conviction that the truths of revelation and the truths of reason, both coming from God, could not finally conflict.

The recovery of Aristotle — through Islam

Scholasticism was lit by a spark from outside Christendom. For centuries the Latin West had known only a fraction of Aristotle — a few logical works. Then, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, a wave of translations, many made in Toledo from Arabic versions, poured the whole Aristotelian corpus into Europe: physics, metaphysics, ethics, the works on the soul. And it arrived not raw but already thought through by the great Muslim philosophers (falasifa), whose commentaries the Christians studied as intently as Aristotle himself. Al-Farabi (d. 950) had built a Neoplatonized Aristotelian cosmos; Avicenna (Ibn Sina, d. 1037) had framed the distinction between essence and existence and argued for God as the Necessary Being — the one being whose essence entails its existence, on which all contingent things depend — an argument that runs straight into Aquinas. Averroes (Ibn Rushd, d. 1198) of Córdoba, the supreme commentator, defended pure Aristotelianism so rigorously that the Latins simply called him "the Commentator."

Islamic philosophy also staged, a century before the Latin schoolmen, the very battle scholasticism would fight. The mystic-theologian al-Ghazali (Chapter 27) wrote The Incoherence of the Philosophers, charging that the Aristotelians' claims — the eternity of the world, God's knowledge only of universals and not of particulars, the denial of bodily resurrection — were incoherent and, on three points, heretical. Averroes answered with The Incoherence of the Incoherence, defending philosophy point by point. That debate — how far reason may go, and what happens where it contradicts revelation — is the deep background of the entire Latin enterprise, which was born as the universities (Paris, Oxford, Bologna) came into being to house the new learning.

The method

The scholastic classroom ran on three exercises: the lectio (the close reading of an authoritative text), the quaestio (a question raised by the text), and above all the disputatio — the formal disputed question, in which a master stated a problem on which the authorities appeared to disagree, laid out the arguments on each side, and then determined the answer. The tone was set by Peter Abelard (1079–1142), whose Sic et Non ("Yes and No") juxtaposed 158 questions on which the Church Fathers contradicted one another, forcing the reader to reason toward reconciliation. And the standard textbook became Peter Lombard's Four Books of Sentences (c. 1150), on which every aspiring theologian, Aquinas included, was required to comment.

The early masters and the war over universals

Before the flood of Aristotle, Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109) had already given scholasticism its motto — fides quaerens intellectum, "faith seeking understanding" — and its most famous puzzle, the ontological argument: that God, defined as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived," must exist, since a God existing only in the mind would be less than one existing in reality.

The age's other great quarrel was the problem of universals: when we speak of "humanity" or "redness," is there a real universal thing that all humans or red objects share, or is "humanity" only a name? The realists (following Plato and, in part, Aristotle) held universals to be real; the nominalists (Roscelin) held them mere names, flatus vocis, puffs of breath; Abelard steered a middle "conceptualist" course. It sounds abstract, but everything hung on it — the reality of the Church as more than its members, the meaning of "human nature" that Christ assumed, even the doctrine of the Trinity — and the question divided the schoolmen for three centuries.

The synthesis: Thomas Aquinas

The summit is Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), a Dominican, pupil of Albert the Great, who undertook the boldest reconciliation of all: the full baptism of Aristotle into Christian thought. His Summa Theologica, structured as thousands of disputed questions, sought to address every conceivable point of doctrine in order. Near its beginning stand the Five Ways (quinque viae) — five arguments for the existence of God, from motion, from causation, from contingency, from the degrees of perfection, and from the design and order of nature, each concluding to a first mover, cause, necessary being, or intelligent orderer "which everyone calls God." For Aquinas, reason unaided could climb this far; revelation was needed to go further, but grace perfected nature rather than contradicting it.

It must be said plainly, in the spirit of this book: the Five Ways and the ontological argument are philosophical arguments, debated and contested continuously from their own day to ours — not settled proofs that history can pronounce valid. They are among the most influential arguments ever made, and they remain arguments.

Bonaventure and the Franciscan alternative

Aquinas's confident marriage of Aristotle and faith was never the only path, and its greatest contemporary critic was his friend Bonaventure (1221–1274), the Franciscan "Seraphic Doctor." Formed in the older Augustinian tradition, Bonaventure distrusted the pagan Aristotle and privileged faith, love, and mystical ascent over autonomous reason: philosophy, cut loose from the light of faith, he warned, leads to error, and the mind's true journey (his Itinerarium, "The Soul's Journey into God") climbs through creation to a union that argument cannot reach. The Franciscan school he led kept alive, against the Dominican Aristotelians, the conviction that the heart's ascent matters more than the intellect's — a tension that ran through the universities for centuries.

The later turn: Latin Averroism, 1277, Scotus, and Ockham

The bolder Aristotelians pressed on, and provoked a crisis. In the arts faculty at Paris, Siger of Brabant and the so-called Latin Averroists taught Aristotelian conclusions — the eternity of the world, a single shared intellect for all humanity — that flatly contradicted Christian doctrine, holding them true "according to philosophy" while professing the faith true according to revelation. (Their enemies caricatured this as a doctrine of "double truth"; scholars now agree none of them actually held so crude a position.) The reaction came in 1277, when the Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemned 219 propositions, insisting on God's absolute freedom against any claim that even God was bound by Aristotle's necessities — a shock some historians (following Pierre Duhem) credit with loosening Aristotle's grip and, indirectly, opening the road to modern science.

In its wake two Franciscans reshaped the field. John Duns Scotus (1266–1308), the "Subtle Doctor," refined the universals problem with his notion of haecceity — the "thisness" that makes each individual unique — and argued for the univocity of being. Then William of Ockham (1287–1347) swept much of the edifice away: a thoroughgoing nominalist, he held that only individuals exist and universals are mere concepts, and he wielded the principle of parsimony ever after called Ockham's razor — do not multiply entities beyond necessity. With Ockham, and the widening gap he opened between faith and reason (which, he held, could not prove the articles of belief), the confident scholastic synthesis began quietly to come apart — and the humanists of the coming Renaissance would mock the schoolmen's hair-splitting "how many angels on a pinhead" disputes as decadent word-games.

Three faiths, one project

Scholasticism was never only Christian. The same recovered Aristotle and the same drive to reconcile revelation with reason produced parallel masterworks across the Abrahamic world: the Muslim falasifa already met; and, greatest of the Jewish philosophers, Maimonides (Moses ben Maimon, 1138–1204) of Córdoba and Cairo, whose Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190) set out to reconcile Aristotelian philosophy with the Torah for those torn between them, in direct dialogue with Avicenna and Averroes. The three traditions read the same Greek, argued the same problems — the eternity of the world, the nature of God's knowledge, the reach of proof — and cited one another across the boundaries of faith. It is one of the great shared intellectual projects in history.

The believer's lens

To the schoolman, this labor was itself an act of worship. Reason was not the enemy of faith but its servant and its glory: God had made the human mind in His image and the ordered cosmos as an intelligible book, so to think clearly about God — to trace the Five Ways up to the First Cause, to resolve the contradictions of the Fathers, to map the ranks of the angels — was to honor the Creator with the highest faculty He gave. Fides quaerens intellectum: the believer already holds the truth by faith and now seeks to understand what he holds, climbing by argument toward the God he loves. Aquinas's serene confidence that grace perfects nature, that Aristotle's pagan genius could be taken up into Christian truth, expresses a deep optimism about the unity of all knowledge under one God. And Bonaventure's warning — that the intellect's ascent is empty without the heart's — is the same faith from the other side: the whole enterprise is prayer conducted with the tools of logic, and Aquinas's final "straw" is its most honest confession, that the God reached by argument is infinitely surpassed by the God met in vision.

The skeptical lens

The historian records what scholasticism did — and declines to certify what it claimed to prove. The Five Ways and the ontological argument are contested philosophy, not demonstrated fact; brilliant minds have found them compelling and other brilliant minds have found them fallacious, continuously, for eight centuries, and history cannot pronounce a winner. Two corrections of popular myth, in opposite directions, are due. The picture of the medieval "Dark Ages" as an anti-rational, superstition-drowned void is simply false: scholasticism was intensely rigorous, prized logic and evidence, ran real proto-scientific work (Grosseteste, Roger Bacon, the Oxford Calculators), and built institutions — the university, the disputation, the habit of arguing every side — on which modern thought still stands. But the opposite overclaim, that scholasticism or the Condemnations of 1277 invented modern science, goes too far: the Duhem thesis is debated, and the real story is long, indirect groundwork, not a single birth. What is not in doubt is the movement's scale and seriousness, or that its central question — how far reason can reach toward God — remains genuinely open.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Scholasticism's genius was for order made visible, and its symbolic systems are systems of structure.

  • Medieval reading rested on the four senses of Scripture: the literal (or historical), the allegorical (what to believe), the moral (how to act), and the anagogical (our final destiny). A medieval couplet fixed them in memory: "The letter teaches deeds; allegory, what you believe; the moral, what to do; anagogy, where you are bound." It is the Christian cousin of the Jewish PaRDeS (Chapter 26) — the same conviction that a sacred text speaks on four levels at once.
  • Ramon Llull (1232–1316), the Majorcan mystic, built the age's most literal encoding of truth: his Ars combinatoria, a set of concentric rotating paper wheels lettered with the divine attributes and the categories of being, which the operator turned to generate combinations of concepts — a "thinking machine" meant to demonstrate the truths of the faith and convert Muslims and Jews by pure logic. It is often reckoned a distant ancestor of symbolic logic and the computer.
  • The Gothic cathedral, rising in the same cities and decades as the great summae, has been read (famously by Erwin Panofsky) as a "summa in stone" — built by the same mental habits: manifestatio, the clear articulation of every part so the whole logic is visible, and concordantia, the reconciliation of opposing forces into a stable whole. Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis made its soaring light a theology in itself: material radiance drawing the mind up toward the divine light beyond.
  • And medieval thought loved the diagram of order — the Porphyrian tree branching genus into species, the ranked hierarchies of angels of pseudo-Dionysius, and the "great chain of being," in which every creature held its ordained rung from God down to stone. To picture the cosmos was to picture a perfectly ordered argument.

Connections

Scholasticism grew from the patristic Christianity of the earlier era (Chapter 22) — its Augustine, its creeds — and from the recovered Aristotle carried into Europe by the philosophers of Islam (Chapter 21). It ran in exact parallel with the Kabbalah (26) and Sufism (27) of its own age — each straining to order the whole of reality, though scholasticism reached for that order through logic where the mystics reached through love. Its later, nominalist turn helped clear the ground for the Reformation (Luther was trained in Ockham's tradition — Chapter 31) and for the scientific revolution to come. And its central question — whether, and how far, reason can reach God — has never gone away.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The scholastic movement, its method, and its institutions (the universities of Paris, Oxford, Bologna); the recovery of Aristotle through the Toledo translations and the Muslim commentators (al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes, and the Ghazali–Averroes "Incoherence" debate); the historical figures and texts — Anselm, Abelard's Sic et Non, Lombard's Sentences, Aquinas's Summa, Bonaventure, Siger of Brabant, Scotus, Ockham, Llull, and Maimonides's Guide; and the Condemnations of 1277. These rest on an enormous surviving literature.

Not supported (as history)

The truth of the theological conclusions — that the Five Ways or the ontological argument succeed in proving God — is a matter of philosophy and faith, not a historical finding; the arguments remain genuinely contested. This chapter records that they were made and how, not that they are valid. The "double truth" of the Latin Averroists is itself partly a caricature by their opponents.

A myth worth correcting, and a claim worth limiting

The popular "Dark Ages" picture of an anti-rational medieval void is false; scholasticism was rigorous and laid real groundwork for later science. But the opposite overclaim — that it, or 1277, invented modern science — also overreaches; the Duhem thesis is debated, and the truth is long, indirect groundwork, not a single birth.
