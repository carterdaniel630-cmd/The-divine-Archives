Early Medieval · Shinto

Shinto

In the archive

Japan’s religion of the kami — a faith with no founder and, for most of its history, no separate name, so entangled with Buddhism that scholars still argue whether “Shinto” as an independent religion existed at all before modern times — reconstructed from an eighth-century imperial chronicle, a shrine torn down and rebuilt every twenty years, and the hard task of separating ancient kami-worship from its later reinventions.

The shrine that is always new

At Ise, in central Japan, stands the holiest shrine of the kami — a simple structure of unpainted cypress, raised on posts, roofed in thatch, in a style so plain it looks older than any temple. Beside it lies an empty plot of raked gravel the same size and shape. Every twenty years the shrine is torn down and built again, exact in every joint, on that neighboring ground, the deity moved to the new hall and the old one dismantled — a ritual called the shikinen sengū, carried out for roughly 1,300 years, most recently begun in 2025. The point is not decay but renewal: the building is mortal so that the form, the craft, and the presence within it can be endlessly, perfectly reborn.

That paradox — a tradition that keeps itself ancient by continually remaking itself — runs through the whole of Shinto, and through the problem of writing about it. For “Shinto” is a faith with no founder, no single scripture, and, for most of its history, no separate name. What we can study is real: shrines, festivals, an eighth-century mythology, a thousand years of ritual. What is genuinely disputed is whether any of it was a distinct “religion” called Shinto before quite recent times.

What “Shinto” is — and the problem of the name

The word Shinto (“the way of the kami,” from Chinese shen-dao) names the worship of the kami — the gods, spirits, and sacred powers of Japan: the sun, storms, mountains, rice, ancestors, and the eight million kami (yaoyorozu no kami), a number meaning simply “countless.” Kami are not omnipotent creators but presences to be honored, feared, and kept in good relation through offering and purity.

But the influential thesis of the historian Kuroda Toshio (1926–1993) reshaped the field: he argued that Shinto as an independent religion took shape only in modern times, and that for most of Japanese history kami-worship existed within a Buddhist framework — so fused with Buddhism as to be, in effect, one of its branches. Not everyone accepts the full claim; recent scholars (Breen, Teeuwen) have qualified it, arguing for real continuities in kami-worship. But the core caution is now standard: for the early-medieval period this chapter covers, “Shinto” and “Buddhism” were not two religions a Japanese person chose between; they were a single, blended religious world. That fusion is the heart of this chapter.

The textual record

Shinto’s mythology survives because an eighth-century imperial court wrote it down — for its own reasons. The Kojiki (“Record of Ancient Matters,” 712), compiled by Ō no Yasumaro from oral traditions, and the Nihon Shoki (“Chronicles of Japan,” 720), a committee work in the Chinese annalistic style, both open with the age of the gods and run down to the historical emperors. They are not neutral scripture: they were composed to legitimize the imperial line by tracing it to the gods, and the Nihon Shoki was partly written to correct the Kojiki. To these are added the Fudoki gazetteers, the Man’yōshū poems, the ritual norito prayers of the Engishiki (927), and the Kogo Shūi. The mythology is old — passed down orally before it was ever fixed — but the form we have is an eighth-century political document, and the two cannot be fully separated.

Cosmology and the kami

In the beginning, the Kojiki says, the primordial pair Izanagi and Izanami stood on the floating bridge of heaven, stirred the sea with a jeweled spear, and made the islands of Japan and a host of kami. Bearing the fire-kami, Izanami was burned to death and went down to the land of the dead; Izanagi pursued her, fled her corruption, and, washing himself clean in a river — the first misogi, or purification — brought forth from the very act of cleansing the three great kami: Amaterasu, the sun, from his left eye; Tsukuyomi, the moon, from his right; and Susanoo, the storm, from his nose.

When Susanoo’s violence drove Amaterasu to hide in a cave and plunge the world into darkness, the other kami lured her out with a mirror and revelry — restoring the sun. Her grandson Ninigi was sent down to rule, carrying the sacred regalia; his descendant Jimmu became, in the tradition, the first emperor. The claim that the imperial house descends from the sun-goddess is the theological center of the whole mythology — and it is a claim of faith, not a finding of history.

Purity, pollution, and worship

If one idea organizes Shinto practice, it is purity. The tradition is far less concerned with sin than with kegare — pollution, the defilement of death, blood, disease, and disorder — and with restoring purity through ritual cleansing. Worship centers on the jinja (shrine), entered through a torii gate that marks the passage from ordinary to sacred ground; visitors rinse hands and mouth at the temizuya basin; priests (kannushi) and shrine maidens (miko) perform harae (purification), recite norito prayers, present offerings, and hold matsuri — the festivals that carry the kami through the community. The most rigorous purification, misogi, immerses the body in cold river or waterfall, re-enacting Izanagi’s cleansing. The concern is not moral guilt but ritual cleanness.

The angry dead: goryō and the god Tenjin

Death was the deepest pollution, and the dead could be dangerous — none more so than those who died with a grievance. In the Heian period a widespread fear arose of the goryō, the vengeful spirits (onryō) of people who had suffered unjust deaths and now struck back at the living with plagues, storms, and disaster. The remedy was as striking as the fear: rather than exorcise such a spirit, one placated and deified it, turning a feared ghost into a protective god. The classic case is Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), a brilliant scholar and minister brought down by rivals’ slander and exiled to die far from the capital. After his death, a run of calamities in Kyoto — the deaths of his enemies, epidemics, and above all lightning striking the imperial palace — was blamed on his furious spirit. The terrified court exonerated him, restored his rank, and enshrined him as a god: Tenjin, worshipped at the great Kitano and Dazaifu Tenmangū shrines, where over time the vengeful ghost mellowed into a benevolent patron of scholarship, prayed to by students to this day. It is one of the most revealing patterns in Japanese religion — the sacred generated not only from awe but from fear appeased, the angry dead transformed into a kami by worship.

The fusion with Buddhism

The defining fact of Shinto in this era is its union with Buddhism — shinbutsu-shūgō, “the amalgamation of kami and buddhas.” When Buddhism arrived in the sixth century it did not displace the kami; the two grew together. From the early eighth century, jingū-ji — Buddhist temples built at Shinto shrines — appeared, so that sutras were chanted to the kami and kami were understood as protectors of the Dharma.

In the Heian period (794–1185) this hardened into a theology: honji suijaku, “original ground and manifest trace.” The kami, it held, were local manifestations (suijaku, “traces”) of universal buddhas and bodhisattvas (honji, “original ground”) — Amaterasu identified with the cosmic Buddha Dainichi. The Shingon school’s Ryōbu Shintō and the Tendai school’s Sannō Shintō wove kami and buddhas into single systems. For roughly a thousand years, until the forced separation of 1868, this blended religion — not a “pure” Shinto — was simply the religion of Japan.

The mountain way: Shugendō

The fusion of kami and buddhas produced, in the mountains, a distinct tradition of its own. Shugendō — “the way of trial and power” — grew from Japan’s ancient reverence for mountains as sacred realms where the dead and the gods dwell, and fused it with esoteric Buddhism (Shingon and Tendai), Daoism, and shamanism into a path of ascetic power. Its legendary founder is En no Gyōja (“En the Ascetic,” 7th century), a mountain wizard of many miracles. Its practitioners, the yamabushi (“those who lie down in the mountains”), sought spiritual power through severe austerities in the peaks — fasting, standing under freezing waterfalls, chanting sutras and mantras, and traversing sacred ranges like Yoshino and Ōmine as a ritual re-enactment of death and rebirth. Part priest, part shaman, part wonder-worker, the yamabushi served villages as healers, exorcists, and diviners, and became one of the most characteristic religious figures of medieval Japan — the living embodiment of the kami-and-buddha fusion, worked out not in a temple’s texts but on the body, in the mountains.

Shrines, treasures, and the ground

The material record is grand and specific. Ise Jingū, sacred to Amaterasu, in its austere shinmei-zukuri style of raw cypress, is rebuilt every twenty years. Izumo Taisha, one of the oldest and largest shrines, sacred to Ōkuninushi, is renewed on a longer cycle; its region held an alternative mythic tradition the imperial Kojiki had to absorb and subordinate — a hint that the “national” myth papered over older regional powers.

At the symbolic center stand the Three Sacred Treasures, the imperial regalia: the mirror Yata no Kagami (said to be kept at Ise), the sword Kusanagi no Tsurugi (at Atsuta Shrine), and the jewel Yasakani no Magatama. Crucially for an honest account: they are never shown — not to the public, not to scholars, arguably not even to the emperor. Their existence, age, and nature cannot be independently verified; what history can attest is the tradition of them and their role in enthronement, not the objects themselves.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Shinto encodes the sacred less in doctrine than in thresholds, objects, and words.

  • The torii gate encodes the boundary itself — the line between profane and sacred; to pass through it is already a small purification.
  • The shimenawa, a thick twisted rope of rice straw hung with shide (white zigzag paper streamers), marks a space, tree, rock, or object as the dwelling or presence of a kami — a visible fence around the holy.
  • The mirror is the supreme sacred object: at Ise a mirror is the shintai, the “god-body,” the vessel the kami inhabits — not an image of Amaterasu but the very thing her presence resides in. The three regalia are read as encoded virtues: the mirror for wisdom, the sword for valor, the jewel for benevolence.
  • Kotodama, “word-spirit,” is the belief that spoken sounds carry real spiritual power — that the syllables of a norito, correctly uttered, act upon the world. It makes ritual language a sacred technology, the Japanese cousin of the mantra.
  • The architecture encodes theology: Ise’s plainness, its perishable cypress, and its perpetual renewal are a statement — that purity and presence, not permanence of stone, are what the sacred requires.

A necessary caution, as the tradition’s own honesty demands: much of what is now presented as timeless “Shinto symbolism” was codified far later, and the fiercely nationalist State Shinto of 1868–1945 — the emperor as a living god, the shrines as arms of the state — was a modern political construction, not an ancient faith, and must not be read back onto the kami-worship of this era.

Connections

Shinto is the Japanese member of this era’s great pattern: an indigenous, nature-centered polytheism meeting a universal world religion — precisely the encounter the neighboring Norse chapter describes for northern Europe, though here the two fused rather than one replacing the other. Its near-inseparable other half is the Buddhism of the same centuries, especially the esoteric Shingon and Tendai schools that are the Japanese branches of the Tantra of the previous chapter. It absorbed Chinese Confucian and Daoist cosmology through the same channel that brought its writing system. Its later reinventions — the 1868 separation and State Shinto — belong to a much later era and are noted here only to be kept out of this one.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The dating of the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) and their role as imperial-legitimation texts; the antiquity and continuity of kami-worship and shrine ritual.
  • The reality of shinbutsu-shūgō and the honji suijaku theology as the dominant religious form of the era.
  • The shrines themselves — Ise and its twenty-year renewal, Izumo — the tradition of the three regalia, and the centrality of purity/pollution and purification ritual.
  • The goryō cult of pacifying vengeful spirits by deification (Sugawara no Michizane enshrined as Tenjin), and Shugendō, the syncretic mountain asceticism of the yamabushi.

What’s not supported (as history)

  • The divine descent of the imperial line from Amaterasu — a claim of faith, not history.
  • A pure, original Shinto cleanly separable from Buddhism in the medieval period — the evidence points the other way.
  • The projection of modern State Shinto (1868–1945) back onto ancient kami-worship; and the authenticity of the three regalia as physical objects — never shown, never verified.

What’s genuinely open

  • How much of the Kojiki mythology is genuinely old oral tradition and how much is eighth-century political shaping.
  • Whether “Shinto” was a distinct religion before modern times at all — the Kuroda thesis is influential but debated.
  • The real independent power and religion of Izumo before the imperial myth absorbed it, and the dating of many shrine origins.
