Late Antiquity · Early Christianity

Early Christianity

In the archive

A small, persecuted, astonishingly diverse movement within the Jewish world of the Roman East — which within three centuries captured the very empire that crucified its founder. Told with the tools of history, and with a clear line kept between what the tradition holds sacred and what the evidence can show.

A fish scratched on a wall

In the dim tunnels of the Roman catacombs, a simple sign recurs — a fish, in a few curved strokes. To a Roman passer-by it meant nothing; to those who knew, it was a compressed creed. The Greek for fish, ΙΧΘΥΣ (ichthys), was read as an acrostic: Ιησοῦς Χριστός Θεοῦ Υἱός Σωτήρ — Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior. In an age when to be a Christian could mean death, a whole confession was hidden in the outline of a fish. This is the story of how a scattering of house-assemblies hiding their faith in symbols became, in three centuries, the official religion of Rome.

A note on faith and history

This chapter reconstructs the early movement as historians reconstruct any ancient movement — from texts, dates, and remains. That method has limits, stated honestly. History can weigh whether a man named Jesus lived, was crucified, and was proclaimed risen by his followers; it cannot, by its nature, confirm or deny that God raised him or that he was the Son of God. Those are claims of faith, held by billions, and this work neither asserts them as proven history nor dismisses them — it marks them clearly as what they are. What a tradition holds sacred and what the material record establishes are two different statements, kept distinct here while respecting both.

The historical Jesus: what can be known

On the bare facts there is a scholarly consensus of unusual breadth, across believers and skeptics alike. A Jewish man named Jesus of Nazareth lived in Roman Galilee in the early first century, was baptized by John the Baptist, taught as an itinerant proclaiming the coming Kingdom of God, and was crucified in Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate (c. 30–33 CE). This is attested by multiple independent early sources and, briefly, by non-Christians — Josephus (c. 93 CE) and Tacitus (c. 116 CE). Beyond that outline, certainty fades: the earliest sources are the letters of Paul (c. 50s CE), which say little of Jesus’s life; the Gospels — Mark (c. 70), Matthew and Luke (c. 80–90, drawing on Mark and a hypothesized sayings-source, Q), and John (c. 90–100) — were written decades later, in Greek, as theological proclamations, not neutral biographies. What Jesus “really said and did” beneath this layered testimony is the subject of two centuries of the unresolved “quest for the historical Jesus.”

The resurrection and the birth of the church

At the center stands a claim history cannot adjudicate. Jesus’s followers, devastated by his execution, came within a very short time to proclaim that God had raised him from the dead — the engine of everything that follows. Paul preserves what is likely the earliest Christian creed (1 Corinthians 15), a formula dating to within a few years of the crucifixion. Historians can establish that the first Christians sincerely proclaimed the resurrection, and that this belief turned a broken band of Galileans into a missionary movement of extraordinary energy. Whether the resurrection happened lies beyond the historian’s reach and within the domain of faith. This chapter records the belief and its world-changing consequences, and leaves the verdict where it belongs.

Paul and the parting from Judaism

Christianity began entirely within Judaism (Chapter 10) — Jesus and all his first followers were Jews, and the Jerusalem community under James and Peter kept the Torah. The figure who turned a Jewish messianic sect into a universal religion was Paul of Tarsus, a Pharisee and former persecutor transformed by a vision of the risen Christ. His radical conviction — that through Christ Gentiles could be included without becoming Jews, without circumcision or the full Law — opened the movement to the whole Roman world. Hardened by the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE and by mutual rejection, Christianity and Judaism gradually became two separate religions — a painful “parting of the ways” with a long and bitter legacy.

The many Christianities

There was no single, unified early Christianity. The first two centuries saw a startling diversity of movements competing to define the faith: Jewish Christians (the Ebionites) who kept the Law; Marcion (c. 140 CE), who rejected the Jewish scriptures and their God and, by publishing his own pared-down scripture, forced the wider church to define its canon; the various Gnostic Christians (the next chapter); and the “proto-orthodox” stream that would prevail. The winners wrote the history — and only with modern discoveries like the Nag Hammadi library (Chapter 17) has the sheer variety of the earliest movement come back into view.

The scriptures and the making of a canon

The New Testament — 27 Greek documents — took shape over roughly three centuries around the letters of Paul (about seven near-universally accepted as his, others disputed, the “Pastorals” widely regarded as later works in his name), the four Gospels, Acts, other epistles, and Revelation. Alongside circulated a vast further literature — other gospels, acts, and apocalypses (the apocrypha and Gnostic texts) — that the church would exclude. The fourfold Gospel and Pauline collection formed early, but the full 27-book list was not fixed until the later fourth century (Athanasius’s letter of 367 CE first names exactly our canon).

Ritual, ethics, and the life of the community

Early worship was intimate and domestic — believers gathered in private houses (the earliest identified Christian building, at Dura-Europos, is a converted house of c. 240 CE) to pray, sing, read scripture, and share two central rites: baptism, the water-initiation joining the believer to Christ’s death and resurrection, and the Eucharist, the shared bread and wine of the Last Supper. Their ethics — the Sermon on the Mount, the command to love God and neighbor and even enemies, and a special care for the poor, sick, and outcast — were radical in the Roman world, and their practical charity is widely credited by historians as a real engine of the movement’s growth.

Persecution, martyrdom, and the turn of Constantine

For three centuries the church was an illegal or semi-legal sect, its refusal to worship the Roman gods or the imperial cult (Chapter 13) marking Christians as subversive “atheists.” Persecution was mostly local, punctuated by empire-wide waves under Nero (who scapegoated Christians for the fire of 64 CE), Decius, and Diocletian (the “Great Persecution,” 303 CE); the martyrs became the heroes of the faith. Then the tide turned: Constantine, crediting a vision of the cross with his victory at the Milvian Bridge (312 CE), legalized Christianity with the Edict of Milan (313), convened the Council of Nicaea (325) to settle disputes over the nature of Christ, and by 380 CE under Theodosius it was the official religion of the empire. The persecuted had become the establishment.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The fish and the hidden creed. The ΙΧΘΥΣ fish was the great encoded emblem of the persecuted church — at once a picture, an acrostic creed (Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior), and a secret sign, drawing on the Gospel image of “fishers of men.”

The monograms of the Name. Encoding clustered around the name of Christ: the Chi-Rho (☧) superimposes the first two Greek letters of Christos (the sign on Constantine’s shields); the older staurogram or Tau-Rho, in the earliest Gospel manuscripts, was used inside the word “cross” as a tiny pictogram of the crucified Jesus — the earliest visual reference to the crucifixion, two centuries before the scene was openly shown; and Alpha and Omega encoded Christ as “the beginning and the end.”

The reticence of the cross. Strikingly, the cross was rare in the first centuries — to a Roman world crucifixion was the most shameful of deaths, so early Christians more often showed Christ as the youthful Good Shepherd, the praying orant, the anchor (hope), or the dove. The cross rose to dominance only after Constantine abolished crucifixion.

666: a number that names an enemy. The “number of the beast,” 666, in Revelation 13:18, is framed as a riddle. Applying Hebrew gematria to Nero Caesar (nrwn qsr) yields exactly 666 — and an ancient variant reading 616 matches the Latin form of the name. Most scholars read the beast as the persecuting emperor Nero, his name encoded to shield the author from Roman eyes: sacred numerology as coded political resistance.

Connections

Christianity is unthinkable without Second Temple Judaism (Chapter 10) — its scriptures, its messianic hopes, its God — and it grew in the religious marketplace of the Roman Empire (Chapter 13), whose imperial cult it defied and whose roads and Greek it used. Its theology drew on Greek philosophy (Chapter 15): John opens by identifying Christ with the Logos. It is the sibling and rival of the Gnosticism and mystery cults of the next chapters and of the Rabbinic Judaism forming in the same centuries. Forward, its story dominates much of this archive: the great Christological councils, the split of East and West, the rise of Islam partly in response, the medieval church, the Reformation, and the movements that flow from it to the present.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The existence and crucifixion of Jesus — a Galilean Jewish teacher baptized by John and executed under Pilate c. 30–33 CE — on a very broad consensus, multiple early sources plus Josephus and Tacitus.
  • That his followers proclaimed him risen within a very few years (the pre-Pauline creed of 1 Cor 15), launching a rapidly spreading movement led by Paul, whose Gentile mission opened it to the Roman world.
  • The diversity of early Christianities, the slow canon formation, the rites of baptism and Eucharist, the reality of persecution and martyrdom, and the legalization under Constantine and establishment under Theodosius.

What’s not supported (as history)

  • The miraculous and theological claims — the virgin birth, the miracles, and above all the resurrection and divinity of Jesus — are matters of faith, not propositions history can establish; recorded here as the tradition’s beliefs, neither asserted as fact nor disproven.
  • The traditional eyewitness authorship of the Gospels, and the assumption they are straightforward biography rather than later, theologically-shaped testimony.
  • The old picture of one uniform original Christianity later corrupted by “heresies” — the evidence shows diversity from the very beginning.

What’s genuinely open

  • The historical Jesus beneath the Gospel portraits — how much of his teaching and biography can be securely recovered.
  • The precise dating and authorship of several New Testament books, and the shape of the hypothesized Q source.
  • The timing of the parting of the ways with Judaism, and why Christianity grew so successfully (charity, martyr-witness, message, Constantine’s patronage).
