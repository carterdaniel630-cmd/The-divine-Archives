Early Iron Age · Pre-exilic Israel

Pre-exilic Israel

In the archive

A blessing "by Yahweh and his Asherah"

Around 800 BCE, at a lonely desert way-station called Kuntillet Ajrud in the northeastern Sinai, travelers rested, and someone wrote a blessing on a large storage jar. In early Hebrew letters it reads, roughly: "I bless you by Yahweh of Samaria and by his Asherah." Nearby, at a tomb in Khirbet el-Qom in the Judean hills, another inscription of the same century blesses a man "by Yahweh… and by his Asherah." Asherah, in the older religious world of the region, was the great mother goddess. And here she is, twice, apparently paired with the God of Israel — his Asherah — in Hebrew, by Yahweh-worshippers.

For anyone who assumes that Israel was monotheist from the beginning, these jars are a jolt. They are also the perfect doorway into this chapter, because they point to its central, well-evidenced, and initially surprising thesis: the monotheism for which ancient Israel became famous was not its starting point but its achievement — the end of a long, contested development, not the beginning. The religion of Iron Age Israel and Judah, as reconstructed by archaeology and critical scholarship, was for most of its history not the strict worship of one God but something more crowded, more Canaanite, and more human. This is the story of how it changed.

A note on method: how we know any of this

Because this chapter will often differ from the familiar biblical picture, it is worth pausing — in the educational spirit of this project — to explain how historians reconstruct the religion of early Israel. They work from three kinds of evidence, and the tensions between them drive the whole field:

1. The Hebrew Bible — an incomparably rich source, but not a neutral chronicle. It reached its present form over centuries and was edited by writers with strong theological commitments (above all, the conviction that Israel should have worshipped Yahweh alone). It tells us what those editors believed and wanted, which is not always what most Israelites actually did. 2. Archaeology — the villages, shrines, altars, household figurines, and, above all, inscriptions left by the people themselves, which often capture ordinary religion the Bible's editors disapproved of. 3. Comparative texts — especially the library of clay tablets from Ugarit (modern Ras Shamra, on the Syrian coast), discovered in 1929. Ugarit was a Canaanite city, and its myths handed scholars, for the first time, the religious world out of which Israel grew — the same service the Gilgamesh tablets performed for Genesis in Chapter 1.

Reading these three together, most scholars now reconstruct a history that runs, in effect, backwards to the Bible's own narrative — and that reversal is the single most important thing to understand here.

Where Israel came from

The Bible tells of Israel arriving from outside — slaves fleeing Egypt, wandering Sinai, conquering Canaan under Joshua. The archaeological picture is different. The earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible is the Merneptah Stele, an Egyptian monument of about 1208 BCE that lists "Israel" among peoples in Canaan already defeated by Pharaoh — so Israel existed, in Canaan, by around 1210 BCE. And when archaeologists survey the central highlands for that period, they find not the scars of an invasion but a quiet population explosion: the number of settled sites leaps from fewer than a hundred in the Late Bronze Age to several hundred small new villages in Iron Age I (roughly 1200–1000 BCE). These villages are materially continuous with Canaanite culture — same pottery, same building styles — with a few tell-tale differences (an apparent avoidance of pork among them).

The mainstream conclusion is striking: the early Israelites were, for the most part, Canaanites — highland farmers and herders who coalesced into a new people as the great Bronze Age empires collapsed. A dramatic Exodus of a whole nation and a lightning conquest, as literally described, are not supported by the archaeology; a smaller group carrying an Exodus memory may lie behind the tradition, but the people of Israel largely emerged within the land.

If Israel was Canaanite, where did its distinctive god come from? Here scholars point south. Several of the oldest poems in the Bible say Yahweh "came from" Sinai, Seir, Edom, Teman — the desert southeast of Canaan. And Egyptian topographical lists from the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE mention "Yhw in the land of the Shasu" — Yahweh (or a place named for him) among nomads in exactly that southern region. This underpins the Kenite–Midianite hypothesis: that Yahweh began as the god of southern desert peoples (the Bible even makes Moses's Midianite father-in-law Jethro a priest of Yahweh), and that his worship spread north into the Canaanite highlands, where it fused with the existing religion. The hypothesis is widely held but not proven — a genuine open question flagged below.

The gods of early Israel

To grasp early Israelite religion you have to meet the Canaanite pantheon Ugarit revealed, because Israel inherited it. At its head was El — the wise, elderly high god, "father of the gods," king of the divine council. Below him were Baal, the young storm-and-fertility god who rides the clouds and battles the Sea and Death; Asherah (Ugaritic Athiratu), El's consort and mother of the gods; her fierce counterpart Anat; and dozens more.

Now notice something about Israel's own god. The very name Isra-el contains El, not Yahweh. The Bible freely calls Yahweh by El's titles (El Elyon, "God Most High"; El Shaddai) and — crucially — almost never polemicizes against El the way it rages against Baal. The scholarly consensus is that Yahweh and El were identified and merged: the incoming southern god Yahweh took over the role, titles, and mythology of the Canaanite high god El. Baal, by contrast, remained a rival, which is why the prophets attack him so bitterly — the fight against Baal is the fight to make Yahweh, not Baal, the effective god of rain and life.

The older layers of the Bible still preserve this crowded world if you read closely. Deuteronomy 32:8–9, in its oldest attested text, has the Most High divide up humanity "according to the number of the sons of God," each nation allotted to a divine son — and Yahweh's allotted portion is Israel. Psalm 82 opens with God standing up in the divine council, among the gods, to judge them. These are not the words of a religion that thinks Yahweh is the only god who exists; they are the words of monolatry — the worship of one god while assuming others are real. (Two terms worth fixing: monolatry = worshipping one god without denying that others exist; monotheism = holding that only one god exists at all. Israel travels from the first to the second.) And Asherah — as those desert jars attest — was for many Israelites Yahweh's consort, exactly as she had been El's.

The Baal Cycle and the battle with the sea

Ugarit did not just hand scholars a list of gods; it handed them a living mythology, and one story in particular reaches deep into the Bible. The Baal Cycle, six clay tablets of the fourteenth century BCE, tells how the storm-god Baal wins kingship among the gods by defeating two great enemies: Yam, the chaotic Sea, and Mot, Death itself, who swallows Baal into the underworld before Baal rises again. It is a myth of the storm-rider conquering chaos and death to secure his throne and the rains — and its language survived, transferred onto Yahweh. When a psalmist praises the God who "divided the Sea by his strength" and "broke the heads of the dragons on the waters," crushing the many-headed Leviathan (Psalm 74:13–14); when Isaiah promises that Yahweh will punish "Leviathan the fleeing serpent… and slay the dragon that is in the sea" (Isaiah 27:1); when God rebukes the Sea or pierces the chaos-monster Rahab (Job 26; Isaiah 51:9) — the biblical poets are reusing the imagery of Baal's combat with Yam and the sea-dragon Lotan (Leviathan's exact Ugaritic cognate), and crediting the victory to Yahweh alone. Even Isaiah's hope that God "will swallow up death forever" (Isaiah 25:8) inverts the Baal Cycle, in which it was Death who did the swallowing. The theology is transformed; the mythic vocabulary is inherited — the same relationship this project traced between Genesis and the Enuma Elish, a shared Near Eastern imagination re-forged around one God.

From many gods to one: the arrow of history reversed

Here is the chapter's core educational point. The Bible's own storyline is: Israel began pure (Yahweh alone), then repeatedly "went whoring" after other gods, and was punished. But the historical evidence points the other way. Israel began looking much like its Canaanite neighbors — Yahweh worshipped alongside El (soon merged), Baal, Asherah, and a host of household spirits — and only gradually, over centuries, did a movement arise insisting that Yahweh alone be worshipped, and finally that Yahweh alone exists.

Scholars call this the "Yahweh-alone movement." Its champions were a minority for much of the monarchy: certain prophets and priests who fought an uphill battle against the ordinary, comfortable polytheism of most people. The great eighth-century prophets — Amos, Hosea, Isaiah of Jerusalem, and Micah — are its voice, thundering against the worship of other gods and, just as fiercely, against social injustice (of which more below). Full-blown monotheism — the flat declaration that the other gods are nothing, "the work of human hands" — appears clearly only later, in the exile, in the poet scholars call Second Isaiah. So the famous monotheism of the Bible is real, but it is the hard-won conclusion of Israel's religious history, not its premise.

Law and the moral order

Israel's distinctive contribution to religious ethics grows from the idea of covenant (berit) — a binding treaty-like relationship between Yahweh and his people, modeled on the political treaties of the age, in which Yahweh gives the law and Israel owes exclusive loyalty. Its most famous distillation is the Decalogue (the Ten Commandments); its oldest legal core is the Covenant Code (Exodus 21–23).

An educational aside worth making: that Covenant Code is written largely in casuistic ("case") law — the "if a man does X, then Y shall follow" form — which is exactly the form of Mesopotamian law codes like Hammurabi's (Chapter 3), and it even shares specific provisions (the goring ox, retaliation "eye for eye"). Israel's law belongs to the broader Near Eastern legal tradition; what it adds is the framing of law as the stipulation of a covenant with a god rather than the decree of a king.

And in the prophets, Israel produced something genuinely new in the ancient world: the insistence that right worship without justice is worthless to God. Amos has Yahweh say he hates Israel's festivals and will not accept its sacrifices — "but let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream." Micah reduces religion to "do justice, love mercy, and walk humbly with your God." This fusion of the cultic and the ethical — a deity who cares less about the correctness of your sacrifice than about how you treat the poor, the widow, and the stranger — is one of the Iron Age's most consequential religious ideas.

Ritual and cult — official and folk

Official religion had a magnificent center: the Temple in Jerusalem, built (the Bible says) by Solomon. Tellingly, its described design is thoroughly Phoenician/Canaanite — a three-part plan (porch, main hall, inner sanctum) matched at excavated temples like ‘Ain Dara in Syria, fronted by two great free- standing bronze pillars named Jachin and Boaz, with a huge bronze basin ("the Sea") and, in the innermost Holy of Holies, two winged cherubim forming the throne of an invisible God above the Ark of the Covenant. Sacrifice, offered by a hereditary priesthood, was the central act.

But — and this is essential — for most of the pre-exilic period worship was not confined to Jerusalem at all. Israelites worshipped Yahweh (and others) at countless local high places (bamot): open-air hilltop shrines with altars, standing stones (masseboth, aniconic pillars marking a divine presence), and wooden sacred poles (asherim, associated with the goddess). Archaeology has even uncovered a small Yahwistic temple at Arad in Judah, complete with an altar and two standing stones in its sanctuary. In the northern kingdom of Israel, the king Jeroboam set up golden calf (or bull) images at Dan and Bethel as Yahweh's pedestals — condemned by the Bible, but plainly normal practice at the time.

Beneath even the local shrines lay family and folk religion — the daily piety of ordinary households, which the depth of this project requires us to take seriously. Its most eloquent witness is the Judean pillar figurine: a small clay figure of a woman supporting her breasts, of which hundreds have been found in Iron Age Judahite homes, most likely representing a goddess of fertility and protection (many think Asherah) invoked for the ordinary crises of childbirth, nursing, and survival. Households also kept teraphim (household gods/ancestor images), and there is evidence of a cult of the dead — offerings to ancestors. This was the religion most Israelites actually lived.

Death itself, in this pre-exilic world, led not to heaven or hell but to Sheol — a dim, dusty underworld beneath the earth where all the dead, righteous and wicked alike, sank to a shadowy half-existence, much like the Mesopotamian Kur of Chapter 3. There was as yet no resurrection and no judgment of the dead; the hope of a blessed afterlife would come later, partly under the Persian and Hellenistic currents flagged in Chapter 6. Yet the dead were not thought wholly gone: families fed and tended them at the tomb, and some sought them out. The Bible's own story of King Saul and the medium of Endor (1 Samuel 28), who calls up the ghost of the dead prophet Samuel from the ground to speak to the doomed king, is a vivid witness to a necromancy and cult of the dead that the reformers outlawed but could not pretend away.

The turning point was a pair of royal reforms. Late in the eighth century, King Hezekiah is said to have begun dismantling the high places; and around 622 BCE, King Josiah carried out a sweeping purge — centralizing all legitimate worship in the Jerusalem Temple and destroying the high places, the Asherah cult objects, the vessels for Baal, and even the Nehushtan, a bronze serpent attributed to Moses that people had been burning incense to. This reform was tied to the "discovery" in the Temple of a book of the law that most scholars identify with an early form of Deuteronomy — the manifesto of the Yahweh-alone movement. Josiah also defiled the Tophet in the Valley of Hinnom (Hebrew ge-hinnom — the origin, much later, of the word Gehenna for hell), where, the texts say, children had been sacrificed "to Molech." Whether, and how often, Israelites actually practiced child sacrifice is debated, but the reformers clearly believed they had to stamp it out.

The textual record: how the Bible was built

Because this is the tradition that produced the most influential book in history, its formation deserves a clear, educational account. The Torah (the first five books) was not, as tradition held, written by Moses. Since the nineteenth century, critical scholarship has argued it is a composite of several sources woven together — the Documentary Hypothesis, associated above all with Julius Wellhausen, which distinguished four strands:

- J (the Yahwist), which calls God "Yahweh" from the start — vivid, anthropomorphic storytelling (older layer); - E (the Elohist), which calls God "Elohim" until Moses; - D (the Deuteronomist), the source behind Deuteronomy and Josiah's reform (seventh century); - P (the Priestly source), concerned with ritual, genealogy, and order (exilic/post-exilic).

Two honest caveats, in keeping with the standard. First, this classic four-source model is now heavily revised and debated: many scholars favor "supplementary" or "fragmentary" models, push the sources later, or doubt E entirely. But the core insight — that the Torah is a composite of multiple sources from different centuries, not a single Mosaic composition — remains the mainstream position. Second, embedded in the Bible are genuinely archaic fragments — poems like the Song of Deborah (Judges 5) and the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15) — that may be among the oldest Hebrew literature, giving us glimpses of very early Yahwism (a storm-warrior god marching up from the south). The great narrative history of Israel's monarchy — the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua through Kings) — was shaped later still, to explain, in Yahweh-alone terms, why the kingdoms fell.

What the ground shows: the inscriptions

Few periods reward the "what's supported" test as concretely as this one, because Iron Age Israel left inscriptions we can read. Each is worth knowing for exactly what it does — and does not — prove:

- The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE): the earliest mention of "Israel"; proves a people called Israel existed in Canaan by ~1210 BCE. - The Tel Dan Stele (c. 840 BCE): an Aramean king's victory monument that refers to the "House of David" (bytdwd) — the first mention of David outside the Bible, strong evidence that a Davidic dynasty was real. (It does not settle how large or grand David's kingdom was — the fierce "minimalist vs. maximalist" debate over the United Monarchy continues.) - The Mesha Stele (the "Moabite Stone," c. 840 BCE): the Moabite king Mesha boasts of throwing off Israel's rule — and names both Israel's king Omri and "Yahweh," a foreign monarch attesting Israel's god. - Kuntillet Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom (c. 8th century): "Yahweh and his Asherah," our starting point — direct evidence of Yahwistic religion that included a goddess or her symbol. - The Ketef Hinnom silver amulets (c. 600 BCE): two tiny rolled silver scrolls, found in a Jerusalem burial cave, inscribed with a version of the Priestly Blessing ("Yahweh bless you and keep you…," Numbers 6:24–26). They are the oldest surviving fragment of any biblical text, and — worn on the body as protective charms — a beautiful witness to lived, apotropaic religion just before the exile.

The believer's lens

The tradition that grew from this world — and the Hebrew Bible as it was finally edited — tells a clear and powerful story: that there is one God, YHWH, who chose Israel, brought them out of Egypt, gave them the Law at Sinai, and bound them in covenant; that Israel's calling was to worship him alone and do justice; and that the prophets were sent to recall a faithless people to that one Lord. Read from inside the faith, the history of Israelite religion is the history of a revelation progressively understood and too often betrayed — the people forever tempted toward the Baals and Asherahs of their neighbors, the prophets forever calling them back to the God who is truly one. This is the account Judaism, Christianity, and Islam all inherit, and to the believer its truth is the covenant itself: a God who acts in history and holds a people answerable.

The skeptical lens

The historian and archaeologist tell the story the other way around, and this chapter is largely built on their reconstruction. The evidence — the "YHWH and his Asherah" inscriptions, the Ugaritic texts, the biblical text read critically — indicates that early Israelite religion was not originally monotheist but part of the wider West Semitic world: YHWH was worshipped alongside other gods, may have had a divine consort in Asherah, and shared imagery with the Canaanite storm-god Baal. Strict monotheism, on this reading, was a development — the achievement of a "Yahweh-alone" party and the exilic and post-exilic editors, not the starting point — so the Bible's own narrative of an original pure monotheism repeatedly corrupted is the reverse of the likely historical arrow. The Bible is also a layered, much-edited document, not a contemporary chronicle. None of this touches the theological claim of a God who reveals himself; it marks the boundary, as always, between the faith's self-understanding and what the inscriptions and strata actually show — which is a religion that became monotheist over centuries.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Israel's symbolic world is unusually important, because two of its choices — about the divine Name and about divine images — shaped half the religious history that followed.

The Name. Israel's god has a personal name, written with four Hebrew consonants YHWH — the Tetragrammaton ("four letters"). Its meaning is uncertain and much discussed: the Bible links it (Exodus 3:14) to the verb "to be" — "I am who I am" or "I will be what I will be" — and some scholars read it as a causative, "he who brings into being." Over time the Name grew so holy that it ceased to be pronounced aloud; readers substituted Adonai ("my Lord") or simply ha-Shem ("the Name"). Much later, when Hebrew scribes (the Masoretes) added vowel marks to the consonantal text, they placed the vowels of Adonai onto YHWH as a reminder to say "Adonai" — a hybrid that Christian readers later misread as "Jehovah," a word no ancient Israelite ever spoke. The reverent un-naming of God is one of the most consequential pieces of sacred encoding in history.

The alphabet itself. It is easy to forget that the very script these people used was a revolutionary technology. The alphabet — one sign per sound — was invented in the Bronze Age Levant (the "Proto-Sinaitic" script, c. 1900–1500 BCE, adapting a few Egyptian hieroglyphs by acrophony, using a picture for its initial sound) and matured into the Phoenician and paleo-Hebrew alphabets. It made literacy vastly easier than Egypt's hundreds of hieroglyphs or Mesopotamia's cuneiform, and it is the ancestor of Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Arabic, and this very page. An honesty note: the famous Jewish practice of gematria — assigning number-values to Hebrew letters to find hidden meanings — is a much later, Hellenistic-era development and should not be read back into pre-exilic Israel, where the alphabet's power was linguistic and administrative, not numerological.

Aniconism, and its limits. A signature of developed Israelite religion is aniconism — the refusal to depict Yahweh in any image (the second commandment). In the Holy of Holies there was no statue, only the empty space above the cherubim: an invisible God enthroned on nothing you could see. The standing stones (masseboth) mark this same aniconic instinct — a bare pillar for a presence that has no form. Yet the hundreds of household figurines show that this aniconism was an ideal fought for by reformers, not a universal practice — the reality on the ground was far more image-rich, which is precisely why the reformers had to keep smashing things.

The Temple as cosmos. Like the temples of Egypt and Mesopotamia, Solomon's was a model of the ordered world: its three zones of increasing holiness, its lampstand (the menorah), its cherubim and palm-and-flower carvings evoking a garden of Eden, its bronze "Sea" recalling the waters of chaos held in check — architecture as theology.

Connections

Backward and beside, this chapter's connections are dense: to Canaan/Ugarit (the El–Baal–Asherah world Israel grew from), to Mesopotamia (the shared flood of Chapter 1, the creation and law parallels of Chapter 3 — Genesis 1's ordered cosmos answering the Enuma Elish, the Covenant Code echoing Hammurabi), and to Egypt (the Exodus memory, and the Great Hymn to the Aten's echo in Psalm 104, Chapter 2). Forward, everything here is prologue to the transformation that the next era must open with: the Babylonian exile of 586 BCE, which destroyed the Temple, ended the monarchy, and — paradoxically — forged the crisis in which Israelite monolatry finally hardened into full monotheism and the religion we can properly call Judaism was born. It is also in and after the exile, under Persian rule, that the Zoroastrian-influence question raised in Chapter 6 comes due.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • Israel emerged largely within Canaan in Iron Age I (Merneptah Stele; the highland settlement explosion; material continuity with Canaanite culture), rather than through a large-scale external Exodus and conquest as literally described.
  • Early Israelite religion was not originally monotheistic. Yahweh was worshipped alongside other deities; he was identified/merged with the Canaanite high god El; and Asherah was venerated in Yahwistic contexts (Kuntillet Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom).
  • Worship was widespread and local (high places, standing stones, the Arad sanctuary, the northern calf shrines) and included a rich household/folk religion (the Judean pillar figurines, teraphim, ancestor offerings). The pre-exilic dead went to Sheol, a neutral shadowy underworld, with no developed resurrection or judgment yet.
  • The Ugaritic Baal Cycle (Baal vs. Yam and Mot) supplies the mythic vocabulary the Hebrew poets reused and re-credited to Yahweh — the divine combat with the Sea and with Leviathan/Rahab (Psalm 74, Isaiah 27, Job 26) and the "swallowing up of death" (Isaiah 25).
  • The centralizing reforms of Hezekiah and (especially) Josiah (c. 622 BCE), tied to an early form of Deuteronomy, are historically grounded.
  • The Hebrew Bible is a composite text compiled over centuries (multiple sources, not Mosaic authorship), though the exact source model is debated.
  • A Davidic dynasty existed (Tel Dan Stele); the Ketef Hinnom amulets (c. 600 BCE) are the oldest surviving biblical text; the alphabet is a Levantine Bronze Age invention.

What's not supported by the evidence

  • The biblical framing of an original pure monotheism later corrupted by apostasy. The historical arrow runs the other way: from polytheism/monolatry toward monotheism.
  • A historical Exodus and conquest at the scale and in the manner the Bible narrates.
  • Reading later developments — full monotheism, rabbinic practice, gematria — back into the pre-exilic period.

What's genuinely open

  • Whether "his Asherah" refers to the goddess herself or to a wooden cult object/symbol bearing her name (scholars are divided).
  • The historicity and scale of the United Monarchy of David and Solomon: that a Davidic line existed is supported; whether it ruled a large, wealthy empire is hotly debated (minimalists vs. maximalists).
  • The precise origin of Yahweh (the Kenite–Midianite/southern hypothesis is strong but unproven).
  • The exact dating and composition of the biblical sources.
  • The extent and nature of child sacrifice in Israel and Judah.
