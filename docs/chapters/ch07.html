Early Iron Age · Pre-exilic Israel

Pre-exilic Israel

In the archive

Around 800 BCE, someone blessed a traveler “by Yahweh… and by his Asherah.” For anyone who assumes Israel was always monotheist, that desert graffito is a jolt — and the doorway to this chapter’s thesis: Israel’s monotheism was not its starting point but its achievement.

A blessing “by Yahweh and his Asherah”

Around 800 BCE, at a lonely desert way-station called Kuntillet Ajrud in the northeastern Sinai, someone wrote a blessing on a large storage jar: “I bless you by Yahweh of Samaria and by his Asherah.” At a tomb in Khirbet el-Qom in the Judean hills, another inscription of the same century blesses a man “by Yahweh… and by his Asherah.” Asherah, in the older religious world of the region, was the great mother goddess. And here she is, twice, paired with the God of Israel — his Asherah — in Hebrew, by Yahweh-worshippers.

These jars point to this chapter’s central, well-evidenced, and initially surprising thesis: the monotheism for which Israel became famous was not its starting point but its achievement — the end of a long, contested development, not the beginning. The religion of Iron Age Israel, as reconstructed from archaeology and critical scholarship, was for most of its history not the strict worship of one God but something more crowded, more Canaanite, and more human. This is the story of how it changed.

A note on method: how we know any of this

Because this chapter often differs from the familiar biblical picture, it helps to explain how historians reconstruct early Israel. They work from three kinds of evidence: (1) the Hebrew Bible — incomparably rich, but a later, edited text shaped by writers convinced Israel should have worshipped Yahweh alone, so it tells us what they believed more than what most people did; (2) archaeology — the villages, shrines, household figurines, and especially inscriptions ordinary people left; and (3) comparative texts, above all the Canaanite tablets from Ugarit (discovered 1929), which revealed the religious world Israel grew out of — the same service the Gilgamesh tablets did for Genesis. Read together, they yield a history that runs, in effect, backwards to the Bible’s own narrative — and that reversal is the key thing to understand.

A note on faith and history. What follows is the historical reconstruction — what the material evidence indicates about how the religion of Israel actually developed over time. That is a different kind of statement from a claim of faith. Many Jews and Christians hold the biblical narrative as sacred and theologically true, and describing how the historical religion changed is not a verdict on those convictions. In keeping with this archive’s standard, two things are kept distinct and both are respected: what a tradition holds sacred, and what the material evidence shows. Neither is used here to debunk the other — this is description, not polemic, in either direction.

Where Israel came from

The Bible tells of Israel arriving from outside — slaves fleeing Egypt, conquering Canaan under Joshua. The archaeology tells a different story. The earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible, the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE), lists “Israel” already in Canaan. And the central highlands show not an invasion but a population explosion: from fewer than a hundred Late Bronze sites to several hundred small new villages in Iron Age I (c. 1200–1000 BCE), materially continuous with Canaanite culture. The mainstream conclusion is striking: the early Israelites were largely Canaanites — highland farmers who coalesced into a new people as the Bronze Age empires collapsed. A whole-nation Exodus and lightning conquest, as literally described, are not supported; a smaller group may carry the Exodus memory, but Israel largely emerged within the land.

Where did its distinctive god come from? Several of the oldest biblical poems say Yahweh “came from” Sinai, Seir, Edom — the desert southeast of Canaan — and Egyptian lists of the 14th–13th centuries BCE mention “Yhw in the land of the Shasu,” nomads in exactly that region. This underpins the Kenite–Midianite hypothesis: that Yahweh began as a southern desert god (the Bible even makes Moses’s Midianite father-in-law a priest of Yahweh) whose worship spread north and fused with highland religion — widely held, but not proven.

The gods of early Israel

Ugarit revealed the Canaanite pantheon Israel inherited: at its head El, the wise elderly high god, king of the divine council; below him Baal, the young storm-and-fertility god; Asherah, El’s consort and mother of the gods; and many more. Notice that the very name Isra-el contains El, not Yahweh; that the Bible freely gives Yahweh El’s titles (El Elyon, El Shaddai); and that it almost never attacks El the way it rages at Baal. The consensus: Yahweh and El were merged — the incoming southern god took over the role and mythology of the Canaanite high god — while Baal remained a rival, which is why the prophets fight him so bitterly.

The older biblical layers still preserve this crowded world. Deuteronomy 32:8–9, in its oldest text, has the Most High divide humanity “according to the number of the sons of God,” with Yahweh allotted Israel; Psalm 82 has God stand up in the divine council, among the gods. This is monolatry — worshipping one god without denying others exist — not yet monotheism, the claim that only one god exists. And Asherah, those jars attest, was for many Israelites Yahweh’s consort, as she had been El’s.

The Baal Cycle and the battle with the sea

Ugarit did not just hand scholars a list of gods; it handed them a living mythology, and one story reaches deep into the Bible. The Baal Cycle, six clay tablets of the fourteenth century BCE, tells how the storm-god Baal wins kingship by defeating two great enemies: Yam, the chaotic Sea, and Mot, Death itself, who swallows Baal into the underworld before Baal rises again. Its language survived, transferred onto Yahweh. When a psalmist praises the God who “divided the Sea by his strength” and broke the heads of the dragons, crushing the many-headed Leviathan (Psalm 74:13–14); when Isaiah promises that Yahweh will slay “Leviathan the fleeing serpent… the dragon that is in the sea” (Isaiah 27:1); when God pierces the chaos-monster Rahab (Job 26; Isaiah 51:9) — the biblical poets are reusing the imagery of Baal’s combat with Yam and the sea-dragon Lotan (Leviathan’s exact Ugaritic cognate) and crediting the victory to Yahweh alone. Even Isaiah’s hope that God “will swallow up death forever” (Isaiah 25:8) inverts the Baal Cycle, in which it was Death who did the swallowing. The theology is transformed; the mythic vocabulary is inherited — the same relationship this project traced between Genesis and the Enuma Elish.

From many gods to one: the arrow reversed

Here is the core point. The Bible’s storyline is: Israel began pure, then “went whoring” after other gods and was punished. The evidence points the other way. Israel began looking much like its Canaanite neighbors — Yahweh alongside El (soon merged), Baal, Asherah, and household spirits — and only gradually did a minority movement insist that Yahweh alone be worshipped, and finally that Yahweh alone exists. Scholars call it the “Yahweh-alone movement,” and its voice is the great eighth-century prophets — Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah. Full-blown monotheism — the flat declaration that other gods are “nothing” — appears clearly only later, in the exile. The famous monotheism of the Bible is real, but it is the hard-won conclusion of Israel’s history, not its premise.

Law and the moral order

Israel’s ethics grow from covenant (berit) — a treaty-like bond in which Yahweh gives the law and Israel owes exclusive loyalty — distilled in the Decalogue and codified in the Covenant Code (Exodus 21–23). That code is written largely in casuistic (“if…then”) law, exactly the form of Mesopotamian codes like Hammurabi’s (Chapter 3), sharing specific provisions — Israel’s law belongs to the broader Near Eastern tradition, reframed as the stipulation of a covenant with a god. And in the prophets Israel produced something new: the insistence that worship without justice is worthless to God. Amos has Yahweh hate Israel’s festivals — “but let justice roll down like waters”; Micah reduces religion to “do justice, love mercy, and walk humbly with your God.”

Ritual and cult — official and folk

Official religion centered on the Temple in Jerusalem, whose described design is thoroughly Phoenician/Canaanite — a three-part plan matched at excavated temples like ‘Ain Dara, two great bronze pillars (Jachin and Boaz), a bronze “Sea,” and, in the Holy of Holies, two winged cherubim forming the throne of an invisible God above the Ark. But for most of the period, worship was not confined to Jerusalem: Israelites worshipped at countless local high places (bamot) with altars, standing stones (masseboth), and wooden sacred poles; archaeology even uncovered a Yahwistic temple at Arad with two standing stones, and the northern kingdom kept golden calf images at Dan and Bethel as Yahweh’s pedestals.

Beneath the shrines lay family and folk religion — the piety most Israelites actually lived. Its great witness is the Judean pillar figurine, a small clay woman supporting her breasts, found by the hundreds in Iron Age Judahite homes, most likely a goddess of fertility and protection (many think Asherah). Households kept teraphim (household gods) and made offerings to the dead. Death itself led not to heaven or hell but to Sheol, a dim underworld where all the dead sank to a shadowy half-existence, much like the Mesopotamian Kur — no resurrection, no judgment yet; the story of King Saul and the medium of Endor (1 Samuel 28), calling up the dead prophet Samuel, witnesses a necromancy the reformers outlawed but could not pretend away. The turning point was the royal reforms: Hezekiah began dismantling the high places, and around 622 BCE King Josiah carried out a sweeping purge — centralizing worship in Jerusalem and destroying the high places, the Asherah objects, and even the Nehushtan, a bronze serpent people burned incense to — tied to a “book of the law” most identify with an early Deuteronomy. Josiah also defiled the Tophet in the Valley of Hinnom (Hebrew ge-hinnom, later the word Gehenna for hell), where children were said to be sacrificed “to Molech” — a practice whose reality is debated.

The textual record: how the Bible was built

The Torah was not, as tradition held, written by Moses. Critical scholarship argues it is a composite — the Documentary Hypothesis (Wellhausen) distinguished four strands: J (the Yahwist, vivid and anthropomorphic), E (the Elohist), D (the Deuteronomist, behind Josiah’s reform), and P (the Priestly source, exilic/post-exilic). Two honest caveats: the classic four-source model is now heavily revised and debated (supplementary and fragmentary models, later datings), though the core insight — a composite Torah, not a Mosaic composition — holds; and embedded in the Bible are genuinely archaic poems (the Song of Deborah, Judges 5; the Song of the Sea, Exodus 15) that glimpse very early Yahwism. The narrative history of the monarchy — the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua–Kings) — was shaped later, to explain in Yahweh-alone terms why the kingdoms fell.

What the ground shows: the inscriptions

  • Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE): earliest mention of “Israel” — a people by that name existed in Canaan by ~1210 BCE.
  • Tel Dan Stele (c. 840 BCE): refers to the “House of David” — the first mention of David outside the Bible and strong evidence of a Davidic dynasty (though not of the scale of his kingdom, which is debated).
  • Mesha Stele (c. 840 BCE): a Moabite king names both Israel’s king Omri and “Yahweh.”
  • Kuntillet Ajrud & Khirbet el-Qom (8th c.): “Yahweh and his Asherah.”
  • Ketef Hinnom silver amulets (c. 600 BCE): two tiny rolled silver scrolls with the Priestly Blessing (Numbers 6:24–26) — the oldest surviving fragment of any biblical text, worn as protective charms.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The Name. Israel’s god has a personal name of four consonants, YHWH — the Tetragrammaton. Its meaning is uncertain: the Bible links it (Exodus 3:14) to the verb “to be” — “I am who I am” — and some read a causative, “he who brings into being.” The Name grew so holy it ceased to be spoken; readers said Adonai (“my Lord”) or ha-Shem (“the Name”). Later scribes wrote the vowels of Adonai onto YHWH as a reminder — a hybrid Christians misread as “Jehovah,” a word no Israelite ever spoke.

The alphabet. The script itself was revolutionary: the alphabet — one sign per sound — was invented in the Bronze Age Levant (“Proto-Sinaitic,” c. 1900–1500 BCE, adapting a few hieroglyphs by acrophony) and matured into Phoenician and paleo-Hebrew, ancestor of Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Arabic, and this page. Honesty note: Jewish gematria (letter-number values) is a much later, Hellenistic development and should not be read back into pre-exilic Israel.

Aniconism, and its limits. Developed Israelite religion refused to depict Yahweh (the second commandment): in the Holy of Holies there was no statue, only empty space above the cherubim; the standing stones mark the same instinct — a bare pillar for a formless presence. Yet the hundreds of household figurines show aniconism was an ideal fought for by reformers, not a universal practice — which is exactly why they kept smashing things.

The Temple as cosmos. Like the temples of Egypt and Mesopotamia, Solomon’s was a model of the ordered world: three zones of increasing holiness, the lampstand (menorah), garden imagery evoking Eden, and the bronze “Sea” recalling the chaos-waters held in check — architecture as theology.

Connections

Backward and beside: to Canaan/Ugarit (the El–Baal–Asherah world Israel grew from), to Mesopotamia (the shared flood of Chapter 1; Genesis 1 answering the Enuma Elish; the Covenant Code echoing Hammurabi), and to Egypt (the Exodus memory; the Aten hymn’s echo in Psalm 104). Forward, all of this is prologue to the Babylonian exile of 586 BCE, which destroyed the Temple and — paradoxically — forged the crisis in which monolatry hardened into full monotheism and the religion we can call Judaism was born; it is also then, under Persian rule, that the Zoroastrian-influence question of Chapter 6 comes due.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • Israel emerged largely within Canaan in Iron Age I (Merneptah Stele; the highland settlement explosion; cultural continuity), not through a large-scale external Exodus and conquest as literally described.
  • Early Israelite religion was not originally monotheistic: Yahweh was worshipped alongside other deities, merged with the high god El, and paired with Asherah in Yahwistic contexts.
  • Worship was widespread and local (high places, standing stones, the Arad sanctuary, the northern calf shrines) and included a rich household/folk religion (the pillar figurines, teraphim, ancestor offerings); the pre-exilic dead went to Sheol, a neutral shadowy underworld, with no developed resurrection or judgment yet.
  • The Ugaritic Baal Cycle (Baal vs. Yam and Mot) supplies the mythic vocabulary the Hebrew poets reused and re-credited to Yahweh — the combat with the Sea and with Leviathan/Rahab (Psalm 74, Isaiah 27, Job 26).
  • The reforms of Hezekiah and especially Josiah (c. 622 BCE), tied to an early Deuteronomy.
  • The Bible is a composite text compiled over centuries; a Davidic dynasty existed (Tel Dan); the Ketef Hinnom amulets are the oldest surviving biblical text; the alphabet is a Levantine invention.

What’s not supported

  • The biblical framing of an original pure monotheism later corrupted by apostasy; the historical arrow runs from polytheism/monolatry toward monotheism.
  • A historical Exodus and conquest at the scale and in the manner the Bible narrates.
  • Reading later developments — full monotheism, rabbinic practice, gematria — back into the pre-exilic period.

What’s genuinely open

  • Whether “his Asherah” means the goddess or a wooden cult object/symbol bearing her name.
  • The historicity and scale of the United Monarchy of David and Solomon (a dynasty is supported; a large empire is hotly debated).
  • The precise origin of Yahweh (the southern/Kenite–Midianite hypothesis is strong but unproven).
  • The dating and composition of the biblical sources; and the extent of child sacrifice.
