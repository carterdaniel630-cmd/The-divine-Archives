Early Medieval · Tibetan & Vajrayana Buddhism

Tibetan & Vajrayana Buddhism

In the archive

For weeks, monks bend over a table, tapping colored sand grain by grain from metal funnels into a vast, intricate diagram — a palace of deities rendered in dyed stone, dazzlingly precise. And then, when it is finished and has been contemplated, they destroy it: sweep the whole shimmering cosmos into a heap of grey dust and pour it into a river. The sand mandala is Tibetan Buddhism in miniature — a tradition of overwhelming ritual richness built entirely to teach that nothing whatever is to be clung to.

The third vehicle

Buddhism is often described as three "vehicles." The Theravada (ch11) preserves the earliest path; the Mahayana (ch20) opened liberation to all beings through the compassion of the bodhisattva. Vajrayana — the "Diamond" or "Thunderbolt Vehicle," named for the vajra, the indestructible adamantine scepter — is the third: a tantric development of Mahayana, grown from the same Indian Tantra (ch24) that transformed Hinduism, which claims to offer the swiftest route of all, the attainment of full Buddhahood not over countless lifetimes but potentially within a single one. It does this through powerful and secret techniques — deity visualization, mantra, mandala, and the direct transmission of a realized teacher — that are held to be too potent to practice without a qualified guru (lama) and initiation.

Buddhism comes to the Land of Snows

Vajrayana became the state religion of Tibet through a decisive act of importation. Around 760 CE the Tibetan king Trisong Detsen invited two Indian masters — the philosopher Śāntarakṣita and, above all, the tantric adept Padmasambhava ("Guru Rinpoche," the "Lotus-Born") — to the Land of Snows. Tradition holds that Padmasambhava subdued the wild local gods and demons of Tibet, binding them by oath into protectors of the Dharma, and with the king founded Samye, the first monastery. A colossal translation project rendered the Sanskrit scriptures into Tibetan, creating the "Old Translations" that anchor the tradition. The Buddhism that took root did not erase the indigenous religion, Bön — the older Tibetan tradition of spirits, ritual, and (later) its own reformed monastic system — but absorbed and contended with it, so that Tibetan religion carries a deep substrate of the land's own gods, now sworn to the Buddha.

The four schools

Tibetan Buddhism developed four principal schools, each a distinct lineage of transmission.

  • Nyingma, "the Ancient Ones," the oldest, traces itself to Padmasambhava and the first translations. It preserves Dzogchen ("Great Perfection"), a teaching of the mind's primordial, sky-like purity, and the tradition of terma — "treasure texts" believed to have been hidden by Padmasambhava to be rediscovered by destined masters (tertöns) in later ages.
  • Kagyu, the "Oral Lineage," specializes in yogic practices passed mouth-to-ear from master to disciple, tracing to the Indian adept Nāropa, his Tibetan translator-student Marpa, and Marpa's disciple, the beloved poet-hermit Milarepa. Its heart is Mahāmudrā and the "six yogas."
  • Sakya became renowned for scholarship and, in the 13th century, for a period of political rule over Tibet under Mongol patronage.
  • Gelug, "the Virtuous," the newest and now largest, was founded on the 14th–15th-century monastic reform of Tsongkhapa, who restored strict discipline and rigorous study. From the Gelug come the Dalai Lamas and Panchen Lamas.

The tantric method

What distinguishes Vajrayana is its method. In deity yoga, the practitioner does not merely worship a Buddha but, through visualization and initiation, identifies with an enlightened deity, "taking the result as the path" — rehearsing Buddhahood until it becomes real. The mandala is the sacred map of this practice: a palace of the deity and its retinue, laid out in perfect sacred geometry, which the meditator enters in the mind. Mantra — sacred sound — is central (the tradition's very name, Mantrayāna, means "the Mantra Vehicle"); the most famous, Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ, is the mantra of Avalokiteśvara (Tibetan Chenrezig), the bodhisattva of compassion, held to contain the essence of the whole path. And everywhere the two poles of enlightenment are joined: wisdom (prajñā, insight into emptiness) and method/compassion (upāya), symbolized by the ritual bell (wisdom) and vajra (method) held together in the lama's two hands, and dramatized in the "father-mother" (yab-yum) images of deities in union. Even the wrathful deities, fanged and flame-wreathed, are not demons but the ferocious energy of enlightenment itself, turned against ignorance.

The reincarnate lamas

Tibet produced one of the most distinctive institutions in all religion: the tulku system of recognized reincarnations. A great teacher, on dying, is believed to take rebirth deliberately to continue his work, and the child who is his reincarnation is sought out and identified by signs and tests, then enthroned and educated to resume the lineage. The most famous tulku line is the Dalai Lama, regarded as the successive emanation of Avalokiteśvara himself and, from the 17th century until 1959, the temporal ruler of Tibet; a parallel line is the Panchen Lama. The system is a living institution — and a contested one: the recognition of the current Panchen Lama is disputed between Tibetan Buddhists and the Chinese state, and the future of the Dalai Lama's own succession is a matter of international controversy today.

Death, the bardo, and the sky

Tibetan Buddhism mapped death with unusual precision. The Bardo Thödol — "Liberation Through Hearing in the Intermediate State," known in the West as the Tibetan Book of the Dead — is read aloud to the dying and the dead to guide the consciousness through the bardo, the roughly forty-nine-day transitional state, past a sequence of peaceful and wrathful visions that the text insists are projections of one's own mind, toward either liberation or a favorable rebirth (ch47). And in the treeless high plateau, where burial and cremation are difficult, the dead are given the extraordinary rite of sky burial (jhator): the body, now an empty vessel, is offered on a mountain charnel-ground to the vultures — a final act of generosity, feeding one's flesh back to living beings, and a last, literal lesson in impermanence.

The believer's lens

To its practitioners, Tibetan Buddhism is the swiftest path to the Buddha's awakening, and one of great compassion: the goal is enlightenment for the sake of all beings. Its tantric method uses the whole person: the practitioner visualises himself or herself as an enlightened deity (deity yoga), recites the deity's mantra, and enters the deity's palace in the mandala, training the mind to see all appearances as pure. Teachers are central, and the great ones, the tulkus, are recognised as rebirths of earlier masters: the Dalai Lamas as emanations of Avalokiteshvara, compassion itself. The Bardo Thödol guides the dead through the intermediate state toward liberation, and sky burial gives the body to the birds as a last act of generosity. The sand mandala, built grain by grain and swept away, teaches the whole tradition's point: impermanence, and not clinging. For believers, the miraculous deeds of Padmasambhava and the treasure texts he hid are the foundation of the Nyingma school.

The skeptical lens

The historian documents Buddhism's arrival in Tibet from the eighth century under Trisong Detsen, the founding of Samye, the vast translation projects, and the four schools. Figures such as Tsongkhapa, Marpa and Milarepa are historical; behind the legend of Padmasambhava stands a historical teacher, but his miracle stories are hagiography. Historians regard many terma, the "treasure texts" said to have been hidden by him and found centuries later, as later compositions, and the identification of any tulku as the true rebirth of a predecessor is a religious claim that cannot be tested. How much Tibetan Buddhism absorbed from the older Bön religion is unclear because the sources are late. The sociologist also notes that the tulku system also became a way of passing on authority, and that the current disputes over the Dalai and Panchen Lama successions are political as well as religious. The archive documents what the practices are held to accomplish without judging those inner claims.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Tibetan Buddhism is perhaps the most densely visual and symbolic of all religious traditions; its art is its theology.

  • The mandala encodes the enlightened cosmos as sacred geometry — concentric rings of fire, vajras, and lotus petals around a central square palace — and, as sand, encodes impermanence by being destroyed. It is at once a map of the universe, a map of the mind, and a doorway to be entered.
  • The vajra and bell encode the inseparable union of method/compassion and wisdom — the whole of the path held in two hands.
  • Mantra in motion. The prayer wheel, a cylinder packed with printed mantras, is held to "recite" them with every turn; the prayer flags loose their printed prayers on the wind. Sacred sound is multiplied by motion, so that the very landscape prays.
  • The eight auspicious symbols (aṣṭamaṅgala) — the endless knot, the lotus, the wheel, the parasol, the conch, the treasure vase, the golden fish, and the victory banner — recur throughout Tibetan art as a coded vocabulary of blessing, and the thangka scroll-painting encodes entire deities, lineages, and cosmologies for meditation, every color and gesture (mudrā) fixed by meaning.

Connections

Vajrayana is the third great turning of Buddhism, growing out of the Mahayana (ch20) and drawing directly on the Indian Tantra (ch24) that also reshaped Hinduism — the two are siblings of the same tantric age. Its taming of the local gods of Tibet parallels the way Shinto (ch25) and the Norse and Slavic worlds negotiated between old spirits and new creeds. Its map of the after-death bardo belongs with the great journeys to the underworld (ch47); its deity yoga and mandala are the fullest development of sacred symbology and encoding treated throughout this archive; and its living institution of the Dalai Lama carries the tradition into the politics of the modern world.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The historical transmission of Buddhism to Tibet from the 8th century under Trisong Detsen, the founding of Samye, and the great translation projects; the existence and doctrines of the four schools; the historicity of key figures (Tsongkhapa, Marpa, Milarepa, and the historical Padmasambhava behind the legend); the tantric methods of deity yoga, mandala, and mantra; the Bardo Thödol as a text; the tulku institution and the historical line of the Dalai and Panchen Lamas; and the practices of sky burial and the sand mandala. These are all well documented.

Not supported (as established fact)

The miraculous hagiography of Padmasambhava (subduing demons, magical feats) is religious tradition, not history. The terma "treasure text" attributions — that specific scriptures were hidden by Padmasambhava and rediscovered centuries later — are matters of traditional faith; historians generally regard many terma as later compositions. And the specific identification of any tulku as the genuine rebirth of his predecessor is a religious claim, not an empirically verifiable one.

Genuinely open

The precise pre-Buddhist content of Bön and how much of Tibetan Buddhism absorbed from it (the sources are late and contested); the historical kernel within the Padmasambhava traditions; and, as with all such practices, the archive documents what deity yoga and the bardo teachings are held to accomplish without adjudicating those inner claims. The politics of the current succession disputes are ongoing and unresolved.
