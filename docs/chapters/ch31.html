Early Modern · The Reformation

The Reformation

In the archive

The sixteenth-century revolt that split Western Christianity in two — begun as a quarrel over the sale of forgiveness, carried across a continent by a new machine, and ending in a hundred years of war and a Christendom shattered into churches — told with care for what happened, what the reformers claimed, and what remains a matter of faith.

The door at Wittenberg

On the thirty-first of October, 1517 — so the tradition holds — an Augustinian friar and professor named Martin Luther walked to the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg and nailed to it a sheet of ninety-five propositions, in Latin, for academic debate. Whether he actually hammered them to the door is genuinely uncertain; some historians think the dramatic scene a later legend, and that he simply mailed them to his bishop. What is not in doubt is what the Ninety-Five Theses attacked: the sale of indulgences — papal certificates that promised to shorten a soul's time in purgatory, hawked across Germany by the friar Johann Tetzel with the jingle, "as soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs." Luther meant to start a scholarly argument. He started the fracture of the Latin Church.

What the Reformation was

The Protestant Reformation was the movement that, over the sixteenth century, broke the thousand-year unity of Western Christianity under the pope and founded the rival family of Protestant churches. It began as a protest against specific corruptions — indulgences, clerical wealth, the abuses of Rome — but it swiftly became something far larger: a wholesale challenge to the authority of the pope, the priesthood, and the traditions of the Church, in the name of a Christianity refounded on the Bible and on faith alone.

The spark and the machine

Luther's protest would likely have died as a local dispute, as earlier ones had, but for a new technology. The printing press, barely seventy years old, seized on the Theses: within weeks they were translated from Latin into German and printed and reprinted across the empire, and Luther's later pamphlets sold in the hundreds of thousands. For the first time a religious rebel could reach a whole society over the heads of the authorities. The Reformation was the first mass movement of the age of print — and it made the book, not the priest, the center of the faith.

The five "solas"

Protestant theology crystallized into a handful of Latin slogans that defined it against Rome:

  • Sola scriptura — "Scripture alone": the Bible, not the pope or church tradition, is the sole authority for Christian faith.
  • Sola fide — "faith alone": a person is justified, made right with God, by faith and not by good works or the Church's sacraments.
  • Sola gratia — "grace alone": salvation is God's unearned gift, not a reward.
  • and, behind them, solus Christus and soli Deo gloria — Christ alone as mediator, glory to God alone.

From these flowed the rest: the priesthood of all believers (every Christian has direct access to God, needing no priest); the vernacular Bible, so that all could read it — Luther's own German translation reshaped the language itself; and the rejection of purgatory, of most of the seven sacraments, of monasticism, clerical celibacy, and the cult of the saints.

The reformers and the radicals

The movement fractured almost at once into rival streams. Luther led in Germany. In Switzerland, Huldrych Zwingli at Zürich and then John Calvin at Geneva built the Reformed tradition; Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion and his austere doctrine of predestination — that God has eternally chosen the saved and the damned — became the most rigorous system of Protestant thought and spread to the Dutch, the Scots (as Presbyterianism), and the English Puritans. In England the break was as much political as theological: Henry VIII, denied an annulment, made himself head of an English church, which settled into the Anglican via media between Rome and Geneva.

And on the movement's edge stood the Radical Reformation — the Anabaptists, who rejected infant baptism for adult believers' baptism, and often the whole alliance of church and state. Persecuted by Catholics and mainline Protestants alike, and stained by the violent millenarian takeover of Münster in 1534–35, they nonetheless carried ideas — believers' baptism, the separation of church and state, pacifism — that would outlast their persecutors, in the Mennonites, Amish, and later the Quakers and Baptists.

Rome's answer

The Catholic Church did not simply lose. The Counter-Reformation (or Catholic Reformation) answered with reform and reaction: the Council of Trent (1545–1563) reaffirmed the disputed doctrines — the authority of tradition alongside scripture, justification by faith and works, the seven sacraments, transubstantiation — while correcting the worst abuses; the new Society of Jesus (the Jesuits) became the spearhead of Catholic education and global mission; and the Roman Inquisition and the Index of Forbidden Books enforced the boundary. Christendom was now permanently divided into confessions.

A hundred years of war

The split was written in blood. The Peace of Augsburg (1555) tried to hold Germany together with the principle cuius regio, eius religio — the ruler's religion is the region's — but the French Wars of Religion, the Dutch Revolt, the English civil wars, and above all the catastrophic Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) turned Europe into a charnel house in the name of doctrine. The Peace of Westphalia that ended it in 1648 left a continent of confessional states and the first stirrings of an idea born of exhaustion: that the state might have to tolerate more than one faith.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The Reformation was, at its core, a revolution in what counted as sacred — and it moved the holy from the image to the word.

  • The supreme sacred object became the printed vernacular Bible. Where medieval faith had been carried in ritual, relic, and image for the illiterate, sola scriptura made the read and preached word the vehicle of grace, and drove a wave of literacy across Protestant lands.
  • Against the image came iconoclasm: Reformed Christians, reading the commandment against graven images strictly, stripped and whitewashed their churches, smashed statues and stained glass (the Beeldenstorm of 1566), and built plain meeting-houses whose bareness was itself a theology — God is met in the Word, not in gilded splendor.
  • The medieval four senses of Scripture (from the scholastic chapter) were largely rejected for the plain, literal sense: the text meant what it said, and every plowman could read it for himself.
  • And a new sacred medium arose in congregational hymn-singing — Luther's own chorales, the metrical psalms of the Reformed — encoding doctrine in music the whole people sang, no longer a choir performing on their behalf.

Connections

The Reformation grew directly out of the medieval Catholic church it broke from, and out of the scholasticism of the previous chapter — its nominalist turn (Ockham) had already loosened the ground, and Luther was trained in it. It drew on the Renaissance humanists' return to original texts (Erasmus's Greek New Testament put the tools in Luther's hands). It runs parallel to the birth of Sikhism in the same century — another vernacular, scripture-and-devotion movement rejecting priestly mediation — and it set loose forces, from mass literacy to individual conscience to religious pluralism, that shape the modern era to come. The old claim, from Max Weber, that its work-ethic birthed capitalism is much debated, but that it helped make the modern world is not.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

Luther's Ninety-Five Theses and the indulgence controversy of 1517; the role of the printing press; the doctrines and texts of Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin; the English and Radical Reformations; the Council of Trent and the Counter-Reformation; and the wars of religion through the Peace of Westphalia (1648). The documentary record is overwhelming.

Not supported (as history)

The theological claims themselves — justification by faith alone, the sole authority of Scripture, predestination, the real presence or its denial — are matters of faith and theology, recorded here as the convictions people fought over, neither proven nor refuted. Whether the reformers or Rome were right is not a question history can answer.

Genuinely open — and legendary

Whether Luther actually nailed the Theses to the church door is doubted by serious historians. The Weber thesis linking Protestantism to the rise of capitalism is contested. And the deeper question of how far the Reformation caused the modern world of literacy, individualism, and eventual secularism — as opposed to riding forces already in motion — remains a genuine historical debate.
