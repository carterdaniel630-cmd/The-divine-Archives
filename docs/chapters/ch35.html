Modern · New Religious Movements

New Religious Movements

In the archive

The explosion of brand-new religions in the last two centuries — from the American frontier prophets to the global movements of today — and the honest work of describing them as religions, neither dismissed as "cults" nor exempted from the evidence-honesty this book applies to every faith.

The golden plates

On a hill in western New York in the 1820s, a young farmer named Joseph Smith said an angel named Moroni had shown him a book written on golden plates, buried in the earth, which he translated by divine power into the Book of Mormon. From that revelation grew the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints — today a global faith of some seventeen million people, with its own scripture, temples, and priesthood. Whether the plates existed, whether the angel came, whether the translation was inspired or invented — these are exactly the questions this book asks of every tradition, and they are no more and no less answerable for a religion two centuries old than for one four thousand years old. That is the point of this chapter: the birth of new religions did not stop with the ancient world. It accelerated.

What a new religious movement is

"New religious movement" (NRM) is the neutral scholarly term for a religion of relatively recent origin — the term that replaced the older, loaded word "cult," which scholars now avoid because it is used mostly as a weapon. An NRM is typically marked by an innovative response to modern conditions, a stance of counterculture against the mainstream, an eclectic blending of older sources, and a charismatic (sometimes authoritarian) founder. There is no sharp line between a "new religious movement" and a "religion": every faith in this book was once new, and Christianity, Islam, and Sikhism were all, in their day, exactly what we would now call NRMs. The category simply lets us look honestly at the ones still within living memory of their founding.

The American century

The modern wave began, above all, in the religious ferment of nineteenth-century America — a free market of faith with no established church. The millenarian expectation of Christ's imminent return produced the Seventh-day Adventists and, from the same current, the Jehovah's Witnesses, both still awaiting the end of the age. Mary Baker Eddy founded Christian Science on the healing power of right belief. The Latter-day Saints built a new scripture and an American Zion. And these were only the largest; the century swarmed with prophets, communes, and revelations.

A global phenomenon

The twentieth century made the movement worldwide and wildly various. The Bahá'í Faith, born in nineteenth-century Persia, taught the unity of all religions and grew into a global community. In East Asia, Falun Gong and Japan's "new religions" drew millions. The counterculture of the 1960s and after produced the Unification Church (the "Moonies"), the Hare Krishna movement (a modern export of the Bhakti tradition), and Scientology, founded by the writer L. Ron Hubbard on his system of "auditing" and the mind. Some were benign, some controversial, a few catastrophic — the mass deaths at Jonestown (1978) and Heaven's Gate (1997) are the shadow side of charismatic authority, and the reason "cult" carries the fear it does.

The "cult" question, honestly

The word cult does real harm and real work, and both must be acknowledged. It has been used to demonize harmless minority faiths, to justify persecution, and to flatten enormous variety into a single sinister image. Yet a small number of these movements have genuinely destroyed lives through coercion, fraud, and violence. The honest position, and the scholarly one, is to judge each movement by its conduct, not by the mere fact of being new or strange — to reserve alarm for actual harm (coercion, abuse, exploitation) rather than for unfamiliar belief. This book describes NRMs as it describes all traditions: taking their claims seriously as religion, while keeping a clear eye on what the evidence shows.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The new religions are, revealingly, religions of the modern world, and encode themselves accordingly.

  • Many produce a new scripture on the model of the old — the Book of Mormon, the Dianetics corpus, the writings of a founder — claiming the authority of revealed text in an age of print and, now, of the internet.
  • They build new sacred symbols deliberately: the Bahá'í nine-pointed star for the unity of the nine great religions; the LDS angel Moroni atop the temple; Scientology's cross and its symbols of the "thetan."
  • And they often encode a secret, staged knowledge — the graded initiations of Scientology's "operating thetan" levels, the temple ordinances of the Latter-day Saints — the ancient pattern of esoteric revelation reborn in the modern corporation and the members-only manual.

Connections

The new religious movements are the direct heirs of everything before them, recombined: the millenarianism of apocalyptic Judaism and Christianity, the esoteric initiation of the mystery cults and Kabbalah, the devotion of Bhakti (Hare Krishna is literally a transplanted Bhakti order), and the do-it-yourself spirituality that Theosophy and Spiritualism — the subjects of the neighboring chapters — set loose in the modern West. They are what religion looks like in an age of print, science, freedom, and doubt: not the end of faith that secular prophets predicted, but its endless, restless remaking.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The historical origins, founders, and growth of the major NRMs (the Latter-day Saints, Adventists, Jehovah's Witnesses, Christian Science, Bahá'í, Scientology, and the rest); their scriptures and organizations; and the documented tragedies (Jonestown, Heaven's Gate) and controversies.

Not supported (as history) — but claimed as faith

The revelatory claims at the root of each — the golden plates and the angel Moroni, the divine inspiration of any founder's scripture, the metaphysics of "thetans" — are matters of faith (or, for critics, of fabrication), recorded here as belief, not adjudicated. That a claim is recent makes it neither more nor less a matter of faith than an ancient one.

Genuinely open — and contested

The very boundary of the category (when is a movement a "sect," a "cult," a "religion"?); the reliability of much that is written about controversial groups, since both hostile "anti-cult" sources and the movements' own literature are interested parties; and the deep sociological question of why the modern, secular, scientific age has produced not fewer religions but more.
