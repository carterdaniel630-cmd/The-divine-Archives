Prehistory · The Paleolithic

The Paleolithic

In the archive

The deepest reach of the human religious imagination — forty thousand years of painted caves, carved goddesses, deliberate graves, and a lion-headed man who never walked the earth — and the discipline of saying honestly, across so vast a silence, what the evidence can and cannot tell us about the first faith.

The lion-headed man

In a cave in the Swabian hills of southern Germany, some forty thousand years ago, someone took a mammoth tusk and spent an estimated four hundred hours carving it into a standing figure with the body of a man and the head of a cave lion. The Lion Man of Hohlenstein-Stadel is among the oldest known works of figurative art in the world — and its subject is a being that has never existed. To make it, its carver had to hold in mind a creature that was not in the world but in the imagination: a fusion of human and animal, a thing of another order. That single object tells us something momentous about its maker's mind — that it could imagine the unreal, the more-than-human, the sacred. It is as close as we can come to watching religion being born.

What we can and cannot know

This is the first chapter in time and the last this book was able to write, and for a reason: the Paleolithic is the era of maximum uncertainty. There are no texts, no myths recorded, no informants — only objects, bones, and painted walls, tens of thousands of years old, from which we try to read a mind. The temptation to over-read is enormous, and the history of the subject is littered with confident interpretations later abandoned. So the honesty that governs this whole book governs this chapter most of all: we possess the artifacts of Paleolithic religion; we do not possess its meaning, and much of what is written about it is inference wearing the costume of fact.

What the evidence is

Three kinds of trace suggest that the people of the Old Stone Age had something we would recognize as religion.

  • Deliberate burial. From at least 100,000 years ago, humans (and possibly Neanderthals) began burying their dead on purpose, sometimes with grave-goods — the extraordinary graves at Sungir in Russia (c. 34,000 years old) held bodies covered in thousands of painstakingly carved ivory beads and staffs. To provision the dead is to imagine that death is not simply the end.
  • Cave art. Deep in the caves of France and Spain — Chauvet (c. 36,000 years old), Lascaux, Altamira — Ice Age people painted horses, lions, bison, and aurochs with astonishing skill, often far inside inaccessible chambers, in the dark, where no one lived. Human figures are rare; hand-prints are common; and a few haunting therianthropes — half-human, half-animal — stare out of the walls.
  • Portable art. Across Europe, the Upper Paleolithic left small carvings, above all the "Venus" figurines — female forms like the Venus of Willendorf and the 40,000-year-old Venus of Hohle Fels — and therianthropes like the Lion Man.

The interpretations, and their limits

What did it mean? Every answer is a hypothesis. The oldest, the Abbé Breuil's idea of sympathetic hunting magic — painting the prey to ensure the hunt — has largely fallen from favor. A more influential modern reading, associated with David Lewis-Williams, sees the caves as the setting for shamanism: the therianthropes as shamans in trance-metamorphosis, the geometric marks as the entoptic patterns the brain generates in altered states, the cave wall itself as a membrane between this world and the world of spirits. Others read totemism, or ritual bound up with the seasons and the herds. The therianthrope may be a sorcerer, a spirit, a god, or a myth we will never recover. Each interpretation is plausible; none is proven, and the caves keep their secret.

The Venus figurines

The female figurines are a case study in honest uncertainty. For a century they were read as "mother goddesses" and emblems of a fertility cult, and that reading still circulates widely. But it is a modern hypothesis, not an established fact. The figurines vary enormously; some may be self-portraits, some idealized forms, some dolls or amulets, some ritual objects — and there is no consensus that they represent a goddess, or a single thing at all. That a naked female figure means "fertility goddess" is an assumption we bring, not a message the Ice Age left.

The Neanderthal question

Did our cousins the Neanderthals have religion? The evidence is real but fiercely contested: apparent deliberate burials (La Chapelle-aux-Saints, and the famous "flower burial" at Shanidar, whose pollen may be natural rather than offered); the use of red ochre and pigment; and the astonishing Bruniquel cave, where, some 176,000 years ago, someone deep underground arranged broken stalagmites into deliberate rings — an act whose purpose is utterly unknown. Whether any of this is "symbolic" or "religious," or something we have no word for, remains genuinely open.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Even here, at the beginning, the human impulse to encode is visible.

  • The therianthrope — the human-animal hybrid of the Lion Man and the painted "sorcerers" — is perhaps the first symbol ever made: the sign of a crossing between the human and the more-than-human, the animal and the spirit, which will reappear in the animal-headed gods of Egypt and the shape-shifting shamans of a hundred later traditions.
  • The hand-print, stenciled in blown ochre across cave walls the world over, is the oldest human signature — a pressed palm that says I was here, and perhaps that the rock face was a surface one could reach through.
  • Red ochre is the deepest thread of all: used to paint bodies and to redden graves from over 100,000 years ago, and engraved with deliberate cross-hatched patterns at Blombos Cave in South Africa some 73,000 years ago — the earliest known abstract mark-making, the first flicker of encoded meaning, long before the cave paintings of Europe.
  • And the cave itself was encoded space: the art clusters, acoustic studies suggest, where the chambers most resonate — the deep, dark, singing places treated as thresholds to another world.

Connections

The Paleolithic is the root system of everything that follows in this book. Its ancestor-honor and spirit-world survive, living, in the African traditions and the diaspora religions of the modern era; its shamanism is the same current that runs through Norse seiðr, Sámi drumming, and Vodou possession; its therianthropes become the gods of Egypt; its red ochre and provisioned graves are the first chapter of the human refusal to accept death as the end — a refusal every later religion inherits. Written last, it stands first: the silent foundation on which the whole archive is built.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The artifacts themselves — the dated cave art (Chauvet, Lascaux, Altamira), the Venus figurines and the Lion Man, the deliberate burials with grave-goods (Sungir), and the very ancient use of red ochre and abstract engraving (Blombos). That Upper Paleolithic humans made symbolic, imaginative art is not in doubt.

Not supported (as established fact)

That we know what any of it meant. The "Mother Goddess" of the Venus figurines, the specific shamanic readings of the cave art, "hunting magic" — these are interpretations, some more plausible than others, none confirmed. Claims that assert the meaning of Paleolithic religion with confidence are claiming more than the evidence allows.

Genuinely open

Almost everything about meaning: what the caves were for, what the figurines represented, what the dead were provisioned for. Whether the Neanderthals had religion at all. And the deepest question — how far back the religious imagination reaches, and whether the first spark was 40,000 years ago, or 100,000, or older still.
