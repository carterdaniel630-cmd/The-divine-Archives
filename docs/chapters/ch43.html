High Medieval · The Aztec

The Aztec

In the archive

The religion of the Mexica of Tenochtitlan — a cosmos five times created and destroyed, a sun that must be fed with human blood to keep it moving, and a pantheon of terrible and subtle gods — told from the temple the Spanish tore down and the codices they could not entirely burn, with the reality of its sacrifices held carefully apart from the propaganda that magnified them.

The stone at the foot of the stair

When the Spanish first climbed the great pyramid at the heart of Tenochtitlan in 1519, they found its twin summit shrines caked with blood, and at the base of one stairway a vast carved disk: the Coyolxauhqui Stone, showing a goddess dismembered — decapitated, her limbs hacked apart. It was not decoration but a diagram of what happened there. In Mexica myth, the moon-goddess Coyolxauhqui had led her four hundred siblings to murder their mother; the war-god Huitzilopochtli sprang fully armed from the womb, slew and dismembered his sister, and flung her down the mountain. When a sacrificial victim's heart was cut out at the top of the pyramid and the body rolled down the steps, it fell onto that stone — the killing re-enacting the myth, the sun's daily victory over the moon and stars. To the Mexica this was not murder but cosmic maintenance: the most sacred and necessary work in the world.

The eagle on the cactus

The Mexica — the people we call the Aztec — were latecomers, a wandering group who, their myth said, left a homeland called Aztlán at the command of their god Huitzilopochtli and searched generations for the sign he promised: an eagle perched on a cactus (devouring a serpent, in the later telling — the image still on the Mexican flag). They found it on a marshy islet in Lake Texcoco, and there, around 1325, founded Tenochtitlan, which within two centuries became the capital of the greatest empire in Mesoamerica — a city of perhaps 200,000 on its lake, larger than any in contemporary Europe, until the Spanish destroyed it in 1521.

The Fifth Sun and the debt of blood

At the center of Mexica religion lay a cosmogony of catastrophe and obligation. The present world was the Fifth Sun; four previous worlds, four "Suns," had each been created and violently destroyed — by jaguars, wind, fire, and flood. To make the fifth sun move at all, the myth said, the gods gathered in darkness at Teotihuacan and one had to sacrifice himself into the fire: the humble, sore-covered Nanahuatzin leapt in and rose as the sun — but the sun hung motionless until all the gods gave their own blood to set it moving. Humanity thus lives on a debt: the gods died to make the sun go, and human beings must repay them with blood — the "precious water," chalchiuhatl — or the sun will stop, and the Fifth Sun, foretold to end in earthquakes, will fall like the four before it. Sacrifice, for the Mexica, was not the appeasement of an angry god but the repayment of a cosmic debt, the maintenance of a universe that would otherwise run down.

The gods

The Mexica pantheon was vast, shifting, and layered with the gods of the peoples they absorbed. Above and behind it all, some priests and poets spoke of Ometeotl, the dual lord-and-lady of duality, the invisible and intangible source ("like the night, like the wind") who had no temple and no image. Below moved the great active gods: Tezcatlipoca, "Smoking Mirror," the capricious, all-seeing lord of fate, night, and sorcery, patron of kings and of the whimsical cruelty of the world; Quetzalcoatl, the Feathered Serpent, god of wind, wisdom, priesthood, and the calendar, the culture-bringer identified with the planet Venus; Huitzilopochtli, the Mexica's own tribal war-and-sun god, to whom the empire's conquests were dedicated; Tlaloc, the ancient, beloved, and terrible rain god, who required the tears (and the lives) of children; Xipe Totec, "Our Lord the Flayed One," god of spring and renewal, whose priests wore the flayed skins of victims as the earth wears new growth; the earth-mother Coatlicue, "Serpent Skirt"; and Mictlantecuhtli, skeletal lord of the underworld.

The Templo Mayor and the sacrifices

At the heart of Tenochtitlan rose the Templo Mayor, a great twin pyramid — the sacred mountain — crowned by two shrines: one to Huitzilopochtli (the sun, war, the dry season), one to Tlaloc (rain, the wet season). Torn down by the Spanish and buried under Mexico City, it was rediscovered in 1978 and excavated to reveal thousands of buried offerings — and, in 2015–2017, the Huei Tzompantli, the great skull rack, a tower and wall built of the skulls of the sacrificed, including women and children, confirming beyond dispute that the sacrifices the Spanish described were real.

But the scale must be told honestly. Spanish and allied chroniclers claimed that 80,400 people were killed in four days at the temple's rededication in 1487 — a figure that is physically impossible (it would require a killing every few seconds around the clock for days) and served the conquerors' need to cast the Mexica as monsters worth destroying. Modern archaeology confirms large-scale, systematic human sacrifice — by heart-extraction with an obsidian blade, and in the flaying, drowning, and burning proper to particular gods — with a real annual toll most scholars now put in the low thousands across the empire. To feed the gods, the empire even fought ritualized "flower wars" (xochiyaoyotl) with neighboring states expressly to take captives. Alongside the killing ran daily autosacrifice: nobles and priests drew their own blood, piercing tongue, ear, and limbs with maguey spines, in the same logic of debt.

Time, the afterlife, and the flower-and-song

The Mexica kept the Mesoamerican calendars — the 260-day sacred tonalpohualli and the 365-day xiuhpohualli — which meshed into a 52-year Calendar Round. At the close of each cycle came the terrifying New Fire Ceremony: all fires were extinguished, the world held its breath at the risk that the sun would not return, and a new fire was kindled on the breast of a sacrificed captive to bind the years for another 52. Where the soul went depended not on virtue but on the manner of death: the drowned and the rain-struck went to Tlalocan, Tlaloc's green paradise; slain warriors and women dead in childbirth became companions of the sun, and after four years, hummingbirds; and the vast majority, dead of ordinary causes, journeyed four years through the nine hells of Mictlan to dissolve at last into nothing.

Yet Mexica religion also had its philosophers and poets. The poet-king of Texcoco, Nezahualcoyotl (1402–1472), wrote verses that reach past the bloody state cult toward the invisible Ometeotl, "the Giver of Life," the unknowable god beyond all images — and meditated, in the tradition of "flower and song" (in xochitl in cuicatl, the Nahua word for poetry and truth), on the fleetingness of life: "Is it true that one lives on the earth? Not forever on earth; only a little while here." Much of what we know of all this survives because a Franciscan friar, Bernardino de Sahagún, spent decades after the conquest compiling, with Nahua elders and scribes, the vast bilingual Florentine Codex — the greatest single record of the Mexica world, written down even as it was being destroyed.

The believer's lens

To the Mexica, theirs was not a religion of gratuitous cruelty but of grave, sustaining responsibility. The sun overhead was not a given but an achievement, purchased by the gods' own self-sacrifice and kept alive, day by day, only by human offering; to let the ritual lapse was to let the world die. The sacrificed were not merely victims but, in the theology, messengers and gods: some, like the youth chosen to embody Tezcatlipoca for a year of honor before his death, became the deity they died for. To die on the stone, or in battle to feed the sun, was the most glorious of ends, opening the warrior's paradise. Autosacrifice bound every noble personally into the cosmic debt; the calendar rounds and the New Fire bound the whole people to the turning of time. And behind the fierce state cult, the poets reached toward a single, invisible Giver of Life and mourned the brevity of existence with a tenderness that complicates every caricature. Seen from inside, the Mexica were a people carrying, with terrible seriousness, the burden of keeping the universe alive.

The skeptical lens

The historian's first task is to distrust the sources — for almost everything comes through the conquerors and their converts. The 80,400-in-four-days figure is conquest propaganda, logistically impossible, and the totalizing image of the Aztec as mere blood-mad butchers was a moral license for genocide and plunder; sacrifice was real and large, but embedded in a coherent theology and not unique to the Americas. Conversely, the romantic counter-image of a peaceful, philosophical Nezahualcoyotl worshipping a single hidden God has itself been questioned: some scholars argue the "Aztec monotheism" of Ometeotl was over-read into the sources by the twentieth-century scholar Miguel León-Portilla, projecting a tidy philosophy onto a messier reality. And one famous story is very likely a post-conquest fabrication: the tale that Moctezuma took Cortés for the returning god Quetzalcoatl appears mainly in later, Spanish-shaped sources and probably did not happen as told — a myth that flattered the conquest. Our richest source, Sahagún's Florentine Codex, is itself a Christian friar's project, filtered through conversion and the questions he chose to ask. The Mexica reach us, like the Maya, largely through the eyes of those who destroyed them.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The Mexica encoded their cosmos in stone, screenfold book, and calendar.

  • The great Sun Stone (Piedra del Sol), a carved basalt disk over three meters across, is a cosmogram of the Five Suns: the face of the sun (or the earth-monster) at its center, ringed by the signs of the four previous world-ages and the twenty day-signs. Popularly (and wrongly) called "the Aztec calendar," it is really an encoded statement of the cyclical, doomed cosmos.
  • The tonalpohualli encoded destiny: each of its 260 days, a combination of twenty signs and thirteen numbers, carried its own fortune, and a specialist read a newborn's day-sign to know its fate — a sacred binary-and-vigesimal cipher of time and character.
  • The pictographic codices (the surviving Borgia group and the post-conquest Florentine and Mendoza) encoded ritual, calendar, tribute, and myth in a sophisticated system of standardized images and glyphs, painted on deerskin and bark-paper screenfolds.
  • The Templo Mayor itself was encoded space: not merely a temple but the sacred mountain Coatepec ("Snake Mountain") where Huitzilopochtli was born and slew Coyolxauhqui, so that the whole pyramid re-enacted the myth, the victims falling as the dismembered goddess fell — architecture as theology in motion.
  • And the Feathered Serpent, Quetzalcoatl, joins earth and sky, matter and spirit, in a single sign shared across Mesoamerica — the Mexica cousin of the Maya Kukulkan (Chapter 29).

Connections

The Aztec are one of the three great temple religions of the pre-Columbian Americas in this book, beside the Maya (Chapter 29) and the Inca (Chapter 44). Within Mesoamerica the Mexica were heirs to a long common heritage — the ballgame, the 260-day count, the Feathered Serpent, the debt of blood — reaching back through the Toltecs to Teotihuacan and the Olmec, and shared much with the Maya. Across the unbridged ocean, their temple-mountains, divine kingship, and cyclical cosmic time rhyme uncannily with the ziggurats of Mesopotamia and the pyramids of Egypt (Chapters 2–3) — an independent invention of the oldest religious forms, reached without a single borrowed idea, and shattered within a generation of the Spanish arrival.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The city of Tenochtitlan and the Templo Mayor (rediscovered 1978); the twin cult of Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc; the Coyolxauhqui Stone and the Huei Tzompantli skull rack; the Fifth Sun cosmogony and the debt-of-blood theology; the pantheon; the calendars and the New Fire Ceremony; the reality of large-scale human sacrifice; and the poetry and philosophy preserved in Sahagún's Florentine Codex and the Nezahualcoyotl tradition.

Not supported — propaganda and myth

The inflated sacrifice numbers of the chroniclers (the 80,400 of 1487 is impossible and served the conquest); the flat caricature of a purely bloodthirsty people; and, very probably, the story that Moctezuma mistook Cortés for Quetzalcoatl, a largely post-conquest construction.

Genuinely open

The precise scale of sacrifice (low thousands a year is the current estimate, but the record is partial); how far the "monotheism" of Ometeotl was a genuine Mexica theology versus a modern scholarly reconstruction (the León-Portilla debate); and how much of the whole picture is distorted by reaching us almost entirely through Spanish and mission-educated sources.
