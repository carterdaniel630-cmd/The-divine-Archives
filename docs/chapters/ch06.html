Early Iron Age · Zoroaster

Zoroaster

In the archive

In a desert temple in Yazd, a flame has been fed by hand and never allowed to die for some fifteen centuries. It is the living icon of a religion that taught the universe is a battlefield of light against dark — and that every human choice helps decide the war.

A fire that has not gone out

In the desert city of Yazd, in central Iran, a flame burns behind glass in a temple, and it has been burning — fed by hand, never allowed to die — since, by tradition, the year 470 CE. Priests approach it with cloth masks so their breath will not defile it. It is an Atash Behram, a “Fire of Victory,” and to those who tend it the fire is not a god but a living icon of the one it points to: the light of truth against the dark.

Its founder was a priest and poet the Iranians called Zarathustra and the Greeks called Zoroaster, and he did something almost no one before him had dared: he looked at the crowded, inherited pantheon of his people — the same Indo-Iranian gods we met among the Vedic peoples — and radically simplified it. He proclaimed one supreme, wholly good creator, Ahura Mazda, and recast existence as a moral drama in which every person must choose a side. The ideas he set in motion — a cosmic war of good and evil, a devil, angels, heaven and hell, a last judgment, a savior, the resurrection of the dead, and the final remaking of the world — would echo, directly or by deep resemblance, through Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

The prophet, and the problem of his date

Almost everything about Zarathustra the man is uncertain, beginning with when he lived. The later tradition placed him around the sixth century BCE, but the archaic language of his own hymns — the Gathas, in Old Avestan, strikingly close to the Rigveda’s Sanskrit — leads most linguists to place him far earlier, around 1000 BCE, give or take a couple of centuries (serious proposals range from 1500 to 600 BCE). Whether he was a single historical individual cannot be strictly proven; what we have is his voice in the Gathas, arguing and praying with his Wise Lord in the first person.

The textual record

The scripture is the Avesta, transmitted orally for centuries and written down remarkably late — only under the Sasanian empire (c. 5th–6th century CE), in an alphabet invented for the purpose. It is layered: the Gathas (Zarathustra’s own hymns, embedded in the Yasna liturgy); the Yasna, the Visperad, and the Yashts (hymns to individual yazatas like Mithra and Anahita, preserving very old myth); and the later Vendidad of purity law. Centuries afterward came a body of Pahlavi (Middle Persian) works — the Bundahishn and the Denkard. That late writing-down matters: our fullest systematic statements of doctrine were recorded after the rise of Judaism, Christianity, and even Islam, though the oral tradition behind them is far older.

Cosmology and the gods

At the summit stands Ahura Mazda, the “Wise Lord” — uncreated, wholly good, the source of light, life, truth, and order. Zarathustra’s reform set him alone at the top and denounced the old daevas (cognate with the Vedic devas) as false gods and demons — the very inversion of vocabulary we traced in the Vedic chapter.

Under Ahura Mazda are the Amesha Spentas, the “Bounteous Immortals,” six great beings (seven with the Lord’s own spirit) who work like archangels but are better understood as aspects of the Wise Lord — Good Mind, Truth, Dominion, Devotion, Wholeness, Immortality — each linked to a good creation (fire, earth, waters, plants, cattle, humankind, sky). Below them are the yazatas, older Indo-Iranian gods rehabilitated as his servants, such as Mithra of the covenant and Anahita of the waters.

Opposed to the good creation stands the hostile spirit Angra Mainyu (later Ahriman) — the principle of the Lie (druj), destruction, and death. The Gathas frame the origin as a primal choice between two spirits, one bounteous and one destructive, and every creature must repeat that choice. Here lies a genuine ancient argument: is Zoroastrianism a monotheism (one supreme God, evil a derivative and defeated rebel — the Gathas’ apparent view) or a dualism of two co-eternal principles (the later systematic view, taken to its extreme in Zurvanite speculation)? The tradition has held both. Crucially, the war is not eternal: time is linear and finite, running to a guaranteed end in which good wins.

Law and the moral order: asha, and the weight of a choice

The organizing principle is asha — truth, rightness, cosmic and moral order — the same inheritance, letter for letter, as the Vedic rta. Its opposite is druj, the Lie. The whole ethical life compresses into a famous triad: Humata, Hukhta, Hvarshta — good thoughts, good words, good deeds. And behind it lies an idea of startling dignity: free will. Because the cosmos is a real war, every human being is a genuine combatant whose choices matter to the outcome — to act rightly is to help Ahura Mazda win. Few religions have given ordinary moral choices so cosmic a stake.

Ritual, fire, and the care of the dead

Worship centers on fire (Atar) — not a god to be adored but the purest earthly symbol of Ahura Mazda’s light, the point toward which prayer is directed. (The old slur “fire-worshippers” misses the theology: the fire is an icon, not an idol.) Sacred fires are graded, from the supreme Atash Behram down. The central liturgy is the Yasna, in which priests prepare and offer haoma — the same sacred plant-drink the Vedic peoples called soma.

The most distinctive practice concerns death. A corpse is nasu — intensely polluting — and must not defile the good creations of earth, fire, or water. So the dead were traditionally neither buried nor burned but exposed on raised stone platforms, the dakhmas or “Towers of Silence,” for the sun and vultures to strip the bones. Membership is marked by the Navjote initiation, investing the child with the sudreh (a white sacred shirt with a “pocket of good deeds”) and the kusti cord. The hereditary priests, the Magi, tended the fires — and it is Magi, in cultural memory, who appear in Matthew’s Gospel as the “wise men” following a star.

The soul’s journey: the Bridge of the Separator

The tower of silence disposes of the body; the soul goes elsewhere. Zoroastrianism worked out one of the earliest detailed maps of what happens after death, and it is relentlessly individual. For three days the soul lingers near the body; at dawn on the fourth it comes to the Chinvat Bridge, the “Bridge of the Separator,” where the divine judges Mithra, Sraosha, and Rashnu weigh its thoughts, words, and deeds on golden scales. There the soul meets the daēnā — the personification of its own conscience, the self it has made. To the righteous she comes as a radiant fifteen-year-old maiden, naming herself as the soul’s own good thoughts and deeds; to the wicked, as a hideous hag. For the good soul the bridge broadens into an easy road to the House of Song (paradise); for the wicked it narrows to the edge of a razor, and the soul falls into the House of the Lie (hell). This heaven and hell are provisional, held until the general resurrection — and the motif of a soul-testing bridge, and of meeting one’s own deeds embodied, would reappear in later traditions, not least the Islamic bridge of aṣ-Ṣirāṭ.

Zoroastrian eschatology sits within a grand, countable timeline, laid out fully in the Pahlavi Bundahishn: the whole cosmic drama runs twelve thousand years, divided into four ages of three thousand — the ideal creation, the making of the material world and its assault by Angra Mainyu, the long “mixture” of history in which we live, and the final downward age. That last age brings the saviors: in the systematized tradition, three Saoshyants born a thousand years apart from the miraculously preserved seed of Zarathustra — Ushedar, Ushedarmah, and finally Astvat-ereta (“he who embodies righteousness”), each born to a virgin who bathes in the lake where the seed is kept. The last raises the dead, creation passes through a final ordeal of molten metal (painless to the righteous), and then comes Frashokereti, the “making wonderful”: evil annihilated for good, the dead restored in perfect immortal bodies, the whole creation healed. The fully worked-out three-savior scheme survives only in the late Pahlavi books, and how much goes back to Zarathustra is an open question.

What the ground shows, and the reach of the tradition

Unlike the textless Indus or the monument-less Vedic world, Zoroastrianism enters history with imperial clarity. The Achaemenid kings — Darius above all — carved their devotion to Ahura Mazda into rock: at Behistun Darius credits the Wise Lord with his victories, and the winged emblem hovers over Persepolis. Whether the early Achaemenids were strictly Zoroastrian or related Mazda-worshippers is debated, but by the Sasanian era it was the state church of a Persian superpower, until the Islamic conquest reduced it to the small living communities of today — the Parsis of India chief among them. Backward, it is the Iranian sibling of Vedic religion (asha/rta, haoma/soma, the daeva/deva inversion); forward, its fingerprints are argued across the western religious tradition.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Fire, first of all. The supreme symbol is the tended flame — the visible sign of asha and of Ahura Mazda’s light, kept pure and, in the highest temples, unextinguished for centuries. It is a symbol precisely because it is not an idol: it points beyond itself.

The Faravahar — and an honest uncertainty. The most recognizable emblem is the Faravahar, a bearded figure rising from a great winged disk. Popular interpretation reads a whole code into it — three rows of wing-feathers as good thoughts, words, and deeds; three tail-rows as their evil opposites; the ring as eternity. That reading is meaningful to living Zoroastrians, but its true origin is debated: scholars dispute whether the figure is the fravashi (a person’s guardian-spirit) or the khvarenah (the “divine glory” that alights on legitimate kings), and the feather-decoding appears to be a modern devotional gloss, not an attested ancient doctrine — a symbol genuinely sacred to believers whose meaning scholars cannot securely read.

The knotted cord. The kusti is encoded doctrine worn on the body: woven of 72 threads (for the 72 chapters of the Yasna), wound three times about the waist (good thoughts, words, deeds) over the sacred shirt, and tied with knots recalling articles of the faith. Untying and retying it with prayer through the day is a small, repeated re-enactment of allegiance to asha.

Number and glory. The sacred seven recurs — Ahura Mazda and the six Amesha Spentas, and the seven good creations — and khvarenah, the luminous divine glory, is itself a visible encoding of legitimacy and grace, imagined as a radiance or a bird of light settling on the worthy.

Connections

The backward connection is to the Vedic world: two branches of one Indo-Iranian religion that ended, through Zarathustra’s reform, as near mirror-images. The forward connection is one of the most debated in the study of religion. In 539 BCE Cyrus the Great freed the exiled Judeans, and for two centuries Judaea lived under Persian, Zoroastrian-inflected rule — and it is in and after this period that ideas scarce in the older Hebrew Bible appear developed in later Jewish, then Christian and Islamic, thought: a personalized Satan; ranked, named angels and demons; a coming savior; heaven and hell; the resurrection of the dead; and a final judgment. Zoroastrian Frashokereti — the making-wonderful, when a savior (the Saoshyant, even held virgin-born) raises the dead and creation is restored perfect — has a strong claim to be the first fully articulated apocalypse.

How much the Abrahamic traditions owe to Zoroastrianism is genuinely open. The parallels are real and the historical opportunity undeniable; but our systematic Zoroastrian texts were written down late, so skeptics argue for independent development or two-way influence, while others hold the older oral tradition shaped Jewish thought during the exile. The honest verdict: striking, important, and unresolved — a thread this archive picks up directly in the Second Temple Judaism and Christianity chapters to come.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • Zoroastrianism is an ancient Iranian religion founded on Zarathustra’s teaching; his own hymns (the Gathas) survive within the Avesta and are linguistically very archaic.
  • Its core: Ahura Mazda as supreme good creator; the Amesha Spentas and yazatas; asha vs. druj; Angra Mainyu/Ahriman; the triad of good thoughts, words, deeds; and a linear history ending in good’s triumph.
  • Fire as central symbol (not idol), the Yasna and haoma, exposure of the dead, and the sudreh/kusti initiation.
  • The individual afterlife — the Chinvat Bridge, the weighing of deeds, and the daēnā met as radiant maiden or hag — is attested from the Avesta; the twelve-thousand-year timeline and three Saoshyants are laid out in the (later) Pahlavi Bundahishn.
  • Its Indo-Iranian kinship with Vedic religion, and its status as the imperial religion of Achaemenid and especially Sasanian Persia.

What’s not supported

  • A confident, precise date (or certain individual historicity) for Zarathustra; estimates span roughly 1500–600 BCE.
  • That Zoroastrian influence on Abrahamic eschatology is a settled, proven fact; the parallels and opportunity are real, but direction, timing, and extent are debated.
  • The popular decoding of the Faravahar as an ancient attested doctrine; even whether it depicts the fravashi or the khvarenah is disputed.
  • The characterization of Zoroastrians as “fire-worshippers.”

What’s genuinely open

  • Whether the system is best described as monotheism (evil a defeated rebel) or a true dualism of two primordial principles — the tradition has held both.
  • The precise nature and extent of Zoroastrian influence on the Abrahamic and dualist traditions.
  • The botanical identity of haoma (the same question as Vedic soma; unresolved).
  • How much of the later Pahlavi systematization reflects Zarathustra’s own teaching versus centuries of development.
