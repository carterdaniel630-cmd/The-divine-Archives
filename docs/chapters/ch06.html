Early Iron Age · Zoroaster

Zoroaster

In the archive

A fire that has not gone out

In the desert city of Yazd, in central Iran, a flame burns behind glass in a temple, and it has been burning — fed by hand, never allowed to die — since, by tradition, the year 470 CE. Priests approach it with cloth masks over nose and mouth so that their breath will not defile it; no stranger may enter its sanctum. It is an Atash Behram, a "Fire of Victory," the highest grade of sacred fire, and to the people who tend it the fire is not a god but a living icon of the one it points to: the light of truth against the dark, order against the lie, in a universe the founder of their religion taught was a battlefield.

That founder was a priest and poet the Iranians called Zarathustra and the Greeks called Zoroaster, and he did something in the Iranian Bronze/early Iron Age that almost no one before him had dared: he looked at the crowded, inherited pantheon of his people — the same Indo-Iranian gods we met among the Vedic peoples in the last chapter — and radically simplified it. He proclaimed one supreme, wise, and wholly good creator-god, Ahura Mazda, and he recast the whole of existence as a moral drama in which every human being must choose a side. It is one of the most consequential single acts of religious imagination in history, because the ideas Zoroaster set in motion — a cosmic war of good and evil, a devil, angels, heaven and hell, a last judgment, a savior, the resurrection of the dead, and the final remaking of the world — would echo, directly or by deep resemblance, through Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, and so through half the religious life of the planet.

The prophet, and the problem of his date

Almost everything about Zarathustra the man is uncertain, beginning with when he lived. This has been, in one scholar's words, "an embarrassment of long standing" to the field. The later Zoroastrian tradition placed him around the sixth century BCE ("258 years before Alexander"), and many older Western scholars accepted that. But the language of his own hymns tells a different story: the Gathas, the seventeen hymns attributed to Zarathustra himself, are composed in an archaic dialect, Old Avestan, so close to the language of the Rigveda that linguists now generally place him far earlier — somewhere around 1000 BCE, give or take a couple of centuries, with serious proposals ranging from 1500 to 600 BCE. Whether he was a single historical individual at all, rather than a tradition's founding figure, cannot be strictly proven. What we have is his voice in the Gathas: a reformer arguing, praying, and wrestling with his Wise Lord in the first person.

The textual record

The scripture of Zoroastrianism is the Avesta, and like the Veda it was composed and transmitted orally for many centuries before being written down — here, remarkably late, only under the Sasanian empire (roughly the 5th–6th centuries CE), in an alphabet invented for the purpose. Much was lost in the intervening upheavals; what survives is layered:

- The Gathas — the oldest core, the hymns of Zarathustra himself, embedded within the Yasna, the liturgy of the principal worship ceremony. Dense, difficult, and intensely personal, they are our one direct window onto the prophet's own thought. - The Yasna (72 chapters, recited during the central ritual), the Visperad (extensions for high feasts), and the Yashts — hymns to individual divine beings (yazatas) such as Mithra and the waters-goddess Anahita, often preserving very old, pre-Zoroastrian mythology. - The Vendidad (Videvdad, "the law against the demons") — a later book of purity rules and mythic material.

These make up the "Younger Avesta," in a later form of the language. Centuries after, a large body of religious literature was written in Pahlavi (Middle Persian): the Bundahishn ("Primal Creation"), which lays out the cosmology and cosmic history, the encyclopedic Denkard, and others. This late date of writing matters enormously for one of the debates below: our fullest systematic statements of Zoroastrian doctrine are recorded after the rise of Judaism, Christianity, and even Islam, even though the oral tradition behind them is far older.

Cosmology and the gods

At the summit stands Ahura Mazda, the "Wise Lord" — uncreated, wholly good, the source of light, life, truth, and order, the maker of the world. Zarathustra's reform pushed the inherited gods down or aside and set Ahura Mazda alone at the top; the old daevas (cognate with the Vedic devas) he denounced as false gods and demons — the very inversion of vocabulary we traced in the Vedic chapter, where the devas are the true gods.

Under Ahura Mazda are the Amesha Spentas, the "Bounteous Immortals" — six great beings (with Ahura Mazda's own spirit, seven) who function much like archangels but are better understood as emanations or aspects of the Wise Lord: Good Mind (Vohu Manah), Truth/Order (Asha Vahishta), Desirable Dominion, Devotion, Wholeness, and Immortality. Each is also linked to one of the good creations (fire, the earth, the waters, plants, cattle, humankind, the sky). Below them are the yazatas, "those worthy of worship" — including older Indo-Iranian gods rehabilitated as Ahura Mazda's servants, such as Mithra, lord of covenant and the later center of his own Roman mystery cult, and Anahita of the waters.

And then the fault line that defines the religion. Opposed to the good creation stands a hostile spirit, Angra Mainyu (later Ahriman) — the principle of the Lie (druj), destruction, and death, with his own swarm of daevas. The Gathas frame the origin as a primal choice: two spirits, one bounteous (Spenta Mainyu) and one destructive, who at the beginning chose, respectively, life and truth or non-life and the Lie — and every creature must repeat that choice. Here lies a genuine and ancient argument, discussed below: is Zoroastrianism a monotheism (one supreme God, with evil a derivative, defeated rebel — the reading many find in the Gathas) or a dualism (two co-eternal principles locked in war — the reading of much later systematic theology, taken to its extreme in the "Zurvanite" speculation that made both twins offspring of Time)? The tradition has held both, at different times.

Crucially, the war is not eternal. Time is linear and finite, running toward a guaranteed end in which good wins — which brings us to the most influential doctrines of all, the eschatology, treated below.

Law and the moral order: asha, and the weight of a choice

The organizing principle of Zoroastrian ethics is asha — truth, rightness, cosmic and moral order — and it is, letter for letter, the same inheritance as the Vedic rta from the last chapter. Its opposite is druj, the Lie. To live in asha is to align oneself with Ahura Mazda and the good creation; to serve the Lie is to side with Ahriman.

The whole of the ethical life compresses into a famous triad: Humata, Hukhta, Hvarshta — good thoughts, good words, good deeds. And behind it lies an idea of startling dignity and consequence: free will. Because the cosmos is a real war, every human being is a genuine combatant whose choices matter to the outcome. To think, speak, and act rightly is not merely to save one's own soul; it is to help Ahura Mazda win. Few religions have given the ordinary moral choices of ordinary people so cosmic a stake.

Ritual, fire, and the care of the dead

Zoroastrian worship centers on fire (Atar) — not as a god to be adored but as the purest earthly symbol of Ahura Mazda's light and truth, the point toward which prayer is directed. (The old slur that Zoroastrians are "fire-worshippers" misses the theology entirely; the fire is an icon, not an idol.) Sacred fires are graded: the supreme Atash Behram, consecrated from many fires in an elaborate ritual; the Adaran; and the humbler Dadgah. The central liturgy is the Yasna, in which priests recite the seventy-two chapters and prepare and offer haoma — the very same sacred plant-drink the Vedic peoples called soma, the shared Indo-Iranian inheritance.

The most distinctive Zoroastrian practice concerns death. A corpse is nasu — intensely polluting, the momentary triumph of the demon of decay — and it must not be allowed to defile the good creations of earth, fire, or water. So the dead were traditionally neither buried nor burned but exposed: laid out on raised stone platforms, the dakhmas or "Towers of Silence," for the sun and the vultures to strip the bones, which were only then gathered away. The practice, still followed in places (though banned in Iran since the 1960s and increasingly difficult elsewhere), is the logical endpoint of a religion for which purity is cosmic allegiance.

Membership is marked by initiation. In the Navjote ceremony a child is invested with the sudreh, a white sacred undershirt with a small "pocket of good deeds," and the kusti, a cord wound three times around the waist and tied with knots — of which more in the symbology below. The hereditary priests, the Magi, tended the fires and the rites; it is Magi, in cultural memory, who appear in the Gospel of Matthew as the "wise men" following a star to Bethlehem.

The soul's journey: the Bridge of the Separator

The tower of silence disposes of the body; the soul goes elsewhere. Zoroastrianism worked out one of the earliest detailed maps of what happens to a person after death, and it is relentlessly individual. For three days the soul lingers near the body; at dawn on the fourth it comes to the Chinvat Bridge, the "Bridge of the Separator," where the divine judges Mithra, Sraosha, and Rashnu weigh its thoughts, words, and deeds on golden scales. There the soul meets the daēnā — the personification of its own conscience, the self it has made in life. To the righteous she comes as a radiant fifteen-year-old maiden, more beautiful than any in the world, naming herself as the soul's own good thoughts and deeds; to the wicked she comes as a hideous hag. For the good soul the bridge broadens into an easy road to the House of Song (paradise); for the wicked it narrows to the edge of a razor, and the soul falls into the House of the Lie (hell). This heaven and hell are provisional, held until the general resurrection — and the motif of a soul-testing bridge, and of meeting one's own deeds embodied, would reappear in later traditions, not least the Islamic bridge of aṣ-Ṣirāṭ.

The shape of time and the three saviors

Zoroastrian eschatology sits within a grand, countable timeline, laid out fully in the Pahlavi Bundahishn: the whole cosmic drama runs twelve thousand years, divided into four ages of three thousand. In the first, creation exists only in an ideal, spiritual state; in the second, Ahura Mazda makes the material world, which Angra Mainyu then assaults, mixing good and evil and setting the war in motion; the third age is the long "mixture" of history in which we live; and the fourth, the final three thousand years, runs down to the end. That last age is marked by the coming of saviors. In the systematized tradition there are three Saoshyants, born a thousand years apart from the miraculously preserved seed of Zarathustra himself — Ushedar, Ushedarmah, and finally Astvat-ereta ("he who embodies righteousness"), the Saoshyant proper — each born to a virgin who bathes in the lake where the seed is kept. The last raises the dead, and all creation passes through a final ordeal of molten metal, painless to the righteous and purifying to all, after which comes Frashokereti, the "making wonderful": evil annihilated for good, the dead restored in perfect immortal bodies, and the whole of creation healed. It has a strong claim to be the first fully articulated vision of a redeemed end of history — though, as the honesty section notes, the fully worked-out three-savior scheme is preserved only in the late Pahlavi books, and how much of it goes back to Zarathustra is exactly the open question.

What the ground shows, and the reach of the tradition

Unlike the textless Indus or the monument-less Vedic world, Zoroastrianism enters the historical record with imperial clarity. The Achaemenid Persian kings — Darius the Great above all — carved their devotion to Ahura Mazda into the living rock: at Behistun, Darius credits Ahura Mazda with his victories in one of the most important royal inscriptions of the ancient world, and the winged emblem hovers over the reliefs at Persepolis. Whether the early Achaemenids were strictly Zoroastrian or simply Mazda-worshippers of a related Iranian religion is debated, but by the Sasanian era Zoroastrianism was the state church of a Persian superpower, and it remained so until the Islamic conquest of the 7th century CE, after which it dwindled to the small but living communities of today — the Parsis of India chief among them.

The comparative and downstream significance is immense. Backward, Zoroastrianism is the Iranian sibling of the Vedic religion: asha and rta, haoma and soma, the daeva/deva inversion all testify to a common Indo-Iranian ancestor split into two mirror-image faiths. Forward, its fingerprints are argued across the western religious tradition — a matter important and contested enough to hold to the end.

The believer's lens

To the Zoroastrian, existence is a cosmic battle between Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord and source of all good, truth, and light, and the hostile spirit Angra Mainyu, the source of the Lie and of evil — and every human being is a combatant, called to choose. This is the tradition's most bracing idea: the world is a real war, the outcome genuinely at stake, and a person's good thoughts, good words, and good deeds materially strengthen the side of asha (truth, order) against druj (the Lie). Fire, kept ever-burning, is the emblem of that truth and of Mazda's presence — not worshipped but faced, as one faces the light. And the story ends well: after the long ages, a final judgment, the resurrection of the dead, and the frashokereti, the "making wonderful," when evil is defeated forever and the world is restored to perfection. To live rightly is to labor, with the Wise Lord, toward that certain victory — a faith of moral seriousness, hope, and the dignity of the human choice.

The skeptical lens

Zoroastrianism poses the historian a severe dating problem: its founder Zarathustra cannot be securely placed, with estimates ranging across more than a millennium (from c. 1500 to c. 600 BCE), and the tradition was transmitted orally for centuries — the Avesta written down, and much of the system (the twelve-thousand-year cosmic timeline, the saviors) fully articulated, only in the much later Pahlavi literature of Sasanian and post-Sasanian times. So the historian must constantly ask how much of "Zoroastrianism" is Zarathustra's and how much is later systematization. The most consequential open question is influence: the striking parallels between Zoroastrian ideas (a cosmic good-evil dualism, angels and demons, a final judgment, bodily resurrection, a coming savior) and later Jewish, Christian, and Islamic beliefs are real, and many scholars think Persian religion shaped Second Temple Judaism (Chapter 10) during and after the exile — but the direction, dating, and extent of that borrowing are genuinely contested, and the evidence is frustratingly thin. What is firm is the Gathas, the fire-cult, the moral dualism of asha and druj, and the tradition's endurance among the Parsis.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Fire, first of all. The supreme Zoroastrian symbol is the tended flame — the visible sign of asha and of Ahura Mazda's light, kept pure and, in the highest temples, unextinguished for centuries. It is a symbol precisely because it is not an idol: it points beyond itself to the invisible truth.

The Faravahar — and an honest uncertainty. The most recognizable Zoroastrian emblem is the Faravahar: a bearded figure rising from a great winged disk. Popular modern interpretation reads a whole ethical code into it — the three rows of wing-feathers as good thoughts, words, and deeds; the three tail-rows as their evil opposites to be risen above; the ring as the soul's eternity or a covenant. That reading is meaningful to living Zoroastrians, but the historian must add: its origins and true meaning are genuinely debated. Scholars dispute whether the figure represents the fravashi (the pre-existent guardian-spirit of a person) or the khvarenah, the "divine glory" or royal radiance that alights on legitimate kings; the tidy "good-thoughts-words-deeds" decoding of the feathers appears to be a modern devotional gloss rather than an attested ancient doctrine. A perfect small case of the rule this project keeps: a symbol can be genuinely sacred to believers and still have a meaning scholars cannot securely read.

The knotted cord. The kusti is a genuine piece of encoded doctrine worn on the body. It is woven of 72 threads — answering to the 72 chapters of the Yasna — wound three times about the waist (for good thoughts, words, and deeds) over the sacred shirt, and tied with a set number of knots recalling core articles of the faith. Untying and retying it, with prayer, several times a day is a small, repeated re-enactment of allegiance to asha: the believer literally girds the creed onto the body.

Number and glory. The sacred seven recurs — Ahura Mazda and the six Amesha Spentas, and the seven good creations they oversee — structuring the cosmos into a countable order. And khvarenah, the luminous divine glory, is itself a kind of visible encoding of legitimacy and grace, imagined as a radiance or a bird of light settling on the worthy.

Connections

The great backward connection, already drawn, is to the Vedic world of the previous chapter: two branches of one Indo-Iranian religion that ended, through Zarathustra's reform, as near mirror-images — the Iranian ahuras good and daevas demonic, the Indian devas good and asuras demonic.

The forward connection is one of the most debated in the study of religion. In the sixth century BCE the Persian empire conquered Babylon, and it was Cyrus the Great who freed the exiled Judeans (539 BCE) and sent them home; for two centuries Judaea lived under Persian, Zoroastrian-inflected rule. And it is precisely in and after this period that ideas scarce or absent in the older Hebrew Bible appear, developed, in later Jewish literature and then in Christianity and Islam: a personalized Satan as a cosmic adversary; ranked, named angels and demons; a coming savior/messiah figure; heaven and hell (and an intermediate state); the resurrection of the dead; and a final judgment and remaking of the world. Zoroastrian Frashokereti — the making-wonderful, when the Saoshyant (a savior tradition even held to be virgin-born) raises the dead, evil is destroyed, and creation is restored perfect — has a strong claim to be the first fully articulated apocalypse in religious history.

How much of this the Abrahamic traditions owe to Zoroastrianism is genuinely open. The parallels are real and widely noted, and the historical opportunity for influence (the Persian period) is undeniable. But the case must be stated with care, because our systematic Zoroastrian texts were written down late — after the very developments they are invoked to explain — so that skeptics can argue for independent development, or even influence in the other direction, while others maintain the oral Zoroastrian tradition long predates the contact and shaped Jewish thought during the exile. The honest verdict is: striking, important, and unresolved. This project will return to it directly in the Second Temple Judaism chapter of the next era, and in the chapters on Christianity, Islam, and the dualist heresies (Manichaeism, Gnosticism) that Zoroastrian ideas most visibly touched.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

  • Zoroastrianism is an ancient Iranian religion founded on the teaching of Zarathustra, whose own hymns (the Gathas) survive within the Avesta and are linguistically very archaic (Old Avestan, close to Rigvedic Sanskrit).
  • Its core: Ahura Mazda as supreme good creator; the Amesha Spentas and yazatas; the opposition of asha (truth/order) and druj (the Lie); Angra Mainyu/Ahriman as the hostile spirit; the ethical triad of good thoughts, words, and deeds; and a linear, finite history ending in the triumph of good.
  • Fire as central sacred symbol (not idol), the Yasna liturgy and haoma, exposure of the dead (dakhmas), and the sudreh/kusti initiation (Navjote) are all well attested.
  • The individual afterlife — the three-day wait, the Chinvat Bridge, the weighing of deeds, the daēnā met as radiant maiden or hag, and the provisional House of Song / House of the Lie — is attested from the Avesta onward. The twelve-thousand-year cosmic timeline and the three Saoshyants born of Zarathustra's preserved seed are laid out in the (later) Pahlavi Bundahishn.
  • Its Indo-Iranian kinship with Vedic religion (asha/rta, haoma/soma, daeva/deva inversion) and its status as the imperial religion of Achaemenid and especially Sasanian Persia.

What's not supported by the evidence

  • A confident, precise date (or even certain individual historicity) for Zarathustra. Estimates span roughly 1500–600 BCE.
  • The claim that Zoroastrian influence on Jewish, Christian, and Islamic eschatology is a settled, proven fact. The parallels and the historical opportunity are real; the direction, timing, and extent are debated, complicated by the late writing-down of the Avesta.
  • The popular decoding of the Faravahar (feathers = good/evil thoughts, words, deeds) as an ancient, attested doctrine; it is a modern devotional reading, and even whether the figure depicts the fravashi or the khvarenah is disputed.
  • The characterization of Zoroastrians as "fire-worshippers."

What's genuinely open

  • Whether the system is best described as monotheism (evil as a derivative, defeated rebel — arguably the Gathas' view) or as a true dualism of two primordial principles (the later systematic and Zurvanite view). The tradition itself has held both.
  • The precise nature and extent of Zoroastrianism's influence on the Abrahamic and dualist traditions.
  • The botanical identity of haoma (the same question as Vedic soma — Ephedra is the leading candidate; unresolved).
  • How much of the later Pahlavi systematization reflects Zarathustra's own teaching versus centuries of development.
