Axial Age · Confucianism & Daoism

Confucianism & Daoism

In the archive

Two answers to the same broken world, born in the same age of collapse: one says mend it by getting the small things exactly right — every bow, every rite, every duty; the other laughs and says the rules are the sickness — stop striving, return to the uncarved block, and do nothing that is left undone.

Two answers to a broken world

Through one window, a middle-aged man out of office teaches a handful of students that to mend a world tearing itself apart you begin with the small things — how you bow, how you mourn a parent, how a ruler addresses a minister. Order is not imposed from above; it is cultivated, one honest person and one correct ceremony at a time. Through the second window, another voice — half legend — laughs at the whole project. Your rules and rites are the symptom, not the cure; the more laws you carve, the more thieves you make. Return to the uncut block, to the valley, to water that conquers stone because it never fights. Do nothing — wu-wei — and nothing is left undone.

These are China’s two great native traditions, born in the same collapse of the Zhou order (Chapter 9) into the Warring States. Confucianism is the tradition of the first man, Kongzi, “Master Kong,” whom the West calls Confucius. Daoism gathered around the second voice — the Daodejing, its supposed author Laozi, and the wild philosopher Zhuangzi. The Chinese came to call the two, with Buddhism, the three teachings (sanjiao) — not rival churches but three registers a single person might move between: Confucian in the office, Daoist in the garden, Buddhist before death.

The world that made them: the Warring States

Neither can be understood apart from the catastrophe that produced them. The Zhou order had ruled by the Mandate of Heaven, its nobles bound in ancestor-worship and reciprocal duty. By the sixth century BCE that order was a memory; real power lay with states that swallowed one another through centuries of war — the Warring States (c. 475–221 BCE). Into the chaos poured the Hundred Schools of Thought: Confucians, Daoists, the Mohists (impartial universal love), the Legalists (harsh law and state power, who would unify China by force), and more. The Axial Age in China is this argument — and Confucianism and Daoism are the two answers that outlived the rest.

Confucius: the man and the Analects

Kongzi (trad. 551–479 BCE) was, by his tradition’s own account, a failure in his own time — a scholar of modest birth in the state of Lu who sought office to enact his ideas, wandered from court to court, and never got the chance. His influence is entirely posthumous, and colossal: for two thousand years the teacher of a civilization. We know his teaching mainly from the Analects (Lunyu), terse sayings opening “The Master said…” But the Analects is not a book Confucius wrote; it is a compilation by disciples over generations, a layered text not stabilized until the Warring States or after. Recently excavated bamboo manuscripts (the Anhui University Zhongni yue and Wangjiazui Kongzi yue) confirm that by c. 300 BCE collections of sayings attributed to Confucius circulated in variant forms — exactly what a layered text should look like. We can recover Confucianism; recovering the literal words of Confucius is harder.

Virtue, ritual, and the cultivated person

Confucius offered little cosmology and few gods — asked about serving the spirits, he said one who cannot yet serve the living should not ask about serving the dead. His concern was this world, and his answer turned on interlocking ideas: ren (仁, humaneness — the master virtue, whose negative form is the first recorded Golden Rule: “do not impose on others what you do not desire”); li (禮, ritual/propriety — not mere ceremony but the whole fabric of correct, considerate conduct, which performed sincerely cultivates the virtue it expresses); xiao (孝, filial piety, the root of all virtue); and junzi (君子, the “exemplary person”) — a word that once meant an aristocrat by birth, which Confucius quietly redefined as nobility by character, attainable by anyone through self-cultivation. To these he added the rectification of names: “let the ruler be a ruler, the father a father” — disorder begins when names and realities part. The political vision is government by moral example, not force: a virtuous ruler draws the people as wind bends grass. This was his direct answer to the Legalists.

The two who completed it: Mencius and Xunzi

Confucianism became a system through two successors who agreed on the goal and split on human nature. Mencius (Mengzi, c. 372–289 BCE) held nature innately good — the “four sprouts” of virtue are native (the spontaneous alarm at seeing a child about to fall into a well proves compassion is inborn); cultivation is gardening. He also sharpened the Mandate of Heaven into a doctrine of righteous rebellion: a tyrant forfeits the Mandate and may justly be overthrown. Xunzi (c. 298–238 BCE) taught the reverse — nature is crooked and self-seeking, and goodness is an achievement produced by ritual and effort, a warped board steamed straight. For Xunzi, Heaven is not a moral agent but impersonal nature; the moral order is wholly human. His naturalism made him, ironically, the teacher of two great Legalists.

Laozi and the Daodejing: the Way that cannot be named

The founding Daoist text is the Daodejing (“Classic of the Way and its Power”), some five thousand characters of paradoxical poetry ascribed to Laozi, said to have ridden west on an ox and written the book at a frontier pass before vanishing. Archaeology has been decisive here: the oldest copies — the Guodian bamboo slips (buried c. 300 BCE, ~40% of the text, in variant clusters) and the Mawangdui silk manuscripts (c. 200 BCE) — show a text that accreted from circulating clusters of sayings, reaching final form only in the late Warring States. Most specialists date its ideas to the fourth–third centuries BCE, and many hold “Laozi” may have been less a person than a tradition. Its teaching inverts the Confucian project: the Dao (道, the nameless source, “the Dao that can be spoken is not the eternal Dao”); de (德, the natural potency of things in accord with the Dao); wu-wei (無為, effortless non-interference — water seeking the low place, wearing away stone); and ziran (自然, self-so spontaneity — the uncarved block, pu, before the knife of distinctions cut nature into pieces). Ritual, it warns, is “the husk of loyalty and faith, and the beginning of disorder”; the best ruler is the one the people barely know is there.

Zhuangzi: the laughing philosopher

If the Daodejing is Daoism’s scripture, the Zhuangzi is its wild literary heart. Master Zhuang (late 4th century BCE) is far more securely historical than Laozi, though scholars credit only the Inner Chapters (the first seven) to him, the rest added later and the whole edited c. 4th century CE by Guo Xiang. He dreams he is a butterfly and wakes unsure whether he is Zhuang who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it is Zhuang — a parable of the relativity of every fixed viewpoint. He mocks the solemn Confucians, converses with skulls, and praises the “useless” tree that grows huge because no carpenter wants it. Big and small, useful and useless, life and death are human distinctions, not features of the Dao — and the sage who sees this moves “free and easy,” greeting even death as one more of the Dao’s transformations.

The great divide: two Daoisms

One of the most misunderstood distinctions in the study of religion: “Daoism” is really two things. Philosophical Daoism (daojia) is the tradition of ideas in the Daodejing and Zhuangzi — the contemplative naturalism most Westerners mean by “Taoism.” Religious Daoism (daojiao) is an organized religion of gods, priests, liturgy, alchemy, exorcism, and the quest for immortality, emerging centuries later — conventionally dated to 142 CE, when the deified Laozi is said to have descended to Zhang Daoling on Mount Heming, founding the Way of the Celestial Masters (Tianshi dao), the first Daoist church with a priesthood, communal confession, and a parish structure. The two are continuous — religious Daoism reveres the Daodejing as scripture — but they are not the same, and collapsing them is a common error.

Ritual, folk religion, and the state cult

Beneath both elite traditions ran the older folk religion of China (Chapter 9), which never went away: veneration of ancestors, offerings at family altars, hungry ghosts, local gods of soil and stove and city wall, diviners and mediums. Confucianism did not replace this; it ritualized and dignified it, making ancestor-reverence and mourning into moral cornerstones. Confucianism also became, over centuries, the state cult — Confucius venerated with sacrifices, temples raised across the empire, and mastery of the Confucian classics made the basis of the imperial examinations that selected officials, binding state to tradition for two millennia. What began as one out-of-work teacher’s counsel became the operating system of a civilization.

The texts: the Classics and the canon

Confucianism organized around the Five Classics (Wujing) — the Books of Documents, Odes, Rites, Changes (Yijing), and the Spring and Autumn Annals — revered as the inheritance of antiquity Confucius was said to have edited (not authored). Later the Four Books (the Analects, the Mencius, and the Great Learning and Doctrine of the Mean) became the core curriculum. Daoism’s founding pair, the Daodejing and Zhuangzi, were later joined by the vast Daozang, the Daoist canon of over a thousand texts.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The Yijing: reality in broken and unbroken lines. Nothing better shows the Chinese conviction that the cosmos is legible than the Yijing, the “Book of Changes,” at once a Confucian classic and a divination manual. Its atoms are two lines — unbroken for yang (active, bright), broken for yin (receptive, dark). Stacked three deep they yield the eight trigrams (bagua); six deep, the sixty-four hexagrams, a complete map of every situation and its tendency to change. One casts lots and builds a hexagram from the bottom up, then reads its judgment. The system encodes the deepest Chinese intuition — that reality is not static substance but ceaseless transformation — into a binary notation so elegant that Leibniz recognized in it his own binary arithmetic. Honesty note: the ascription of the trigrams to the mythic Fuxi and the “Ten Wings” commentaries to Confucius is legend; the commentaries were most likely written in the Han dynasty.

Yin-yang and the five phases. The taijitu — the swirl of dark and light, each half carrying a seed of the other — is a late emblem of an ancient idea: the two principles are not opposed armies but complementary phases of one process. Alongside runs the Five Phases (wuxing: wood, fire, earth, metal, water), a grammar of cyclical generation and conquest linking seasons, directions, colors, and organs in a cosmology of resonance.

Talismans and the uncarved block. In religious Daoism the written character itself became power: fu talismans, esoteric quasi-writing glyphs drawn by priests to command spirits and heal — heir to the ancient sense (Chapter 9) that writing is a sacred, efficacious act. Daoism also encodes its philosophy in humble physical images that are the teaching: the uncarved block, the valley and the female (the power of the low and yielding), the empty vessel (usefulness residing in emptiness), and water (the soft overcoming the hard). To meditate on the image is to grasp the doctrine.

Connections

Both traditions grow directly out of Chapter 9 — the Zhou world, the Mandate of Heaven, ancestor-reverence, and oracle-bone divination are their shared inheritance, and the Yijing is the bridge. Across the Axial Age they stand beside the era’s other awakenings: the Buddha (Chapter 11) and the Upanishadic sages (Chapter 5) turning inward in India, the Greek philosophers (Chapter 8) reasoning toward first principles, and the prophets of Israel (Chapters 7, 10) — Karl Jaspers’s Axial Age. The comparison with Greece is especially rich: both produced rival schools arguing in public over the good life — but where Greek thought prized abstract definition and formal logic, Chinese thought prized concrete example, harmony, and the cultivation of conduct. Forward lie the arrival of Buddhism in China and the three-way conversation of the three teachings, the flowering of religious Daoism, and the great medieval Neo-Confucian synthesis — all later chapters.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • A historical teacher, Confucius (trad. 551–479 BCE), taught in Lu during the Zhou collapse; a tradition of collecting his sayings existed by c. 300 BCE (confirmed by excavated bamboo manuscripts).
  • The core Confucian concepts (ren, li, xiao, junzi, government by example) and the elaborations of Mencius (innate goodness; the right of rebellion) and Xunzi (crooked nature; Heaven as impersonal nature).
  • The Daodejing in partial form by c. 300 BCE (Guodian) and near-complete by c. 200 BCE (Mawangdui); the Zhuangzi’s Inner Chapters from a real 4th-century-BCE thinker; the later emergence of organized religious Daoism (Celestial Masters, trad. 142 CE); the Yijing as a genuine Zhou divination system.

What’s not supported

  • That Confucius wrote the Analects (a later, layered compilation) or personally authored the Five Classics.
  • The Laozi legend as literal biography (the elder contemporary of Confucius, the ox, the frontier pass, single authorship); “Laozi” may be a tradition, not a man.
  • That Confucius wrote the Yijing’s Ten Wings, or that Fuxi devised the trigrams; and conflating philosophical (daojia) with religious Daoism (daojiao).

What’s genuinely open

  • Whether a single historical Laozi existed at all, and how the Daodejing reached final form.
  • How much of the Analects preserves Confucius’s own words versus his school’s development; the authorship of the outer Zhuangzi.
  • The mutual influence of the schools during the Warring States, and how much of “Daoism” is a retrospective category imposed by later (Han) classifiers.
