Late Antiquity · Mahayana Buddhism

Mahayana Buddhism

In the archive

The bodhisattva with a thousand arms

In temples across East Asia stands a figure with a thousand arms fanned out like a peacock's tail, an eye opened in the palm of each hand. This is Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of infinite compassion — known in China as Guanyin, "the one who hears the cries of the world." The thousand arms are not decoration but doctrine: they are the reach of a being who has vowed to save every suffering creature, and who has grown a thousand hands and a thousand eyes because two of each were not enough to answer all the world's pain. Most striking of all, Avalokiteshvara stands at the very threshold of nirvana — the liberation the whole of Buddhism seeks — and refuses to enter it, turning back, again and again, so long as a single being remains trapped in suffering.

That refusal is the beating heart of Mahayana, the "Great Vehicle" — the form of Buddhism that would spread across Central and East Asia to become the faith of China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and Tibet, and today the largest branch of the tradition. Where the earliest Buddhism (Chapter 11) had pointed the individual toward their own liberation, the Mahayana lifted up a new and staggering ideal: not to escape the world alone, but to save it entirely — and it rebuilt the whole of Buddhist thought, cosmology, and devotion around that vow. This chapter is about that transformation: a new ideal, a radical new philosophy of emptiness, a heaven of cosmic Buddhas and saviors, and the great expansion that carried the Dharma to half of Asia.

The Great Vehicle and its new ideal

Mahayana arose within the Buddhist world of India around the first century BCE to the first century CE, not as a breakaway sect but as a new movement of scriptures, ideals, and interpretation growing inside the existing tradition. It named itself the Great Vehicle (Mahayana) — great because, it claimed, it could carry all beings to liberation — and coined a dismissive term, "Hinayana" ("Lesser Vehicle"), for the older, individual-focused path. An honesty note, and a courtesy: "Hinayana" is a polemical, pejorative label, and scholars and Buddhists today avoid applying it to the living Theravada (Chapter 11), which is a venerable tradition in its own right, not a "lesser" anything.

The core of the shift was a new answer to the question: what is the goal? Early Buddhism prized the arhat, the saint who attains nirvana and is freed from rebirth. The Mahayana exalted instead the bodhisattva — a being who, out of boundless compassion (karuna), takes a vow to become a fully awakened Buddha for the sake of all sentient beings, and who postpones their own final liberation to labor, across countless lifetimes, for the rescue of everyone else. The bodhisattva walks the path of the six perfections (paramitas): generosity, ethical discipline, patience, energy, meditation, and — crowning them all — wisdom. In principle this path is open to anyone, layperson or monk. Buddhahood, once the achievement of a rare few, became the destiny of all.

The philosophy of emptiness

If compassion was the new heart of the Mahayana, its new mind was a philosophy of dazzling radicalism: the doctrine of emptiness (shunyata). Set out in the Prajnaparamita ("Perfection of Wisdom") sutras and given rigorous form by the great philosopher Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE), founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school, emptiness pushes the Buddha's own teaching of dependent origination (Chapter 11) to its ultimate conclusion: nothing whatsoever possesses inherent, independent existence (svabhava). Everything that is — a chariot, a self, a mountain, even the Buddha's teachings — exists only in dependence on other things, as a web of conditions, and is therefore "empty" of any fixed essence of its own.

This is not nihilism. Nagarjuna teaches two truths — a conventional truth (things function, and the ordinary world is real enough to live and act in) and an ultimate truth (nothing has independent essence) — and insists that clinging to "emptiness" as if it were itself a solid thing is the worst error of all: even emptiness is empty. The most beloved distillation of this vision is the Heart Sutra's paradox — "form is emptiness, emptiness is form" — while the Diamond Sutra presses the same blade against every concept the mind tries to grasp. A second great school, Yogachara ("Yoga Practice," or "Mind-Only"), developed by the brothers Asanga and Vasubandhu, turned inward, analyzing reality as a transformation of consciousness and mapping the depths of the mind, including a "storehouse consciousness" (alaya-vijnana) that carries the seeds of karma. And a third current taught buddha-nature (tathagatagarbha): that within every being, however deluded, lies the innate seed of Buddhahood, waiting to awaken.

The cosmic Buddha and the celestial saviors

The Mahayana also transformed the Buddha himself. The historical Siddhartha (Chapter 11) came to be seen as but one appearance of a vast, cosmic reality, expressed in the doctrine of the three bodies (trikaya): the Dharmakaya, the ultimate "truth body," the Buddha as the formless absolute itself; the Sambhogakaya, the radiant "bliss body" in which celestial Buddhas appear in their heavens; and the Nirmanakaya, the "manifestation body," the physical form (like Siddhartha) in which a Buddha appears on earth to teach. The Lotus Sutra declares that the Buddha's earthly life and death were a skillful display — that in truth his lifespan is immeasurable, and he has been teaching, and will teach, across unimaginable ages.

And so the spare, non-theistic early tradition blossomed into a vast heaven of Buddhas and bodhisattvas, objects of fervent devotion: Amitabha, Buddha of Infinite Light; Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin), the lord of compassion; Manjushri, bodhisattva of wisdom, with his flaming sword; Maitreya, the Buddha yet to come; Bhaisajyaguru, the Medicine Buddha; and Vairocana, the cosmic sun-Buddha. Where early Buddhism had asked the disciple to rely on no one but themselves, the Mahayana offered a universe full of compassionate helpers.

Pure Land: salvation by grace

The most far-reaching devotional development was the cult of Amitabha and his Pure Land. Amitabha, the sutras teach, vowed as a bodhisattva that any being who called upon him with sincere faith would be reborn after death in his paradise, Sukhavati, the "Land of Bliss" in the west — a realm so free of obstacles that, once there, awakening is assured. This opened a path of salvation by faith and grace, accessible to anyone who could utter the Buddha's name (the practice of nianfo / nembutsu), and it became one of the most popular forms of Buddhism in all of East Asia — a striking parallel, in the same centuries and across the same continent, to the turn toward saving grace we have seen in Christianity and the mystery cults (Chapters 16, 18). Something in the religious mood of Late Antiquity, from Rome to the Ganges to China, was reaching for a compassionate savior.

The goddess of mercy: how Avalokiteshvara became Guanyin

One transformation deserves its own telling, for it is among the most remarkable in the history of religion. The bodhisattva of compassion entered China as Avalokiteshvara, an unambiguously male figure ("the lord who looks down"). Over the following centuries he changed sex. From roughly the tenth century, images of the bodhisattva in China grew steadily more feminine, until by the Song dynasty Guanyin was firmly established as a goddess — the white-robed "Goddess of Mercy," the compassionate mother who hears every cry, grants children to the childless, and rescues the desperate. The Lotus Sutra had opened the door: its twenty-fifth chapter, the "Universal Gate," teaches that Avalokiteshvara manifests in whatever form — man or woman, monk or child, human or god — will best save a given being. Chinese folk devotion filled that opening with the beloved legend of Princess Miaoshan, a king's daughter who chose the religious life against her father's will, endured martyrdom, and was revealed as Guanyin. Today Guanyin is arguably the most widely worshipped deity in all of East Asia — a striking case of a tradition's central compassionate figure crossing not only a cultural border but the boundary of gender itself.

Skillful means: the key that unlocks the door

How could the Mahayana reconcile its new teachings with the old? Through one of its most ingenious doctrines: skillful means (upaya). The Buddha, it holds, is like a wise physician or a loving parent, who teaches each person exactly what they are ready to hear — offering simpler teachings to those who need them and deeper ones to those who can bear them. The Lotus Sutra's famous parable tells of a father luring his children from a burning house by promising each the toy cart they desire, only to give them all a far greater one: the earlier Buddhist paths were the promised carts, true and useful, but provisional — steps toward the one Great Vehicle that carries everyone. Upaya let the Mahayana honor the old teachings while transcending them, and gave it an almost limitless capacity to absorb new practices and adapt to new cultures.

The texts — and an honest word on their origins

The Mahayana produced an enormous new body of sutras, presented as the very words of the Buddha (buddhavacana): the Prajnaparamita corpus (including the Heart and Diamond Sutras), the Lotus Sutra, the Pure Land sutras, the witty and profound Vimalakirti Sutra (in which a layman out-teaches the great monks), the immense Avatamsaka (Flower Garland) Sutra, and the Lankavatara, among many others. Here the evidence-honesty of this archive requires a clear distinction. The tradition reveres these as the Buddha's own teaching; historically, however, they were composed some four centuries after his death, beginning around the first century BCE, by later Buddhists. The tradition itself accounts for the gap with the logic of upaya and with beautiful legends — most famously that the Prajnaparamita sutras were entrusted to the nagas (serpent-beings) in the deep until humanity was ready, and retrieved by Nagarjuna. What a tradition holds to be the Buddha's word and what the historical record dates to a given century are, as always in this work, two different statements, and both are set down here.

The cult of the book

The Mahayana did something new with its scriptures: it made the sutra itself an object of worship. Again and again the Mahayana texts promise vast merit to anyone who copies, recites, memorizes, or even honors them — and several declare that the very place where the sutra is kept or preached becomes a shrine of the Buddha, worthy of the reverence once reserved for his bodily relics. Scholars (notably Gregory Schopen) have argued that this "cult of the book" was a deliberate move to raise text-devotion above the older relic-and-stupa piety, and it had enormous consequences: the copying of sutras became a central act of devotion across Asia — and, in China, a major spur to the invention of printing, so that the world's oldest dated printed book is a copy of the Diamond Sutra (868 CE). In the Mahayana the word of the Buddha was not merely to be read but to be venerated — the teaching become a living relic.

How the Mahayana actually began

The origins of the Mahayana were long misunderstood, and recent scholarship has overturned the old picture — a good example of the field correcting itself. It was once widely taught that the Mahayana was a lay movement, a democratic revolt of ordinary devotees against a monastic elite. The evidence now points the other way: the Mahayana appears to have been, for its first centuries, a minority movement of monks — text-devotees and forest ascetics living within the established monastic communities, following new scriptures and ideals while sharing the same cloisters as everyone else. Far from sweeping India, the Mahayanists remained a minority among Indian Buddhists for a long time; only gradually, and especially as Buddhism spread north and east, did the Great Vehicle come to predominate.

The spread that made it a world religion

For it was outward, along the Silk Road, that the Mahayana found its destiny. Carried by monks and merchants through the oasis kingdoms of Central Asia, it reached China by the first centuries CE, where — after a long, creative encounter with Daoism and Confucianism (Chapter 12) — it flourished and transformed, giving rise to distinctively East Asian schools: the meditation tradition of Chan (which would become Zen in Japan), the devotional Pure Land, the Lotus-centered Tiantai, and the Avatamsaka-based Huayan. From China it passed to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam; and northward it seeded the Buddhism of Tibet, where it fused with the tantric currents that this archive treats in a later chapter on Vajrayana and Tantra. The Great Vehicle became the Buddhism of half of Asia.

The believer's lens

The Mahayana, the "Great Vehicle," recasts the Buddhist path around universal compassion. Its ideal is no longer the arhat who wins his own liberation but the bodhisattva, who vows to postpone final nirvana until all beings are freed — a breathtaking enlargement of the goal from personal release to the salvation of the whole cosmos. Its deepest philosophy is śūnyatā, emptiness: nothing has fixed, independent existence, so that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are not two, and liberation is available here and now to the awakened eye. And it opens a vast, grace-filled cosmos of celestial buddhas and bodhisattvas — Amitābha, whose Pure Land the devotee may reach by faith and the calling of his name; Avalokiteśvara, the embodiment of compassion who hears every cry (and who becomes, in East Asia, the beloved goddess Guanyin) — so that even those who cannot master meditation may be saved by devotion. Through skillful means (upāya), the Buddha meets each being where they are. From inside, the Mahayana is Buddhism grown boundless in compassion and open, at last, to everyone.

The skeptical lens

The historian's central observation is that the Mahayana sutras present themselves as the word of the Buddha, yet were composed centuries after his death — from around the first century BCE onward — which the tradition itself half-acknowledges in its beautiful legends of texts hidden away until the world was ready for them. So the critical scholar reads the Perfection of Wisdom, Lotus, and Pure Land sutras as genuinely new religious literature, not the historical Gautama's teaching, however profound — and treats the cosmic buddhas and Pure Lands as the tradition's developing faith, not recoverable history. Exactly how the Mahayana began — as a lay movement, a forest-monastic reform, a "cult of the book," or several currents at once — is genuinely debated, precisely because its own origin-stories are theological rather than documentary. History can trace the texts, their dates, the "cult of the book" (the sutra venerated as a shrine — the Diamond Sutra of 868 is the world's oldest dated printed book), and the spread that made Mahayana the Buddhism of East Asia and Tibet; the emptiness doctrine and the saving buddhas are recorded as the tradition holds them.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Indra's Net: the universe as infinite mutual reflection. The Mahayana's supreme image of sacred encoding comes from the Avatamsaka Sutra: the Net of Indra, a vast net stretching across the heavens, with a flawless jewel at every knot, and each jewel reflecting every other jewel in the entire net — including all the reflections in all the other jewels, endlessly. It is a breathtaking symbol of the Mahayana's deepest insight — the total interpenetration and interdependence of all things, each phenomenon containing and reflecting all others, nothing existing on its own. To contemplate Indra's Net is to see emptiness and interbeing encoded in a single dazzling picture.

The gesture and the seed-sound. Mahayana devotion developed a rich visual and sonic code. The mudras — the ritual hand-gestures of Buddha images — are an encoded language, each position signifying a teaching or power (the gesture of fearlessness, of teaching, of meditation, of touching the earth). And the tradition began to compress its wisdom into sacred sound: the Heart Sutra ends by distilling the whole Perfection of Wisdom into a mantra — gate gate paragate parasamgate bodhi svaha ("gone, gone, gone beyond, gone utterly beyond — awakening, hail!") — the encoding of a vast teaching into a few syllables of power, a practice that would flower fully in the later Vajrayana.

The lotus and the thousand eyes. The lotus — rising immaculate from the mud, as awakening rises from the world of suffering — became the great Mahayana emblem, the throne of the Buddhas and the title of its most celebrated sutra. And Avalokiteshvara's thousand arms and eyes, with which we began, are themselves an encoded doctrine: compassion made infinite, wisdom (the eyes) and skillful action (the hands) united in a single saving form.

Connections

Mahayana is the child of the early Buddhism of Chapter 11 — it keeps the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, karma, rebirth, and anatta, and radicalizes dependent origination into emptiness — even as it transforms the tradition's ideal, cosmology, and devotion. Its philosophy of no-fixed-self and no-fixed-essence is the great counter-melody to the Upanishadic quest for the eternal Self (Chapter 5). Within its own era, its turn toward a compassionate cosmic savior and salvation by grace rhymes remarkably with the contemporaneous rise of Christianity (Chapter 16) and the mystery cults (Chapter 18) — not by mutual influence, so far as we know, but as a shared movement of the Late-Antique religious spirit across Eurasia. Forward, the Mahayana is the root of most of the Buddhist world still to come in this archive: the Vajrayana and Tantra of Tibet and the Himalayas, Chan/Zen, Pure Land, and the whole vast flowering of East Asian Buddhism.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

- The emergence of the Mahayana as a movement of new scriptures and ideals within Indian Buddhism around the 1st century BCE–1st century CE, centered on the bodhisattva ideal and universal compassion. - The philosophy of emptiness (shunyata), Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE) and the Madhyamaka, the Prajnaparamita sutras, the Yogachara ("Mind-Only") school of Asanga and Vasubandhu, and the doctrines of buddha-nature, the trikaya, and skillful means (upaya). - The rise of devotion to celestial Buddhas and bodhisattvas (Amitabha, Avalokiteshvara, and others) and the Pure Land; and the spread of the Mahayana along the Silk Road to China, Korea, Japan, and beyond, where it became the dominant form of Buddhism. - The revised scholarly understanding that the Mahayana was originally a minority, largely monastic movement, not a mass lay revolt. - The East Asian feminization of Avalokiteshvara into the goddess Guanyin (male in India, established as female by the Song dynasty; the Lotus Sutra's "Universal Gate" and the Miaoshan legend), and the Mahayana "cult of the book" — the sutra venerated as a shrine, with copying as a meritorious act (a spur to printing; the Diamond Sutra of 868 CE the oldest dated printed book).

What's not supported by the evidence

- The traditional claim that the Mahayana sutras are the literal words of the historical Buddha — historically they were composed centuries after his death (the tradition holds them buddhavacana and explains the gap by upaya and legend; this chapter records the belief and dates the texts, and does not confuse the two). - The older textbook picture of the Mahayana as a lay-led democratic movement (now overturned). - The use of "Hinayana" as a fair description of Theravada — it is a polemical term, not a neutral one.

What's genuinely open

- The precise social origins and early institutional forms of the Mahayana (an active field since the work of Schopen, Nattier, and others). - The dating, authorship, and interrelation of the major sutras, composed and edited over many centuries. - The biography of Nagarjuna and other founding figures, around whom much legend gathered. - Whether the Late-Antique parallels between Mahayana devotionalism and Mediterranean savior religions reflect any real contact or are independent responses to a shared age.
