Late Antiquity · Mahayana Buddhism

Mahayana Buddhism

In the archive

Where the earliest Buddhism pointed the individual toward their own liberation, the Great Vehicle lifted up a staggering new ideal: not to escape the world alone, but to save it entirely — and it rebuilt the whole of Buddhist thought, cosmology, and devotion around that vow.

The bodhisattva with a thousand arms

In temples across East Asia stands a figure with a thousand arms fanned out, an eye opened in each palm: Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion — in China Guanyin, “the one who hears the cries of the world.” The thousand arms are doctrine: the reach of a being who has vowed to save every suffering creature, grown because two hands were not enough for all the world’s pain. Most striking of all, Avalokiteshvara stands at the threshold of nirvana and refuses to enter it, turning back so long as a single being remains in suffering. That refusal is the heart of Mahayana, the Great Vehicle — the Buddhism of China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and Tibet, today the largest branch of the tradition.

The Great Vehicle and its new ideal

Mahayana arose within Indian Buddhism around the 1st century BCE to 1st century CE, a movement of new scriptures and ideals growing inside the existing tradition. It named itself the Great Vehicle — great because it could carry all beings to liberation — and coined a dismissive term, “Hinayana” (“Lesser Vehicle”), for the older path. An honesty note: “Hinayana” is a pejorative label, and scholars and Buddhists today avoid applying it to the living Theravada (Chapter 11). The core shift was a new goal: where early Buddhism prized the arhat who attains nirvana, the Mahayana exalted the bodhisattva — who, out of boundless compassion (karuna), vows to become a Buddha for the sake of all beings and postpones their own liberation to labor across countless lives for everyone else, walking the path of the six perfections (paramitas). Buddhahood, once the achievement of a rare few, became the destiny of all.

The philosophy of emptiness

The new mind of the Mahayana was a philosophy of dazzling radicalism: emptiness (shunyata). Set out in the Prajnaparamita (“Perfection of Wisdom”) sutras and given rigorous form by Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE), founder of the Madhyamaka (“Middle Way”), it pushes dependent origination to its conclusion: nothing possesses inherent, independent existence (svabhava) — everything exists only in dependence on other things, “empty” of fixed essence. This is not nihilism: Nagarjuna teaches two truths (conventional and ultimate) and insists that clinging to emptiness as a thing is the worst error — even emptiness is empty. The Heart Sutra distills it: “form is emptiness, emptiness is form.” A second school, Yogachara (“Mind-Only,” Asanga and Vasubandhu), analyzed reality as consciousness, mapping a “storehouse consciousness” (alaya-vijnana); and a third current taught buddha-nature (tathagatagarbha) — the innate seed of Buddhahood in every being.

The cosmic Buddha and the celestial saviors

The Mahayana transformed the Buddha himself. The historical Siddhartha became one appearance of a cosmic reality, expressed in the three bodies (trikaya): the Dharmakaya (the formless absolute), the Sambhogakaya (the radiant “bliss body” of celestial Buddhas), and the Nirmanakaya (the earthly manifestation, like Siddhartha). The Lotus Sutra declares the Buddha’s lifespan immeasurable. And the spare early tradition blossomed into a heaven of Buddhas and bodhisattvas and fervent devotion: Amitabha (Infinite Light), Avalokiteshvara (compassion), Manjushri (wisdom), Maitreya (the future Buddha), Bhaisajyaguru (the Medicine Buddha), and Vairocana (the cosmic sun-Buddha) — a universe full of compassionate helpers.

Pure Land, and skillful means

The most far-reaching devotion was the cult of Amitabha and his Pure Land: he vowed that any being who called on him with sincere faith would be reborn in his paradise, Sukhavati, where awakening is assured — a path of salvation by faith and grace open to anyone who could utter his name, and one of the most popular forms of Buddhism in East Asia. It rhymes strikingly, in the same centuries, with the turn toward saving grace in Christianity and the mystery cults (Chapters 16, 18). And how did the Mahayana reconcile the new with the old? Through skillful means (upaya): the Buddha teaches each person exactly what they are ready to hear. The Lotus Sutra’s parable of a father luring his children from a burning house with promised carts makes the earlier paths true but provisional — steps toward the one Great Vehicle that carries everyone.

The texts — and an honest word on their origins

The Mahayana produced an enormous new body of sutras, presented as the Buddha’s own words (buddhavacana): the Prajnaparamita corpus (Heart and Diamond Sutras), the Lotus Sutra, the Pure Land sutras, the Vimalakirti (in which a layman out-teaches the monks), the immense Avatamsaka, and more. Honesty requires a clear distinction: the tradition reveres these as the Buddha’s teaching, but historically they were composed some four centuries after his death, from around the 1st century BCE. The tradition accounts for the gap by upaya and by legend — famously that the Prajnaparamita was entrusted to the nagas until humanity was ready and retrieved by Nagarjuna. What a tradition holds to be the Buddha’s word and what the record dates to a given century are two different statements, both set down here.

How the Mahayana actually began, and how it spread

Recent scholarship has overturned the old picture. It was once taught that the Mahayana was a lay movement, a democratic revolt against a monastic elite; the evidence now points the other way — it was, for its first centuries, a minority movement of monks, text-devotees and forest ascetics living within the established communities, and a minority in India for a long time. Its destiny lay outward, along the Silk Road: carried through Central Asia to China by the early centuries CE, where — after a creative encounter with Daoism (Chapter 12) — it gave rise to Chan (later Zen), Pure Land, Tiantai, and Huayan, then passed to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, and northward seeded the Buddhism of Tibet. The Great Vehicle became the Buddhism of half of Asia.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Indra’s Net: the universe as infinite mutual reflection. The Mahayana’s supreme image comes from the Avatamsaka Sutra: the Net of Indra, stretching across the heavens with a flawless jewel at every knot, each jewel reflecting every other jewel — including all their reflections, endlessly. It is a breathtaking symbol of the deepest Mahayana insight: the total interpenetration and interdependence of all things, each phenomenon containing and reflecting all others, nothing existing on its own.

The gesture and the seed-sound. The mudras — the ritual hand-gestures of Buddha images — are an encoded language, each signifying a teaching or power. And the tradition compressed its wisdom into sacred sound: the Heart Sutra ends by distilling the whole Perfection of Wisdom into a mantra — gate gate paragate parasamgate bodhi svaha (“gone, gone, gone beyond… awakening, hail!”) — a vast teaching encoded in a few syllables, a practice that would flower fully in the later Vajrayana.

The lotus and the thousand eyes. The lotus — rising immaculate from the mud, as awakening rises from suffering — became the great Mahayana emblem and the title of its most celebrated sutra; and Avalokiteshvara’s thousand arms and eyes are themselves encoded doctrine: wisdom (the eyes) and skillful action (the hands) united as infinite compassion.

Connections

Mahayana is the child of the early Buddhism of Chapter 11 — keeping the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, karma, rebirth, and anatta, and radicalizing dependent origination into emptiness — even as it transforms the tradition’s ideal and cosmology. Its no-fixed-essence philosophy is the counter-melody to the Upanishadic quest for the eternal Self (Chapter 5). Within its era, its turn toward a compassionate cosmic savior and grace rhymes with the rise of Christianity (Chapter 16) and the mystery cults (Chapter 18) — not by known influence, but as a shared movement of the Late-Antique spirit across Eurasia. Forward, the Mahayana is the root of most of the Buddhist world still to come: the Vajrayana and Tantra of Tibet, Chan/Zen, Pure Land, and the whole flowering of East Asian Buddhism.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The emergence of the Mahayana within Indian Buddhism around the 1st century BCE–1st century CE, centered on the bodhisattva ideal and universal compassion.
  • The philosophy of emptiness, Nagarjuna and the Madhyamaka, the Prajnaparamita sutras, Yogachara, and the doctrines of buddha-nature, the trikaya, and skillful means.
  • Devotion to celestial Buddhas and bodhisattvas and the Pure Land; the Silk Road spread to East Asia; and the revised understanding that the Mahayana was originally a minority, largely monastic movement.

What’s not supported

  • The traditional claim that the Mahayana sutras are the literal words of the historical Buddha — historically they were composed centuries after his death (the tradition holds them buddhavacana; this chapter records the belief and dates the texts, without confusing the two).
  • The older textbook picture of the Mahayana as a lay-led democratic movement (now overturned).
  • The use of “Hinayana” as a fair description of Theravada — it is polemical, not neutral.

What’s genuinely open

  • The precise social origins and early institutional forms of the Mahayana.
  • The dating, authorship, and interrelation of the major sutras, and the biography of Nagarjuna and other founders.
  • Whether the Late-Antique parallels between Mahayana devotion and Mediterranean savior religions reflect any real contact or are independent responses to a shared age.
