Early Medieval · Islam

Islam

In the archive

From a cave above Mecca came a faith that within a single century stretched from Spain to the Indus, and that today is the religion of roughly a quarter of humanity. Told with the historical record and the tradition’s own sacred understanding both set down, each kept distinct.

“Recite!”

The tradition holds that it began in a cave. A merchant of Mecca, about forty, would retreat to a cave on Mount Hira to pray — and there, one night in Ramadan, a presence overwhelmed him: the angel Gabriel, who seized him and commanded, “Recite!” (Iqra!). Three times the command came, until words were pressed out of him — the first, Muslims believe, of God’s final revelation: “Recite, in the name of your Lord who created… who taught by the pen, taught man what he did not know.” The man was Muhammad, and the recitation would, over twenty-two years, become the Qur’an — the word means “the recitation.”

A note on faith and history

As with early Christianity, the method must be plain. History can examine whether a man named Muhammad preached in seventh-century Arabia, what the movement did, and how its scripture took form; it cannot confirm or deny that the Qur’an is the literal speech of God, that Gabriel spoke in the cave, or that Muhammad was God’s prophet. Those are claims of faith, held by over a billion people, marked here as what they are — neither asserted as proven nor dismissed. One thing sets Islam apart in this archive: it arose in the full light of recorded history, so its historical outline is unusually secure even where its sacred claims lie, as always, beyond history’s reach.

Before the Prophet: the gods of Arabia

Islam defined itself against what came before it: a rich, local, many-godded religion of sacred stones, groves, and shrines, the greatest of them the Kaaba at Mecca, a sanctuary and pilgrimage site long before Islam. Its chief idol was Hubal; three goddesses were honored across the Hijaz — al-Lat (“the Goddess”), al-‘Uzza (“the Mighty,” with a shrine at Nakhla), and Manat (goddess of fate). The Qur’an itself names the three (53:19–20) and rejects the notion that they were daughters of Allah. The Meccans also knew a high god, Allah, acknowledged as creator while lesser powers were worshipped beside him — which is why the Qur’an’s demand was not “believe in God” but “believe in God alone.” Ritual included pilgrimage, circumambulation, sacrifice, divination by arrows before Hubal, and the rhymed oracles of inspired soothsayers (kahins); the tradition reports that the Quraysh went into battle at Uhud (625) crying the names of al-‘Uzza and Hubal.

A sourcing note: much of our detail comes from Muslim writers looking back — above all Ibn al-Kalbi’s Book of Idols (early 9th century) — but not all of it: al-Lat is independently attested centuries earlier in Nabataean and Palmyrene inscriptions, and Herodotus already names an Arabian goddess “Alilat.” The tradition also tells of hanifs, Meccan seekers such as Zayd ibn ‘Amr who rejected the idols for the faith of Abraham before Muhammad’s call; whether a real pre-Islamic hanif movement existed is contested, many scholars reading the reports as a later projection of Qur’anic ideas.

One episode tied to the goddesses is famously controversial: the so-called “Satanic verses.” Early historians (among them Ibn Sa’d and al-Tabari) report that Muhammad once recited lines conceding the intercession of the three — “the exalted cranes” — which were then withdrawn as an insertion of Satan. Later Muslim scholarship came overwhelmingly to reject the story, as mainstream Muslim belief does today. Among historians it remains open: W. Montgomery Watt judged it likely genuine because no believer would invent so awkward a report; others point to weak chains of transmission; Shahab Ahmed’s Before Orthodoxy (2017) argued it was widely accepted in the earliest generations. The archive records the report, its rejection, and the debate, and settles none of them.

The prophet and his message

Muhammad (c. 570–632 CE) belonged to the Quraysh, the tribe governing Mecca, a caravan city built around a cube-shaped shrine, the Kaaba, then a center of Arabian polytheism. Around 610 he began preaching a startling message: that there is only one God (Allah, “the God”), the creator and judge of all, the same worshipped by Jews and Christians; that idolatry is the gravest error; and that the wealthy owe justice to the poor, the orphan, and the widow. In 622 — the pivotal year — he and his followers left for Medina: this migration, the Hijra, begins the Islamic calendar. At Medina he became prophet, lawgiver, judge, and statesman — a fusion of the religious and political that marks Islam ever after — and by his death in 632 had united much of Arabia. An honesty note: the detailed biography (the sira) comes from sources written generations later (Ibn Ishaq c. 750, via Ibn Hisham c. 830), read with due caution, even as the outline stands firm.

The Book

At the center of Islam stands the Qur’an — for Muslims, the literal, uncreated word of God, revealed through Gabriel over twenty-two years in Arabic. Not a narrative like the Gospels but a book of recitations, 114 chapters (suras), meant to be heard and recited, its Arabic held to be of miraculous, inimitable beauty (the doctrine of i’jaz, its inimitability, taken as proof of divine origin). The tradition holds the text was gathered into a single codex under the caliph Uthman (c. 650), variant copies destroyed. Remarkably, the material evidence tends to support an early codification: the oldest manuscripts — the Birmingham folios and the lower text of the Sana’a palimpsest — are strikingly early, though Sana’a preserves genuine variant readings. A revisionist school (Wansbrough; Crone and Cook) once argued for a much later crystallization; the early manuscripts have tempered the most radical versions, while scholarly debate on the details continues.

The oneness of God, and the six beliefs

The heart of Islam is tawhid — the absolute oneness of God: one, without partner, equal, or offspring, beyond all image; to associate anything with God (shirk) is the one unforgivable sin. This shapes everything — the rejection of images of God, the discomfort with the Trinity and Christ’s divinity, the vision of a single sovereign Lord. Sunni tradition frames six articles of belief: God, his angels, his revealed Books (the Torah and Gospel seen as earlier, partly corrupted revelations), his prophets, the Last Day, and divine decree. The prophets run in one line — Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus — all bringing the same message, with Muhammad the last, the “Seal of the Prophets.”

The Five Pillars

Practice rests on the Five Pillars: Shahada, the testimony — “There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the messenger of God” (to say it sincerely is to become a Muslim); Salat, ritual prayer five times daily facing Mecca; Zakat, obligatory almsgiving; Sawm, the fast of Ramadan; and Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca, where millions circle the Kaaba as one. The word Islam means “submission” to God; a Muslim is “one who submits.”

The children of Abraham

Islam understands itself as the restoration of the original monotheism — the faith of Abraham (Ibrahim), who submitted to the one God before Jew or Christian existed — and honors Jews and Christians as “People of the Book” (ahl al-kitab). Jesus (Isa) is deeply revered: born of the Virgin Mary (Maryam, the only woman named in the Qur’an), a mighty prophet and the Messiah, a worker of miracles — but, in the Islamic understanding, not divine and not the son of God, and (in the Qur’an’s much-discussed account) not truly crucified, but taken up by God. The three Abrahamic faiths share a family of figures while dividing sharply on their meaning.

The unseen: jinn, the evil eye, and lived religion

Between God and humankind the Qur’an places a third order of rational beings, the jinn: humans were made of clay, the jinn “from a smokeless flame of fire” (55:15). They are not mere demons — they have free will, can believe or disbelieve, and will be judged; a whole sura (72, al-Jinn) tells of jinn who overheard the Qur’an and believed, and Iblis is said to be “of the jinn” (18:50). Islam took up a figure of pre-Islamic belief — the desert spirits who inspired poets and soothsayers — and folded it into a monotheist cosmos.

Here official and everyday religion meet. Ordinary Muslims guarded themselves against the jinn, against sorcery, and against the evil eye (‘ayn), which a widely transmitted hadith declares “real.” The approved remedy is ruqya, protection by Qur’anic recitation, above all the “two refuges” (suras 113–114), which seek refuge “from the evil of the envier when he envies.” Folk practice went further — written amulets of verses and divine names, protective hand and eye charms, later the visiting of holy men’s tombs — and learned opinion has always been divided, some jurists permitting Qur’anic amulets, others condemning any amulet as a lapse toward shirk. What scholars codified and what people did were never quite the same thing. (The cult of saints belongs chiefly to Sufism, in its own chapter.)

Sunna, hadith, and the law

Second to the Qur’an is the Sunna — the example of Muhammad — transmitted in hadith, reports each ideally carrying a chain of transmitters. As reports multiplied, scholars built a science of authenticating them, assembling in the ninth century the great collections (al-Bukhari, Muslim). From the Qur’an and Sunna, jurists derived the Sharia — the “path,” the comprehensive divine law — through fiqh (jurisprudence), and several schools of law (madhhabs: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi’i, Hanbali) crystallized. Islam became, like Rabbinic Judaism (Chapter 19), a profoundly legal and textual civilization.

The great schism: Sunni and Shia

The wound that never healed opened at Muhammad’s death in 632, over one question: who should lead? Most — the future Sunnis — accepted a succession of chosen caliphs, beginning with the “Rightly Guided” Rashidun. But a party held that leadership belonged to the family of the Prophet, to his cousin and son-in-law Ali and his descendants: the Shia (“party”) of Ali, who regard his line as divinely guided Imams. The division was sealed in blood in 680 at Karbala, where Ali’s son Husayn, the Prophet’s grandson, was killed — a martyrdom mourned every year at Ashura and the emotional heart of Shia devotion. Both share the Qur’an, the Pillars, and the core of the faith, but diverge in authority, law, and sacred history.

The first sects: “Judgment belongs to God alone”

Sunni and Shia were not the only answers to the first civil war. At Siffin (657), Mu’awiya’s soldiers are said to have raised pages of the Qur’an on their spears and called for arbitration; when Ali agreed, a body of his own followers rejected human arbitration of what God had decided, cried la hukm illa li-llah — “judgment belongs to God alone” — and walked out. These were the Kharijites (“those who went out”): leadership belonged to the most pious Muslim whatever his birth, and a grave sinner ceased to be a Muslim at all. Ali crushed them at Nahrawan (658); in 661 the Kharijite Ibn Muljam struck him down with a poisoned sword in the mosque of Kufa. Militant factions fought the Umayyads into the 690s. One moderate current survives: the Ibadis, the majority tradition of Oman, with communities in North and East Africa — who themselves reject the label “Kharijite,” a distinction the archive follows.

A second argument reached into the nature of the Book itself. The Mu’tazila, theologians of reason, divine justice, and human free will, argued that God’s oneness required the Qur’an to be created, not co-eternal with God. The caliph al-Ma’mun adopted the doctrine (827) and in 833 imposed it by an inquisition, the mihna. Its famous resister, Ahmad ibn Hanbal (founder of the Hanbali school), refused despite imprisonment and flogging; under al-Mutawakkil (c. 848–851) the mihna was abandoned. The uncreated Qur’an became Sunni orthodoxy (later systematized by al-Ash’ari, d. 936), and the scholars, not the caliph, emerged as guardians of doctrine. The “uncreated word” stated at this chapter’s opening was itself the outcome of a documented historical struggle — a point of history, not a judgment on the doctrine.

The conquests and the caliphate

Islam’s expansion is one of history’s astonishing facts. Within a century of Muhammad’s death, Arab-Muslim armies had overthrown the Persian Sasanian empire (Chapter 6) and stripped Byzantium of its southern provinces, carrying Islam from Spain to the borders of India and China. The Rashidun (632–661) gave way to the Umayyad dynasty (Damascus) and the Abbasid (Baghdad), under whom the Islamic world entered a long golden age — translating and extending Greek philosophy and science (Chapter 15), advancing mathematics, medicine, and astronomy. Islam was never only a religion; from Medina onward it was a community, a law, and a state.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The word made visible: calligraphy and the flight from images. Islam’s most distinctive encoding flows from tawhid: because God is beyond likeness and images risk idolatry, sacred art turned from depicting God or living beings and poured its genius into the written word of God itself. Arabic calligraphy became the supreme Islamic art — Qur’anic verses across mosque walls and pages, at once scripture, ornament, and object of contemplation. The sacred text is the sacred image.

Geometry as a window on the infinite. Islamic geometric patterns and vegetal arabesques — interlacing stars and polygons repeating endlessly — are widely read as an encoded contemplation of the divine: from a single unit, an infinite unfolding without center or idol, pointing to the oneness and infinity of God. Number in the letters: the abjad assigns numerical values to the 28 Arabic letters (sibling of Hebrew gematria and Greek isopsephy), used in chronograms and esoteric numerology.

The mystery at the head of the suras. Twenty-nine suras open with the muqatta’at, “disconnected letters” recited singly — Alif Lam Mim, Ya Sin — whose meaning is unknown, an authentic encoded mystery at the threshold of the Book. Stone and gold: the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed in 72 AH (691–92) by the caliph ‘Abd al-Malik, raises an octagonal shrine — a form borrowed from Byzantine martyria — over the rock of the Temple Mount, ringed by a mosaic band of Qur’anic text proclaiming God’s oneness (sura 112) and addressing Christians directly: “the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, was only a messenger of God and His word… do not say ‘Three’” (cf. 4:171) — among the earliest dated Islamic religious inscriptions. (Al-Ma’mun later substituted his own name but left the original date, betraying the forgery; why the Dome was built remains debated.) In 77 AH (696–97) the same caliph’s coinage reform replaced Byzantine- and Persian-style coins with purely aniconic dinars bearing only words — the profession of faith, sura 112, the date: money itself became a vehicle of tawhid. The house and the direction: the Kaaba and its Black Stone are the symbolic center — the direction (qibla) every Muslim faces, so the world’s prostrations orient like spokes toward one point. A symbol honesty note: the crescent moon and star is not an original emblem of Islam; it was adopted widely only under the Ottoman Empire, a millennium after Muhammad — as with the Jewish Star of David (Chapter 19), a tradition’s best-known modern symbol can be a late arrival.

Connections

Islam is the third of the great Abrahamic faiths, sibling of Judaism (Chapters 10, 19) and Christianity (Chapter 16): it shares their one God, their prophets, and much of their sacred story, read through tawhid and the finality of Muhammad. It arose amid the Byzantine Christian and Zoroastrian Persian (Chapter 6) worlds, and became heir to Greek philosophy and science (Chapter 15) in its golden age; its legalism parallels Rabbinic Judaism (Chapter 19). Forward, Islam fills much of this archive to come: the flowering of Sufism (its own High-Medieval chapter), Islamic philosophy, theology, and science, the branching of Shia Islam, and the spread of the faith across the world.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The historical core: a prophet Muhammad preached uncompromising monotheism in early-7th-century Mecca and Medina, led a community, and united much of Arabia by 632; the Hijra of 622; and the explosive conquests and caliphate — all in the full light of recorded history.
  • The early codification of the Qur’an (traditionally under Uthman, c. 650), substantially supported by very early manuscripts (Birmingham, Sana’a), even as Sana’a preserves real variant readings.
  • The core structures — tawhid, the Five Pillars, the six articles, the development of hadith, Sharia, and the schools of law, and the Sunni–Shia split sealed at Karbala (680).
  • Pre-Islamic Arabian polytheism — Hubal, al-Lat, al-‘Uzza, Manat, the Kaaba as pre-Islamic sanctuary — named in the Qur’an (53:19–20), described by later Muslim sources, and independently attested for al-Lat in Nabataean and Palmyrene inscriptions and in Herodotus.
  • The first sects: the Kharijite secession after Siffin (657), Ali’s assassination by Ibn Muljam (661), the surviving Ibadis; the Mu’tazila and al-Ma’mun’s mihna (from 833), Ibn Hanbal’s resistance, and its abandonment under al-Mutawakkil.
  • The Dome of the Rock (691–92) and its anti-Trinitarian inscriptions, and the aniconic coinage reform (696–97); the Qur’anic doctrine of the jinn and the long-documented folk practice of protection against jinn and the evil eye.

What’s not supported (as history)

  • The theological claims — the Qur’an as the literal, uncreated speech of God; Gabriel’s revelation; Muhammad’s prophethood — are matters of faith, recorded here as belief, neither proven nor disproven.
  • The detailed traditional biography (sira) and much hadith as straightforward eyewitness fact — compiled generations later, read critically.
  • The crescent-and-star as an ancient symbol of Islam (a late, Ottoman-era adoption).
  • The existence of jinn, or the efficacy of the evil eye or of amulets, as established fact — documented beliefs and practices, not claims history or science can confirm.
  • That ‘Abd al-Malik built the Dome to replace the hajj to Mecca — a single later report, widely doubted.

What’s genuinely open

  • Aspects of the earliest history of the community and the precise process by which the Qur’an reached its canonical form — the traditionalist account, the tempered revisionist critiques, and the manuscript evidence.
  • The historical reliability of specific episodes in the sira and specific hadith.
  • The original meaning of the Qur’an’s disconnected letters (muqatta’at) — unknown to scholarship and much of the tradition alike.
  • The historicity of the “Satanic verses” episode — reported early (Ibn Sa’d, al-Tabari), rejected by later Muslim scholarship and mainstream belief, still debated by historians (Watt; Shahab Ahmed).
  • Whether a real pre-Islamic hanif movement existed; and the original purpose and audience of the Dome of the Rock.
