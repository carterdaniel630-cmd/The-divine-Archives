Early Iron Age · Early Greece

Early Greece

In the archive

When Linear B was deciphered in 1952, the Bronze Age accounts turned out to name Zeus, Poseidon, Athena — even Dionysus. The Greek gods, it emerged, were already old when Homer was young.

Zeus, written in the Bronze Age

In 1952 the architect Michael Ventris deciphered Linear B, the script on clay tablets baked hard in the fires that destroyed the Mycenaean palaces around 1200 BCE. The tablets were not poetry but accounts — inventories of grain, oil, and sheep. Yet scattered through the Bronze Age bookkeeping were names that made the hair stand up: di-we (Zeus), po-se-da-o (Poseidon), a-ta-na (Athena), e-ra (Hera), e-ma-a (Hermes) — and, astonishingly, di-wo-nu-so (Dionysus), a god later Greeks thought had arrived late from the East. They record offerings to them: grain, honey, oil, wine.

The lesson reframes everything that follows: the Greek gods are far older than the Greeks who wrote the Iliad. Zeus and his family were receiving offerings five centuries before Homer, and — as we saw in the Vedic chapter — Zeus’s very name (Dyeus) reaches back to the Indo-European sky-father he shares with the Vedic Dyaus and the Roman Jupiter. This chapter covers early Greece — the Mycenaean roots, the Dark Age, and the Archaic crystallization in Homer and Hesiod — the world before the philosophers and tragedians of classical Athens, who belong to the next era.

A religion with no bible

The first thing to unlearn: Greek religion had no scripture, no creed, no commandments, no church, and no professional clergy in our sense. What mattered was not right belief (orthodoxy) but right practice (orthopraxy) — performing the sacrifices, festivals, and rites correctly, for the community’s safety and the gods’ goodwill. Religion was woven into the polis, the city-state, and into the household.

Yet two poets came as close to “scripture” as the Greeks got. Herodotus said Homer and Hesiod “gave the gods their genealogies, names, honors, and forms.” Homer gave the Iliad and Odyssey; Hesiod the Theogony (a genealogy of the gods and Zeus’s rise) and the Works and Days. An educational caution — the Homeric Question: “Homer” may not have been a single author. The epics grew from a centuries-long oral tradition; Milman Parry and Albert Lord showed they are built from formulaic phrases (“swift-footed Achilles,” “the wine-dark sea”) that let a bard compose in performance. The Iliad we read is that tradition crystallized into writing, not obviously one man’s work.

Cosmology: how Zeus came to power

Hesiod’s Theogony begins with Chaos (a “gap”), then Gaia (Earth), Tartarus, and Eros. Earth bore Ouranos (Sky) and bore him the Titans — until the Titan Kronos castrated his father Sky with a sickle (from the bloody foam was born Aphrodite). Kronos then swallowed his own children to forestall a prophecy — until Rhea hid the infant Zeus, who freed his siblings and, in the ten-year Titanomachy, overthrew the Titans and became king of the Olympians.

This “succession myth” — Sky, then Kronos, then Zeus, with the castration of the sky-god at its center — is one of the clearest cases of Near Eastern influence on Greek religion, closely paralleling the Hurrian-Hittite Kumarbi cycle (Anu castrated by Kumarbi, then overthrown by the storm-god Teshub) and the Babylonian Enuma Elish of Chapter 3. Greek religion is thus a weave of at least three threads: an Indo-European core (Zeus/Dyeus), a Near Eastern overlay (the succession myth; Aphrodite, who owes much to Ishtar/Astarte), and an older Aegean/Minoan substrate.

The result was the family of Olympians — traditionally twelve: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes, and Dionysus (or Hestia). They are anthropomorphic to an extreme — immortal and mighty, but jealous, lustful, and vengeful, morally not much better than the humans they rule. Above even the gods stood Moira, Fate.

Law and the moral order

Greece had no divine law-code. Its morality worked by other logics. There was no “sin” against a holy God in the Hebrew sense; instead: reciprocity (do ut des, “I give so that you may give”); honor and pollution (offense as an affront to a god’s honor, or as miasma, ritual pollution needing purification); the unwritten laws — above all xenia, guest-friendship protected by Zeus Xenios, and the sanctity of the oath; and hubris and nemesis — violent excess provoking divine retribution. Hesiod’s Works and Days preaches that Zeus watches over Dike (Justice).

Ritual: the smoke of sacrifice

The central act was animal sacrifice (thysia), and Hesiod gives its charter myth: at Mekone the trickster Prometheus fooled Zeus into choosing the ox’s bones wrapped in fat — leaving the meat for humans. Ever since, Greeks burned the bones and fat for the gods (who feed on the fragrant smoke) and ate the meat themselves — a shared feast, meat being rare in the Greek diet. (Prometheus paid: he stole fire for humanity and was chained to a rock, and Zeus sent the first woman, Pandora.) Scholars distinguish Olympian sacrifice (burnt offerings to the sky-gods, followed by a shared meal) from chthonic rites for the underworld, the dead, and heroes (poured into the ground, not shared).

The temple was not a place of congregation but the house of the god, sheltering the cult statue; people gathered outside, at the altar. Two Panhellenic institutions knit the city-states together: the oracles — above all Delphi, where Apollo’s priestess the Pythia gave famously ambiguous answers beside the omphalos (“navel of the world”) inscribed know thyself and nothing in excess, and Zeus’s oracle at Dodona, read in a rustling oak — and the great sanctuaries at Olympia (the Games from 776 BCE) and Delphi.

The dead, the heroes, and the promise of the mysteries

On the ordinary fate of the dead, early Greece was as bleak as Mesopotamia: the shades went to the realm of Hades, a dim, strengthless existence — the Odyssey’s Achilles says he would rather be a living day-laborer than king of all the dead. There was no general reward or punishment: a few sinners suffered in Tartarus, a few heroes reached Elysium, but for most, death was a gray diminishment. Two responses in this period promised better, and matter enormously later:

The Eleusinian Mysteries, centered on Demeter and her daughter Persephone (carried to the underworld, sought by her grieving mother, returned for part of each year), gave initiates — sworn to secrecy on pain of death — the promise of a better lot in the afterlife. What was revealed we still do not know. And Orphism, attributed to the singer Orpheus, taught that humans are born from the ashes of the Titans who devoured the child-god Dionysus — and so carry both a Titanic and a divine nature. Orphics held to the transmigration of souls, practiced purity and vegetarianism to escape the cycle, and were buried with inscribed gold tablets giving the soul passwords for the underworld. Here, for the first time in Greek thought, the soul matters more than the body — ideas that feed directly into Pythagoras and Plato in the next era.

Below Olympus: the household, the heroes, and the curse tablet

The great civic festivals and Panhellenic oracles were only the visible top of Greek religion; most of it happened at home and in the ground. Domestic worship was constant and small. At the center of every house burned the hearth of Hestia, who received the first libation of any offering; the storeroom held Zeus Ktesios, “Zeus of the property,” embodied not in a statue but in a kadiskos — a small two-handled jar of water, oil, and fruit wound with wool — that guarded the family’s stores. Zeus Herkeios protected the courtyard, Apollo Agyieus the street door, and at the threshold and crossroads stood Hekate. The ordinary Greek met the divine far more often at these household shrines than at any grand temple.

Then there were the heroes — not gods but powerful dead, great figures of the mythic past (Heracles, Theseus, a city’s founder) whose bones lay in a known tomb and who could bless or blight the community around them. Hero-cult grew from family tomb-visiting into a civic institution in this very period, the eighth century BCE: a wrathful hero might have to be appeased, a friendly one courted as a local protector. The hero was bound to a place — his grave — in a way the sky-gods never were.

And in the darker corner of that chthonic world lay the curse tablet (katadesmos, Latin defixio). To bind an enemy — a rival in love, a business competitor, an opponent in a lawsuit, a racing chariot-team — a Greek would scratch the victim’s name and a binding formula onto a thin sheet of lead, fold or pierce it, and deposit it in a grave, a well, or a sanctuary, so that the dead and the gods below would “pin down” the target. Some sixteen hundred survive, hundreds from Athens alone — folk religion at its most anxious and most literal.

What the ground shows

The Mycenaean palace civilization (with its Linear B gods) collapsed around 1200 BCE; a Dark Age of reduced population and lost literacy followed; then, in the eighth century BCE, came a renaissance — the alphabet, monumental temple-building, the Olympic Games (776 BCE), colonization, and the writing-down of Homer and Hesiod, amid an Orientalizing flow of Near Eastern art and myth. How much religious continuity bridged the Dark Age gap is genuinely uncertain: the gods’ names carried across, but much else may have been remade.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The alphabet that could write breath. The Greeks took the Phoenician alphabet (Chapter 7) around 800 BCE and made one world-changing change: they repurposed unused consonant-signs to write vowels, producing the first fully phonetic alphabet, able to capture the exact sound of speech, song, and meter — making Homer, lyric, drama, and philosophy recordable. Honesty note: Greek isopsephy (letter-number values, the Greek analog of gematria) is a later, mostly Hellenistic development, not early Greek.

Divine attributes as a visual code. Gods are identified in art by a consistent language: Zeus’s thunderbolt and eagle, Athena’s owl and aegis, Poseidon’s trident, Apollo’s lyre and bow, Hermes’s caduceus and winged sandals, Dionysus’s ivy and thyrsus. To read a Greek vase is to read this code.

Reading the will of the gods. Beyond the oracles, the Greeks practiced a full repertoire of divination: inspecting entrails (as in Mesopotamia), reading the flight of birds (augury), and interpreting dreams, thunder, and chance words. The gods signaled constantly; the art was in the reading.

Pillars and passwords. The herms — plain pillars topped with Hermes’s head — stood at doorways and crossroads as apotropaic guardians, a stark aniconic god-sign. And the Orphic gold tablets are the ancient world’s purest encoded sacred text: tiny inscribed leaves buried with the dead, telling the soul what to say below — “I am a child of Earth and starry Heaven, but my race is of Heaven alone” — a literal password for eternity.

Connections

Early Greek religion is a crossroads: its succession myth ties it to the Near East (Kumarbi; the Enuma Elish of Chapter 3), its sky-father to the Indo-European world (Chapters 5–6), its alphabet and love-goddess to the Levant/Phoenicia of Chapter 7, and its flood (Deucalion) to the comparative theme of Chapter 1. Herodotus even claimed the Greeks got their gods from Egypt — an overstatement, but a witness to eastern debts. Forward, all of this seeds the next era’s classical Greece: the myths the tragedians will interrogate, the Orphic ideas of the soul that Pythagoras and Plato will philosophize, and a pantheon that Rome will adopt wholesale.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The major Greek gods were already worshipped in Mycenaean Bronze Age Greece, attested by name in Linear B (c. 1400–1200 BCE).
  • Greek religion was non-dogmatic and non-scriptural — a matter of practice (orthopraxy), civic and household ritual, sacrifice, festivals, and oracles — with Homer and Hesiod as the shared reference for myth.
  • Strong Near Eastern influence on Greek myth, clearest in the succession myth’s parallels with the Kumarbi cycle and Mesopotamian cosmogony.
  • The Greek alphabet was adapted from Phoenician with the innovation of vowels; the grim Hades afterlife coexisted with mystery cults (Eleusis, Orphism) offering initiates a better fate.
  • Below the civic cult ran a rich everyday religion: household worship (Hestia’s hearth, Zeus Ktesios in the kadiskos, Hekate at the crossroads), the civic hero-cults of the powerful dead at their tombs, and the inscribed lead curse tablets (katadesmoi/defixiones) — some 1,600 known.

What’s not supported

  • “Homer” as a single historical author who composed and wrote the epics alone; the oral-formulaic tradition makes this at best uncertain.
  • A single, unified, systematic “Greek religion” or theology; practice was local and various, and Hesiod’s genealogy is one poet’s synthesis.
  • Reading later classical philosophy, developed Orphic doctrine, or Herodotus’s claim of wholesale Egyptian origin back onto the earliest layers.

What’s genuinely open

  • The Homeric Question — the authorship, unity, and composition of the Iliad and Odyssey.
  • How much religious continuity bridged the Dark Age between Mycenaean and Archaic Greece.
  • The origins of specific deities (how much of Dionysus or Aphrodite is native, Near Eastern, or Aegean).
  • The actual content of the Eleusinian secret and the earliest form of Orphism; and the historical kernel, if any, behind the myths (the Trojan War; the Deucalion flood).
