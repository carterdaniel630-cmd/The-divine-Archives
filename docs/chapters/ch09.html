Early Iron Age · Early China

Early China

In the archive

In 1899 a scholar noticed ancient writing on the “dragon bones” sold as medicine in Beijing. They were the fortune-telling tools of the Shang kings — and the oldest Chinese writing, letting us listen in on a Bronze Age court asking its ancestors for advice.

Dragon bones in the medicine shop

In 1899 the scholar Wang Yirong, ill with malaria, was sent a prescription that included “dragon bones” ground up as medicine. Looking closely, he noticed the bones were covered in strange, archaic writing — a script older than any then known. They were traced to fields near Anyang, in Henan, where farmers had been digging up “dragon bones” for years. They were the discarded tools of the royal fortune-tellers of the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE), and they carried the oldest known Chinese writing, from around 1250 BCE.

These oracle bones are not myths but working documents of divination — the direct record of the Shang kings asking their gods and ancestors questions and reading the answers. Through them we hear a religion built on two pillars that would shape China for three thousand years: the worship of ancestors, and the idea that Heaven itself judges the conduct of kings.

How writing was born from divination

The method was pyromancy: a diviner applied a hot point to a hollow in an ox shoulder-blade or turtle shell, made it crack, and read the pattern as the answer of the spirits. A scribe then inscribed the question (“Will it rain?” “Is the ancestor Father Yi sending this toothache?”), sometimes the king’s reading, occasionally the outcome. A striking fact: Chinese writing was born in a divinatory setting — not, as in Mesopotamia, from accounting, nor, as in Egypt, for monuments. The character for “to divine,” bu (卜), is a picture of a crack, and its sound imitates the snap. And unlike hieroglyphs or cuneiform, the oracle-bone script never died: it is the direct ancestor of the characters written by over a billion people today — the only writing system in continuous use for over three millennia.

The Shang cosmos: the Lord on High and the ancestors

At the summit stood Di, or Shangdi — “the Lord on High” — a remote, powerful high god commanding rain, harvest, and victory. But the crucial peculiarity: Di was not worshipped directly, and ordinary people had no access to him. He could be approached only through the royal ancestors, the dead kings of the Shang line. This is the heart of early Chinese religion: ancestor veneration. The dead kings lived on as powerful spirits who could intercede with Di — or, if neglected, inflict illness and disaster. Only the king (with his diviners) could reach the ancestors and, through them, Di, so religious and political authority were one and the same. Around them stood lesser nature powers — the Yellow River, the sacred mountains, the winds of the four directions.

Honesty note: the Shang oracle bones contain no creation myth. The famous stories of Pangu (the cosmic giant) and Nüwa (who shapes humans from clay) are attested only much later and cannot be projected back onto the Shang.

Ritual, the bronzes, and the sacrificed dead

Shang worship was lavish and, by our standards, terrible. Offerings of grain, millet-wine, and animals went to the ancestors — and so, on a large scale, did human beings. The royal tombs at Anyang held sacrificial victims and companion burials (attendants who died with the king, as at Ur in Chapter 3); the intact tomb of the warrior-queen Fu Hao (found 1976) held hundreds of bronzes and sixteen sacrificed humans. The supreme sacred objects were the ritual bronze vessels — cast not to decorate but to feed the dead at ancestral banquets. Their famous decoration is the taotie, a frontal monster-mask of eyes and jaws belonging to no identifiable animal, whose meaning is genuinely unknown — guardian, ward, shamanic vision, or convention; honesty requires leaving it open.

The wu, and the question of shamanism

Beside the king and his diviners stood another kind of religious specialist: the wu (巫), spirit-mediums attested from the oracle bones onward and associated with communicating with spirits, healing, exorcism, and above all rain — in a drought a wu might be exposed to the sun or even burned in a rite meant to force the spirits to send water. How to classify them is one of the liveliest debates in the field. K. C. Chang argued influentially that early Chinese kingship was itself shamanic — the king as paramount shaman, the whole apparatus of bronzes, divination, and animal imagery serving ecstatic communion with the spirit world. David Keightley and others pushed back, reading the Shang theocrats less as ecstatic soul-flight shamans on the Siberian model than as bureaucratic mediators managing an ancestral hierarchy through orderly ritual; a middle position notes that the Chinese evidence more often describes spirit-possession than the classic shaman’s soul-journey. The clearest literary window is later and southern — the Nine Songs (Jiu Ge) of the state of Chu, ecstatic hymns in which a medium woos and is entered by a god. That this dimension is real is not in doubt; whether “shaman” is the right word for it is what scholars dispute.

The Zhou revolution: the Mandate of Heaven

Around 1046 BCE the Shang were conquered by the Zhou, and with them came one of the most consequential religious ideas in history — invented partly to solve a political problem: if the Shang ruled because they alone communed with Di, how could the Zhou justify overthrowing them?

They elevated Tian — “Heaven” — as the supreme power, partly merging it with the older Di but transforming it: Tian was less a personal deity than an impersonal, moral, all-seeing cosmic order that cares about justice. And they proclaimed the Mandate of Heaven (Tianming): Heaven grants the right to rule to a dynasty so long as its kings govern with virtue (de) — and withdraws it when they become corrupt. The last Shang king, they argued, had grown so tyrannical that Heaven stripped him of the Mandate and gave it to the virtuous Zhou; the king became the Son of Heaven (Tianzi). The doctrine’s brilliance is that it is conditional and revocable: it ties every ruler’s legitimacy to his conduct, and reads misrule, disaster, and rebellion as signs that Heaven has turned away. For three thousand years every Chinese dynasty — and every rebellion — was justified in these terms.

Law and the moral order

Early China, like Greece, had no divine law-code. Its ordering principles became the backbone of all later Chinese ethics: li (ritual and proper conduct, whose correct performance keeps society and cosmos in harmony); xiao (filial piety, the root virtue of a religion centered on the family dead); and de (the moral power that secures the Mandate). Heaven is a moral auditor, watching the ruler’s virtue and answering with order or calamity. This fusion of cosmology and ethics is early China’s distinctive contribution — and it set the stage for Confucius, who in the next era looked back to this Zhou ideal of li and virtue as the cure for a collapsing age.

The textual record

Our sources are of two kinds: the contemporary inscriptions — Shang oracle-bone script (jiaguwen) and Zhou bronze inscriptions (jinwen) — which are primary, unfiltered evidence; and the transmitted classics (the Book of Documents, the Odes, the Rites, and the divination manual the Yijing), compiled over later centuries. These preserve Zhou material but must be read critically — they often present an idealized order, and their dates are debated.

What the ground shows

The Shang capital at Anyang yielded the oracle bones, the royal tombs, and the bronze foundries. But early China was not monolithic. The site of Sanxingdui, in Sichuan, produced (from around 1200 BCE) astonishing bronze heads and masks with jutting eyes and a bronze “spirit tree” — an entirely distinct ritual tradition, contemporary with the Shang but unmentioned in any text, with no oracle bones. It is a standing reminder that the “early China” of the texts is only the part that wrote — other, now-silent religious worlds flourished alongside it.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The sacred character. Chinese writing was born in divination and never lost that aura: the written character was revered for millennia (paper bearing writing was not casually discarded), and the continuity of the script — oracle-bone graph to modern character — made the written word a sacred inheritance.

Reading Heaven’s cracks. Shang divination was itself a decoding system: the heat-made cracks were treated as a script written by the spirits, which the trained diviner learned to read — the central technology of Shang statecraft.

The Book of Changes. Out of Zhou divination grew the Yijing: a set of 64 hexagrams, each of six lines that are broken (— —) or unbroken (—), generated by casting yarrow stalks — in effect a binary map of sixty-four archetypal situations of change. Honesty note: the divinatory core (the Zhouyi) is early Zhou, but the systematic doctrine of yin and yang and the philosophical commentaries were added much later and should not be projected back.

Round Heaven, square Earth. The Chinese worked sacred meaning in jade from Neolithic times. The round disc (bi) and the square-and-round tube (cong) are, in later ritual texts, associated with Heaven and Earth — encoding the cosmology of a round heaven over a square earth. (The neat Heaven/Earth reading comes largely from later texts, and the Neolithic jades’ original meaning is uncertain — but the sacredness of jade is beyond doubt.)

The center and the four directions. The Shang conceived their world as a cross: the king and capital at the sacred center, surrounded by the four quarters and their winds — placing the ruler at the pivot between Heaven and the human world.

Connections

Early China is the great study in contrast for this era. Developing in relative isolation, it produced a religion strikingly unlike the others here: no creator god, no anthropomorphic pantheon rich in myth, and no separate priesthood — instead an impersonal moral Heaven, a spirit-world of ancestors, and a king who was priest, diviner, and Son of Heaven at once. Where Israel (Chapter 7) moved toward one transcendent God and Greece (Chapter 8) toward a family of vivid Olympians, China built its sacred order around the family dead and the moral scrutiny of Heaven. Forward, all of it flows into the next era’s Axial Age: Confucius will make the Zhou ideal of li and virtue his teaching; Daoism will draw on the older nature-cosmology and the way (Dao) of Heaven; and the Yijing’s lines will be philosophized into the full yin-yang system. The Mandate of Heaven will outlast every dynasty that invoked it.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • Shang religion is directly documented by the oracle-bone inscriptions (from c. 1250 BCE): pyromantic divination addressed to the high god Di/Shangdi and above all to the royal ancestors, with the king as sole intermediary.
  • Ancestor veneration was the core; human sacrifice and companion burial are attested (Anyang tombs; Fu Hao); ritual bronze vessels were made to feed the dead.
  • The Zhou introduced Tian and the Mandate of Heaven — a conditional, virtue-based, revocable theory of rule — foundational to Chinese thought.
  • Chinese writing originated in divination and is continuous with the modern script; the Yijing has genuine early-Zhou divinatory roots.

What’s not supported

  • Reading later systematized cosmology into the Shang: developed yin-yang and the Five Phases, philosophical Daoism, and the Pangu/Nüwa creation myths are all later.
  • A single, unified “early Chinese religion”: Sanxingdui shows a wholly distinct contemporary ritual culture; early China was plural.
  • The legendary Xia dynasty and sage-kings as established literal history.

What’s genuinely open

  • The precise nature of Di — a distinct high god, or the supreme collective ancestor of the Shang line?
  • Whether early Chinese religion and Shang kingship are rightly called shamanic (K. C. Chang’s shaman-king thesis vs. Keightley’s bureaucratic mediators; possession vs. soul-flight).
  • The meaning of the taotie motif.
  • The original meaning of the Neolithic ritual jades (the Heaven/Earth reading is largely from later texts).
  • The dates and editorial history of the transmitted classics, and how far their idealized Zhou reflects reality.
