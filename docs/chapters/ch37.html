Modern · Theosophy & the Occult Revival

Theosophy & the Occult Revival

In the archive

The late-nineteenth-century rebirth of Western esotericism — a claimed ancient wisdom behind all religions, hidden Masters in the Himalayas, a magical order that fused Kabbalah and tarot into a system of the soul, and a self-styled prophet who called himself the Beast — and the honest reckoning with its fabrications, its enormous influence on the modern world, and the dark uses to which some of its ideas were later put.

The letters from the Masters

In the 1870s and 1880s, a stout, chain-smoking Russian émigré named Helena Petrovna Blavatsky became the center of a sensation. Letters, she said, arrived from her hidden teachers — the Mahatmas or Masters, adepts of a secret brotherhood dwelling beyond the Himalayas — and these letters did not come by post: they materialized, dropping from the ceiling, appearing inside sealed rooms. Through them, Blavatsky claimed to transmit the Secret Doctrine, the single ancient wisdom lying behind every religion the world had ever known. In 1875, in New York, she and Colonel Henry Steel Olcott founded the Theosophical Society to spread it, later moving its headquarters to Adyar, in India.

In 1885 the Society for Psychical Research sent the young investigator Richard Hodgson to India; his report concluded that the Mahatma letters were written by Blavatsky herself, passed through a sliding panel behind a "shrine" cabinet, and branded her "one of the most accomplished, ingenious, and interesting impostors in history." (The SPR formally distanced itself from that verdict a century later, in 1986, judging Hodgson's own method flawed — but no positive proof of the Masters ever emerged.) Both halves of the story are true, and both matter: Theosophy was built on claims that do not survive scrutiny, and it became, all the same, one of the most influential religious currents of the modern West.

What Theosophy is

Theosophy — "divine wisdom" — is the system Blavatsky set out in her two vast books, Isis Unveiled (1877) and The Secret Doctrine (1888): a grand synthesis of Eastern religion and Western occultism, claiming that all faiths are corrupted fragments of one original, secret wisdom-tradition preserved by hidden adepts. From the East it took karma and reincarnation, cosmic cycles, and spiritual evolution; from the West, Hermeticism, Neoplatonism, and Kabbalah. More than any other single movement, Theosophy introduced Hindu and Buddhist ideas to the Western public, gave the English language much of its spiritual vocabulary, and set the template for everything later called "spiritual but not religious."

The doctrine of the Secret Doctrine

Theosophy taught a colossal cosmic evolution. The universe and humanity develop through immense cycles of "rounds" and seven "root races," each rising and falling, some on now-lost continents — Lemuria and Atlantis. Behind the visible world lay hierarchies of planes and beings, crowned by the Masters, the "Great White Brotherhood" of perfected adepts silently guiding human evolution. The individual soul reincarnates, bound by karma, climbing across many lifetimes toward reunion with the divine.

It was a sweeping, syncretic myth of everything — and one element must be named plainly. Blavatsky's root-race scheme, however spiritually she intended it, was later seized and twisted by others into a doctrine of racial hierarchy, feeding the Germanic "Ariosophy" of Guido von List and Lanz von Liebenfels and the milieu of the Thule Society, which in turn fed the occult fringe of Nazism — even as the Theosophical Society itself made the "universal brotherhood of humanity, without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste, or colour" the first of its stated objects. The idea and its abuse must both be recorded; neither cancels the other.

Olcott, Besant, and the search for the World Teacher

Theosophy was never only a doctrine on paper. Colonel Olcott, its co-founder, became — improbably — a central figure in the revival of Buddhism in Sri Lanka: he took the Buddhist refuges publicly in 1880 (the first prominent Westerner to do so), founded Buddhist schools, wrote a widely translated Buddhist Catechism (1881), and is honored in Sri Lanka to this day as a father of the national and Buddhist renaissance. Theosophy's traffic with Asia was, in this respect, real and consequential.

After Blavatsky's death in 1891, leadership passed to the formidable Annie Besant — former secularist, socialist, and campaigner — and to the clairvoyant Charles W. Leadbeater. Together they announced that a new World Teacher was coming, and identified the vehicle for his consciousness in a young South Indian boy, Jiddu Krishnamurti, around whom they built the Order of the Star in the East (1911). The story ended in one of the great renunciations of modern religion: on 3 August 1929, before some three thousand followers at Ommen, Krishnamurti dissolved the Order, declaring "Truth is a pathless land, and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever, by any religion, by any sect" — and walked away from the messiahship prepared for him. Earlier, in 1912, the German Theosophist Rudolf Steiner, uneasy with this orientalizing turn and wishing to stress Western Christian mysticism, broke away to found Anthroposophy — the source, in time, of Waldorf education and biodynamic farming.

The occult revival: the Golden Dawn

Theosophy was the most famous face of a wider Western esoteric revival. Its intellectual bridge from the older magic was the French occultist Éliphas Lévi (1810–1875), who linked the tarot to the Kabbalah and drew the composite Baphomet of Chapter 39. Its magical wing crystallized in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, the most influential ceremonial-magic order in Western history.

The order grew from a set of encoded "Cipher Manuscripts," written in a Trithemian cipher, that surfaced in 1887; from them William Wynn Westcott, Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers, and William Woodman built a working system and founded the Isis-Urania Temple in London on 1 March 1888. The Golden Dawn welded Hermeticism, Kabbalah, tarot, astrology, alchemy, and ceremonial magic into a single graded ladder of initiation — an Outer Order of elemental grades (Neophyte to Philosophus) and a secret Inner Order, the Rosae Rubeae et Aureae Crucis, of adept theurgy. Its whole method was a system of correspondences: each Hebrew letter, planet, color, deity, scent, and tarot card lined up in vast cross-referenced tables. Its members reshaped modern culture — the poet W. B. Yeats, the actress Florence Farr, the mystic Dion Fortune, and A. E. Waite, whose 1909 tarot (drawn by Pamela Colman Smith) became the deck most of the world now knows. In 1900 the order tore itself apart in a schism — Annie Horniman's revolt against Mathers, and Mathers's promotion of a brilliant, difficult young member over the London temple's objections — spawning successor bodies (Alpha et Omega, Stella Matutina).

Crowley and the law of Thelema

That difficult young member was Aleister Crowley (1875–1947), who had joined the Golden Dawn in 1898 and would become the most notorious occultist of the age — the tabloids' "wickedest man in the world." On his honeymoon in Cairo in April 1904, Crowley said, a discarnate intelligence named Aiwass dictated to him, over three days, The Book of the Law (Liber AL vel Legis). It proclaimed a new age, the Aeon of Horus, and a single commandment: "Do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the Law" — balanced by "Love is the law, love under will." This was not, in Crowley's reading, mere licence but the injunction to discover and follow one's deepest True Will. He built his religion, Thelema (Greek for "will"), around it, and two orders to carry it: the A∴A∴ (Argenteum Astrum, "Silver Star"), founded about 1907, and the German-rooted Ordo Templi Orientis (O.T.O.), which he took over after 1912 and remade on Thelemic lines, adding its central sexual-magical rite. His Abbey of Thelema at Cefalù in Sicily (1920) ended in scandal and his expulsion by Mussolini. Reviled in his lifetime, Crowley became, after it, one of the most cited names in modern occultism and popular culture.

The believer's lens

To the Theosophist, Blavatsky was not an impostor but a messenger — a flawed and human one, perhaps, but the bearer of a genuine perennial wisdom, the philosophia perennis that mystics of every tradition had always half-glimpsed. The doctrine's appeal is its unity: it offers a single key to all religions, a cosmos that is just (karma), purposeful (evolution toward the divine), and open to anyone willing to study and to grow, without priest or church. To the ceremonial magician of the Golden Dawn, the graded initiations were a real technology of self-transformation — the systematic remaking of the psyche through symbol, ritual, and imagination, whatever a materialist made of the "spirits" invoked. To the Thelemite, "Do what thou wilt" is not selfishness but the hardest of disciplines: the lifelong labor of finding one's true purpose and aligning every act with it. Seen from inside, these were not frauds but paths of interior work and gnosis, and their fruits — in the felt experience of transformation, and in the poetry, art, and psychology they nourished — were real to those who walked them.

The skeptical lens

The historian's verdict is blunter, and it falls hardest on the claim of antiquity. Blavatsky's "ancient sources" have been shown, in many passages, to be lifted from contemporary nineteenth-century books, sometimes near-verbatim, and dressed as immemorial revelation; the Hodgson report caught the machinery of the "materialized" letters. The Golden Dawn's founding "Cipher Manuscripts" and their supposed German patroness, Fräulein Anna Sprengel, are very probably a pious forgery by Westcott to lend the new order a Rosicrucian lineage it did not have. The tarot's "ancient Egyptian" pedigree is an eighteenth-century invention (from Court de Gébelin), not a survival. Crowley's Aiwass is, to the skeptic, a stratum of his own extraordinary mind. In short, the "ancient wisdom" was, to a very large degree, a modern construction — brilliant, influential, and new.

And the gravest caution is not about fraud but about consequence. The root-race doctrine, whatever its author's universalist intent, was genuinely bent toward racism and fed the occult currents on which Nazism drew. A movement can preach the brotherhood of humanity and still leave a poison in the world; honesty requires saying both.

Symbology and sacred encoding

This is, above all others, the modern age's great tradition of constructed sacred symbolism — and the honesty lies in seeing how much of it was newly assembled from older parts.

  • The tarot became its master-key. The Golden Dawn mapped the seventy-eight cards onto the Kabbalistic Tree of Life of Chapter 26 — the twenty-two trumps to the twenty-two paths between the sefirot — turning a Renaissance card game into an encoded diagram of the soul's journey; the Rider–Waite–Smith deck (1909) fixed that symbolism for the modern world. The encoding is real and intricate; its claimed Egyptian antiquity is not.
  • The Golden Dawn's whole practice was a system of correspondences — the vast tables (later published as Crowley's 777) in which every letter, planet, color, god, scent, and card cross-reference, so that the cosmos becomes a code the magician learns to operate.
  • The Theosophical Society's own seal is a deliberate synthesis in miniature: the ouroboros serpent, the interlaced triangles of the Star of David, the ankh, the Om, and — in its original form — the swastika (then an ancient auspicious sign, before the Nazis defiled it), ringed together to say that all the world's sacred symbols are one.
  • Thelema encodes its law numerically: 93 — the value, by Greek isopsephy (Chapter 15), of both Thelema (will) and Agape (love) — became the movement's cipher and greeting, a gematria in the modern West.

The thread through all of it is the Hermetic maxim "as above, so below": the conviction that the cosmos is an encoded correspondence the initiate learns to read.

Connections

Theosophy and the occult revival are the children of Spiritualism (Chapter 36) and of the Enlightenment's new comparative study of religion, which laid the world's faiths side by side and invited exactly this kind of synthesis. They borrowed the Kabbalah and Hermeticism of the medieval and Renaissance West (Chapters 17, 26) and the karma and reincarnation of India (Chapters 11, 30), and recombined them into something new. They are the direct parents of much that follows: the modern New Age (Chapter 35), the ceremonial magic and Goddess-craft that would feed Wicca (Chapter 38, whose founder Gerald Gardner knew Crowley and the O.T.O.), and a great swathe of twentieth-century art, film, music, and "spirituality." Few movements built on so much that cannot be verified have left so deep a mark on the modern imagination.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The history is richly documented: Blavatsky, Olcott, and the Theosophical Society (1875); the texts (Isis Unveiled 1877, The Secret Doctrine 1888); Olcott's real role in the Sri Lankan Buddhist revival; Besant, Leadbeater, and the rise and 1929 dissolution of the Krishnamurti "World Teacher" project; Steiner and Anthroposophy (1912); the Golden Dawn (1888) and its members; Crowley, The Book of the Law (1904), and Thelema — and the vast, traceable influence of all this on modern religion, art, and the New Age.

Not supported (as history or science)

The hidden Masters and the materializing Mahatma letters (the machinery caught by the SPR's Hodgson in 1885); the root races and the lost continents of Lemuria and Atlantis (no such races or continents existed); the Golden Dawn's forged Sprengel charter; the "ancient Egyptian" tarot; and Blavatsky's claimed primordial sources, much of which is recombined from contemporary books. The "ancient wisdom" was, in large part, a modern construction.

Genuinely open — and a warning

The sincerity and inner experience of practitioners are not in question, and these are living traditions with real fruits in art and psychology. But two things stand: the antiquity these systems claim for themselves is mostly invented, however genuine the systems themselves; and the root-race doctrine was bent toward racism and fed proto-Nazi occultism — a dark legacy a movement preaching universal brotherhood nonetheless left in the world.
