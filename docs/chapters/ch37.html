Modern · Theosophy & the Occult Revival

Theosophy & the Occult Revival

In the archive

The late-nineteenth-century rebirth of Western esotericism — a claimed ancient wisdom behind all religions, hidden Masters in the Himalayas, a magical order that fused Kabbalah and tarot into a system of the soul — and the honest reckoning with its fabrications, its enormous influence, and the dark uses to which some of its ideas were later put.

The letters from the Masters

In the 1870s and 1880s, a stout, chain-smoking Russian émigré named Helena Petrovna Blavatsky became the center of a sensation. Letters, she said, arrived from her hidden teachers — the Mahatmas or Masters, adepts of a secret brotherhood dwelling in Tibet — and these letters did not come by post: they materialized, dropping from the air, appearing inside sealed rooms. Through them, Blavatsky claimed to transmit the Secret Doctrine, the single ancient wisdom that lay behind every religion the world had ever known. In 1875, in New York, she and Colonel Henry Olcott founded the Theosophical Society to spread it. In 1885 the Society for Psychical Research investigated the Mahatma letters and pronounced them an elaborate fraud. Both of those things are true, and both matter: Theosophy was built on claims that do not survive scrutiny, and it became, all the same, one of the most influential religious movements of the modern West.

What Theosophy is

Theosophy — "divine wisdom" — is the system Blavatsky set out in her great books, Isis Unveiled (1877) and The Secret Doctrine (1888): a grand synthesis of Eastern religion and Western occultism, claiming that all faiths are corrupted fragments of one original, secret wisdom-tradition preserved by hidden adepts. From the East it took karma and reincarnation, cosmic cycles, and the idea of spiritual evolution; from the West, Hermeticism, Neoplatonism, and Kabbalah. More than any other single movement, Theosophy introduced Hindu and Buddhist ideas to the Western public and set the template for everything later called "spiritual but not religious."

The doctrine of the Secret Doctrine

Theosophy taught a vast cosmic evolution: the universe and humanity develop through immense cycles of "rounds" and seven "root races," each rising and falling, some on now-lost continents — Lemuria and Atlantis. Behind the visible world lay hierarchies of planes and beings, crowned by the Masters — the "Great White Brotherhood" of perfected adepts guiding human evolution. The soul reincarnates, bound by karma, climbing across lifetimes toward reunion with the divine. It was a sweeping, syncretic myth of everything — and it must be said plainly that its root-race scheme, however spiritually Blavatsky intended it, was later seized and twisted by others into racial hierarchy, feeding the "Ariosophy" that in turn fed the occult fringe of Nazism — even as the Theosophical Society itself preached the universal brotherhood of humanity. The idea and its abuse must both be named.

The occult revival

Theosophy was the most famous face of a wider Western esoteric revival that swept the late nineteenth century. Its magical wing crystallized in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in London in 1888 by Westcott, Mathers, and Woodman — the most influential magical order in Western history. The Golden Dawn welded Hermeticism, Kabbalah, tarot, astrology, alchemy, and ceremonial magic into a single graded system of initiation, and through its members — the poet W. B. Yeats among them — reshaped modern art and occultism alike. One member, Aleister Crowley, broke away to found his own religion, Thelema, on the revelation he claimed to have received in Cairo in 1904 — the Book of the Law and its creed, "Do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the Law." Others followed: Rudolf Steiner broke from Theosophy to found Anthroposophy (the source of Waldorf schools and biodynamic farming), and the era revived Rosicrucianism and the tarot besides.

The World Teacher who refused

Theosophy’s most dramatic episode came after Blavatsky. Under her successors Annie Besant and Charles Leadbeater, the Society came to expect an imminent World Teacher, the vehicle of the coming Lord Maitreya. In 1909 Leadbeater discovered a quiet Indian boy on the beach at Adyar, Jiddu Krishnamurti, and declared him the vehicle; Besant adopted him, and a whole organization, the Order of the Star in the East, was built around him, with tens of thousands of members awaiting his manifestation. Then, on 3 August 1929, before some three thousand followers at Ommen in Holland, Krishnamurti stood up and dissolved the Order, renounced the role, and returned the money and property given for it. “Truth is a pathless land,” he told them, “and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever… Truth, being limitless, unconditioned, unapproachable by any path whatsoever, cannot be organized.” He spent the rest of his long life as an independent teacher repudiating gurus and organizations altogether — a striking case of a manufactured messiah refusing his own cult, and a genuine spiritual voice emerging from the wreckage of the machinery built to produce him.

The invisible made visible

One of Theosophy’s least expected legacies hangs today in the great museums: it helped invent abstract art. In 1905 Besant and Leadbeater published Thought-Forms, a book of vivid color-plates claiming to depict emotions and thoughts as clairvoyantly seen shapes and hues — a systematic code of the invisible. Its images and its premise — that non-representational form and color could carry spiritual meaning directly — rippled through the founders of modern abstraction. Wassily Kandinsky (whose Concerning the Spiritual in Art is steeped in Theosophy), Piet Mondrian (a committed Theosophist), and Kazimir Malevich all reached toward a spiritual abstraction shaped by these ideas. Most striking of all, the Swedish painter Hilma af Klint, a member of the Theosophical Society, was painting large, wholly abstract canvases as early as 1906 — years before the men long credited as abstraction’s pioneers — as a deliberately mediumistic, esoteric project. The drive to paint the unseen, which opened twentieth-century art, ran straight through the occult revival.

Symbology and sacred encoding

This is, above all others, the modern age's great tradition of constructed sacred symbolism — and the honesty lies in seeing how much of it was newly assembled from older parts.

  • The tarot became its master-key. The Golden Dawn mapped the seventy-eight cards onto the Kabbalistic Tree of Life of Chapter 26 — the twenty-two trumps to the twenty-two paths — turning a Renaissance card game into an encoded diagram of the soul's journey. But the "ancient Egyptian" pedigree the occultists claimed for the tarot is an eighteenth-century invention; the encoding is real and powerful, its antiquity is not.
  • The Golden Dawn's whole method was a system of correspondences — each Hebrew letter, planet, color, god, scent, and card lined up in vast tables, so that the whole cosmos became a cross-referenced code the magician could operate.
  • The Theosophical Society's own seal is a deliberate synthesis in miniature: the ouroboros serpent, the interlaced triangles of the Star of David, the ankh, the Om, and — in its original form — the swastika (then an ancient auspicious sign, before the Nazis defiled it), ringed together to say that all the world's sacred symbols are one.

The thread through all of it is the Hermetic maxim "as above, so below" — the conviction that the cosmos is an encoded correspondence the initiate learns to read.

Connections

Theosophy and the occult revival are the children of Spiritualism (the previous chapter) and of the Enlightenment's new comparative study of religion, which laid the world's faiths side by side and invited exactly this kind of synthesis. They borrowed the Kabbalah and Hermeticism of the medieval and Renaissance West and the karma and reincarnation of India, and recombined them into something new. And they are the direct parents of much that follows: the modern New Age, the ceremonial magic that would feed Wicca (the next chapter), Crowley's Thelema, and a great swathe of twentieth-century art, film, and "spirituality." Few movements built on so much that cannot be verified have left so deep a mark.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The history of Theosophy and the occult revival — Blavatsky, Olcott, and the Theosophical Society (1875); the texts; the Golden Dawn (1888) and its members; Crowley and Thelema; Steiner and Anthroposophy — and their vast, documented influence on modern religion, art, and the New Age. The World Teacher episode — Besant, Leadbeater, the Order of the Star, and Krishnamurti’s dissolution of it in 1929 — and Theosophy’s documented role in the birth of abstract art (Thought-Forms, 1905; Kandinsky, Mondrian, and Hilma af Klint) are firmly attested.

Not supported (as history or science)

The hidden Masters and the materializing Mahatma letters (judged fraudulent by the Society for Psychical Research in 1885); the root races and the lost continents of Lemuria and Atlantis (no such history or continents existed); and Blavatsky's claimed ancient sources, much of which scholars have shown to be recombined from contemporary books, sometimes verbatim. The "ancient wisdom" was, in large part, a modern construction.

Genuinely open — and a warning

The sincerity and inner experience of practitioners are not in question, and the traditions are living ones. But two cautions stand: the antiquity these systems claim for themselves (the "Egyptian" tarot, the primordial Secret Doctrine) is mostly invented, and the root-race doctrine was genuinely bent toward racism and fed proto-Nazi occultism — a dark legacy that a movement preaching universal brotherhood nonetheless left in the world.
