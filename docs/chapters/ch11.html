Axial Age · Buddhism

Buddhism

In the archive

For three centuries after his death, Buddhists depicted the Buddha by not depicting him — an empty throne, a footprint, a wheel. It is the perfect emblem of a teacher whose most radical idea was that there is, at the deepest level, no permanent self at all.

A throne with no one on it

On the carved gateways of the great stupa at Sanchi (c. 1st century BCE), crowds of devotees bow and garland — and at the center of their reverence there is nobody there. They kneel before an empty throne beneath a tree, venerate a pair of footprints, a wheel, a parasol shading an absence. For roughly three centuries after his death, this is how Buddhists depicted the Buddha: by not depicting him at all.

It is the most fitting introduction to the tradition, because the man these symbols point to taught the most radical idea in this archive: that there is, at the deepest level, no permanent self — not in you, not in anyone, not even in the enlightened one who “blew out” like a flame. The empty throne is a doctrine in stone. This chapter is Buddhism in its earliest form: the historical Buddha, his teaching, and the community and empire that carried it from a renunciant’s insight to a world religion.

The man, and the limits of his story

The founder was Siddhartha Gautama, of the Shakya people of the Ganges plain, later called the Buddha, “the Awakened One.” That a real teacher existed is not seriously doubted — but we cannot write his biography with confidence, because the life-stories are later and heavily legendary, and even his dates are disputed: traditionally about 563–483 BCE, but much recent scholarship favors a “short chronology” of roughly 480–400 BCE.

The traditional story, told for its meaning: a sheltered prince encounters the Four Sights — an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a serene ascetic — and is shattered by the discovery of aging, sickness, and death. He abandons palace and family (the Great Renunciation), practices extreme asceticism, then rejects it for a Middle Way, and attains enlightenment (bodhi) beneath a fig tree at Bodh Gaya. He preaches his first sermon at Sarnath — “turning the wheel of the Dharma” — teaches for forty-five years, and dies (attains parinirvana) at Kushinagar.

The world that made him: the śramaṇa ferment

The Ganges plain of his day was in religious upheaval. A movement of śramaṇas — “strivers,” wandering renunciants — had risen in revolt against the religion of Chapter 5: they rejected the authority of the Brahmin priests, the efficacy of the Vedic sacrifice, and the whole apparatus of ritual, seeking liberation instead through meditation, ethics, and ascetic discipline. Buddhism was one śramaṇa movement among several — its great contemporary was Jainism (Mahavira), and there were Ajivikas and materialists too. What they shared was a new question: not “how do we please the gods?” but “how do we escape suffering and the endless round of rebirth?”

The teaching: four truths and a missing self

The Buddha framed his insight as the Four Noble Truths, in the manner of a physician’s diagnosis and cure: (1) dukkha — life is pervaded by suffering and unsatisfactoriness; (2) its origin is craving (tanha); (3) its cessation is possible — extinguish craving, and suffering ends: this is nirvana; (4) the way is the Noble Eightfold Path (right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, concentration), grouped into wisdom, ethics, and meditation.

Underlying this are the three marks of existence: anicca (everything is impermanent), dukkha (clinging to the impermanent is painful), and the revolutionary anatta (non-self): there is no permanent soul or essence in a person or anything. What we call a “self” is a bundle of ever-changing processes (the five aggregates) with no fixed “I” behind them.

Here the payoff of the Vedic chapter arrives. The Upanishads (Chapter 5) taught that within you is the atman, the eternal Self, identical with Brahman, and that liberation is finding it. The Buddha’s anatta is the flat contradiction: there is no such Self to find. Where the Upanishadic sage sought and discovered the true self, the Buddha looked and found only a stream of conditioned processes — and taught that clinging to the illusion of a permanent self is the very root of suffering. Two of the East’s greatest religions diverge on exactly this point.

How can there be rebirth without a soul? By dependent origination (pratityasamutpada): nothing exists as an independent substance; everything arises in dependence on conditions. Rebirth is not a soul migrating but a causal continuity — like one flame lighting another, neither the same nor wholly different. Karma drives this, an impersonal moral law, but no eternal self “has” it. And nirvana — literally “blowing out” — is the extinguishing of the fires of craving, which the Buddha refused to define as either annihilation or eternal existence. Asked the great metaphysical questions, he often kept a famous silence (the parable of the man shot with a poisoned arrow, who dies before learning who shot it): liberation, not speculation, was the point. And note what is absent: there is no creator god. Gods exist in Buddhist cosmology but are themselves caught in rebirth — not creators, not saviors. Buddhism is, at root, non-theistic.

Ethics and the community

Because all beings suffer in the same web, the response is compassion (karuna), loving-kindness (metta), and above all ahimsa (non-harming). For laypeople the ethical core is the Five Precepts: to refrain from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants — the natural conduct of one who understands karma, with no divine lawgiver behind them. The Buddha founded the Sangha, the order of monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis, established at his foster-mother’s request, though the texts record his hesitation), governed by the elaborate Vinaya. Monks and laity lived in reciprocity — the laity giving support (dana, earning merit), the monks giving the teaching — and at the center stood the Three Jewels: refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha.

The texts, and the empire that spread them

The Buddha wrote nothing; his teaching was carried orally. Tradition says the First Council convened after his death, where Ananda recited the discourses and Upali the rules. The movement split into some eighteen early schools; the one whose scriptures survive most fully is the Theravada, whose Pali Canon — the Tipitaka (“Three Baskets”: Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma) — was written down only around the first century BCE in Sri Lanka. (The Mahayana transformation belongs to a later chapter.) The figure who turned a regional movement into a world religion was the emperor Ashoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE), ruler of the Mauryan empire, who converted (remorseful, tradition says, after the slaughter at Kalinga), inscribed edicts of Buddhist ethics on rocks and pillars across the subcontinent (the earliest datable Buddhist evidence), built stupas for the Buddha’s relics, and sent missionaries abroad — above all to Sri Lanka. Ashoka is to Buddhism what Constantine would be to Christianity: the imperial patron who made a world religion.

What the ground shows

The record is rich from Ashoka onward: his pillars (the four-lion capital from Sarnath is now India’s state emblem) and rock edicts; the pillar at Lumbini; and the stupa complexes at Sanchi and Bharhut, whose reliefs show early Buddhist aniconism. The first anthropomorphic images of the Buddha appear only around the first century CE, in Gandhara and Mathura — and the Gandharan Buddhas, with their toga-like robes, show the unmistakable influence of Greek art carried east after Alexander (Chapter 8): the beautiful fusion of Greco-Buddhist sculpture.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The eloquence of absence. Early aniconism is the tradition’s first act of sacred encoding: to show the Buddha by an empty throne, a footprint, a Bodhi tree, or a wheel was to say what words could not — that the Awakened One had passed beyond form into nirvana and could not be caught in an image. Absence, here, is the most exact presence.

The wheel. The central symbol is the Dharmachakra, and it is encoded doctrine: its eight spokes stand for the Eightfold Path. To “turn the wheel” is to teach; Ashoka set it atop his pillars, and it turns today on the flag of India.

The stupa as cosmos. The stupa raised over a relic is architecture as cosmology: the dome an image of the heavens, a central axis as the axis of the universe, the relic the still point — and worshippers circumambulate it clockwise, walking the cosmos in miniature.

Teaching by number. The most distinctive Buddhist encoding is its relentless numbered lists — the Four Truths, the Eightfold Path, the Three Marks and Jewels, the Five Precepts and Aggregates, the Twelve links. In an oral tradition the numbered list is a memory technology, a scaffold that holds a vast teaching exactly — the same problem the Vedic reciters solved with interlocking patterns (Chapter 5), solved here by enumeration. The lotus, the Bodhi tree, and the auspicious swastika round out the early vocabulary. Honesty note: the elaborate mantras, mandalas, and iconography many associate with Buddhism belong largely to the later Mahayana and Tantric developments, not this austere early period.

Connections

Buddhism’s deepest tie is to Chapter 5: a child and rebel of the Vedic world, sharing its vocabulary of karma, rebirth, and samsara while rejecting its sacrifice, its priesthood, and — most sharply — its atman. It is the sibling of Jainism in the śramaṇa ferment. Across the Axial Age it belongs with the era’s other transformations — Confucius and the Chinese sages, the Greek philosophers, and the prophets of Israel (Chapters 7, 10) — the great turn, in Karl Jaspers’s phrase, toward inwardness and ethics across Eurasia. Forward, Buddhism’s story is only beginning: the Mahayana, the spread to China, Tibet, Southeast Asia, and Japan, and the Greco-Buddhist art of Gandhara all belong to later chapters.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • A historical teacher (the Buddha) founded a śramaṇa renunciant movement in the Ganges plain c. 5th century BCE, reacting against Vedic ritualism.
  • The core teaching — the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, the three marks (anicca, dukkha, anatta), dependent origination, karma and rebirth without a permanent self, and nirvana — and Buddhism’s non-theism (no creator god).
  • The Sangha and Vinaya, the Pali Canon (written c. 1st century BCE), the patronage of Ashoka and his edicts, and early aniconism followed by the first Buddha images (c. 1st century CE, with Greek influence at Gandhara).

What’s not supported

  • The detailed traditional biography as literal history (the sheltered prince, the exact Four Sights, the miraculous elements are later hagiography).
  • Precise dates for the Buddha (both the traditional and “short” chronologies are reconstructions).
  • That the earliest texts preserve his verbatim words (long oral transmission, later redaction); and reading later Mahayana/Tantric doctrine and iconography back into early Buddhism.

What’s genuinely open

  • The Buddha’s dates and the finer details of his life.
  • How much of the earliest teaching can be securely recovered from the later canon.
  • The precise original meaning of nirvana and anatta, debated within Buddhism since antiquity (does anything “remain” in nirvana? what exactly is denied by “non-self”?).
