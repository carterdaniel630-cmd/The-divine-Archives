Yoruba

Recently added · pending review

The religion of the Yoruba people of southwestern Nigeria, Benin, and Togo — a supreme God, Olódùmarè, ruling through a shining company of orishas; the great divination system of Ifá, whose verses form one of the largest oral libraries on earth; sacred kings, returned ancestors, and a city, Ilé-Ifẹ̀, where its people say the world itself began — and the faith that, carried across the Atlantic in the holds of slave ships, seeded the living religions of Cuba, Brazil, and Trinidad — and left its gods among the nations of Haiti's Vodou.

The city of four hundred gods

Ilé-Ifẹ̀, in what is now Osun State in southwestern Nigeria, is called by its people the city of 401 gods — one for nearly every day of the year (some Ifẹ̀ accounts count 201 of the 401 here), each with a festival, a shrine, a priesthood, a body of songs. It is, in Yoruba understanding, not merely an old city but the first place: the spot where dry land was first spread upon the waters, where the first humans were shaped, where kingship began. When, in the early twentieth century, European excavators and collectors encountered the city's medieval art — life-size heads in brass and terracotta of a serenity and naturalism that stunned them — some refused to believe Africans had made them, and reached for Atlantis and ancient Greece instead. The heads were Yoruba, made at Ifẹ̀ between roughly the twelfth and fifteenth centuries, and they are the surviving signature of the civilisation this chapter describes: one of the great religious cultures of Africa, and — through the diaspora — of the whole Atlantic world.

What the Yoruba religion is

The Yoruba are among the largest peoples of West Africa, numbering in the tens of millions, and their traditional religion — sometimes called Ìṣẹ̀ṣe, "the way of the ancestors' tradition" — is among the most complete indigenous religious systems of the continent. At its summit stands one God, Olódùmarè — a name Idowu, the tradition's classic theologian, reads as greatness and fullness that does not change — called also Olórun, Owner of Heaven: source of all being, too vast and remote for temples or priesthoods of his own. Between God and humanity moves the òrìṣà (orishas): hundreds of divine powers — tradition says 401, a number that means "more than can be counted," though the counts vary by town and authority, and 201 and 601 circulate as well — who govern rivers, iron, thunder, healing, and the crossroads, and who receive the sacrifices and songs God does not. Beneath and among them stand the ancestors, the honoured dead of every lineage, who return in masked form to bless and correct the living. The force that runs through gods, ancestors, kings, and every living thing is àṣẹ — power-to-make-things-happen, the energy of the world, given by Olódùmarè and spent, shared, and renewed in ritual. And the system's memory and mind is Ifá: a divination corpus of 256 signs and thousands of verses, consulted at every hinge of life. Chapter 33 placed this faith within the wider family of African traditional religions; this chapter gives it its own room, as its scale — at home and across the ocean — demands.

Ilé-Ifẹ̀, the first city

A sacred city before it was an excavated one. Ifẹ̀ was a going concern — walled, paved in places with potsherd mosaics, served by ironworkers and by glassmakers whose beads travelled far beyond Yorubaland — centuries before Europeans reached the Guinea coast. Its kings, the Ọọ̀ni, were and remain sacred figures, heads of a dynasty the tradition traces to Odùduwà himself. The city's artistic peak — the height of a flowering that runs from the twelfth to the fifteenth century — came between about 1200 and 1400: terracotta and copper-alloy heads of kings, queens, and gods, cast and modelled with a quiet realism unlike anything else in the Africa of their day. Tradition remembers one early king, Ọbalùfòn II, as the patron — indeed the divine patron — of brass-casting itself. What the ground shows, then, broadly confirms what the tradition claims about rank: Ifẹ̀ was no village cult-site but the ritual capital of a sophisticated urban civilisation, the mother-city from which Yoruba kingdoms across the region claimed their crowns. When a Yoruba king is installed to this day, his legitimacy is still spoken of in Ifẹ̀'s language: the crown comes, in the end, from the place where the world began.

Creation: earth scattered on the waters

In the beginning, in the Ifẹ̀ telling, there was water below and sky above. Olódùmarè resolved to make dry land, and sent down the orisha Ọbàtálá — in some tellings the eldest of the gods — with a chain, a snail shell filled with earth, a palm nut, and a hen. On the way down, the story says, Ọbàtálá stopped, drank palm wine, and slept; and so the task passed to Odùduwà, who climbed down the chain, poured the earth upon the waters, and set the hen to scratching and scattering it — which is why the land is uneven, and why Ilé-Ifẹ̀, "the place where the earth spread out," is the navel of the world. Odùduwà planted the palm nut, from which grew a tree with sixteen branches, read as the first sixteen Yoruba kingdoms; Ọbàtálá, sobered and set to a second task, was given the shaping of human bodies from clay, which Olódùmarè alone breathes life into — a work Ọbàtálá is still said to do, and to do imperfectly when he drinks, which is why his devotees forswear palm wine and why he is the special protector of those born with disabilities. In the other widely recorded sequence, it is Ọbàtálá who completes the earth-making, and Odùduwà arrives afterward and takes the kingship — the version in which the old Ifẹ̀–Ọ̀yọ́ argument over precedence still lives. The archive reports this as the tradition tells it, in its Ifẹ̀ form: other towns, and the diaspora, tell it with the roles and the moral differently weighted, and Odùduwà himself stands differently in different houses of the faith — a god who descended, the first king of Ifẹ̀, or a mortal king raised by memory into a god. The stories are the tradition's own; the disagreements among them are part of the record, and this chapter does not settle them.

Olódùmarè and the orishas

One God, many powers, no devil. Olódùmarè is not fought by any rival evil god; misfortune has other addresses — the àjogun, the tradition's standing company of malefic powers (Ikú, death; Àrùn, disease; Ẹgbà, paralysis; and their company), against whom much ẹbọ is directed and whose dealings Èṣù mediates; witches, broken taboos, neglected ancestors; and above all Èṣù, the messenger, who is no devil but the divine inspector and enforcer, keeper of the crossroads where every sacrifice must first be delivered. Èṣù opens or closes every road: trickster, linguist, policeman of the cosmos, he is saluted in some form before any other orisha can be reached, because nothing travels between heaven and earth except through him. Around him the great orishas hold their provinces. Ọbàtálá, king of the white cloth, maker of bodies, lord of purity and cool patience. Ògún, lord of iron, first to clear the road through the primordial forest with his cutlass, patron of smiths, soldiers, hunters — and, in the modern world, of drivers and all who work metal. Ọ̀sányìn, lord of leaves and of herbal medicine — the healing art is one of the Ifá corpus's own domains. Ọ̀ṣọ́ọ̀sì, the hunter, keen-eyed patron of the chase, whose cult would grow great across the Atlantic. Ṣàngó, lord of thunder, remembered also as a king of Ọ̀yọ́ raised to godhood, whose double-headed axe falls as lightning. Ọya, of wind and storm, of the river Niger and of transformation — Ṣàngó's great counterpart, and a major power in her own right. Ọ̀ṣun, lady of the river that bears her name, of sweet water, love, fertility, and diplomacy. Yemọja, mother of waters, mother — her name says — of fish and of orishas. Ọ̀rúnmìlà, the witness of creation, who was present when every destiny was chosen and who therefore knows them all: the orisha of wisdom, whose voice is Ifá. Some orishas are forces of nature wearing faces; some are ancestors — kings, founders, culture-heroes — remembered into divinity; the boundary between the two kinds is one the tradition itself treats as porous, because àṣẹ flows where it is honoured, and a great human being and a god are points on one scale of power.

One further cult belongs to no single orisha's province: the Ìbejì, the twins. The Yoruba have among the highest twinning rates in the world, and the twins are honoured in a famous cult of their own; the carved figures made for them — the ère ìbejì — are among the best-known of all Yoruba art forms.

Orí: the head, and destiny

The most personal god a Yoruba person has is their own head. Orí — literally the physical head, and by extension the inner head, the personal spirit and destiny within — is chosen, the tradition teaches, before birth: kneeling in the house of Àjàlá, the potter of heads, each soul picks an orí from the shelves, good or flawed, and with it the broad shape of a life; the choice is then forgotten, and Ọ̀rúnmìlà, who witnessed it, can be asked through Ifá what was chosen. This is not fatalism, and the tradition is explicit about the counterweight: character — ìwà — can redeem a poor head or ruin a fine one. "Character is beauty," runs the proverb, ìwà l'ẹwà; a destiny is a field, not a sentence, and sacrifice, effort, and good character are how it is worked. Orí receives offerings before any orisha — the rite is ìbọrí, the feeding of the head, the most common personal ritual in the tradition — because no blessing can reach a person except through their own head. Philosophers of the tradition — and Yoruba religion has always produced them — find in this one of its deepest teachings: heaven deals the hand, but the person plays it, and is answerable for the playing.

Ifá: the library that is not written

At the centre of the system stands a book that exists only in memory. Ifá divination works like this: the priest, the bàbáláwo — "father of secrets" — casts sacred palm nuts (ìkin) or the divining chain (ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀) to generate one of 256 signs, the odù — the 256 built up from sixteen principal odù, the ojú odù or méjì, whose pairings generate the rest; each odù opens a body of verses, the ẹsẹ Ifá — stories, myths, proverbs, and precedents, around eight hundred verses to an odù by UNESCO's reckoning, and the corpus is still growing, as living verse does. The priest recites the verses that fit the case — a birth, a marriage, an illness, a kingship, a journey — and the client finds their situation inside the stories, together with the ẹbọ, the prescribed offering or action, that the verse lays down. A bàbáláwo's training runs to years of memorisation; the greatest are walking libraries, and the office has historically been male, though women diviners, ìyánífá, are attested and their standing is debated within the tradition today. What Ifá is for is as important as how it works: it is the faith's mechanism for making uncertainty bearable and decisions legitimate — in the classical kingdoms, no king was crowned and no grave matter settled without it. UNESCO proclaimed the Ifá divination system a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005 and inscribed it on its Representative List in 2008, honouring what its own element text calls an extensive corpus of texts. Practitioners have a plainer word for that corpus — the word they have always used: a library — of history, philosophy, medicine, and law — carried in trained minds instead of on shelves. Ifá is not the tradition's only oracle: ẹ̀rìndínlógún, divination by sixteen cowries, is historically the more widely consulted of the two, and the one that travelled most broadly into diaspora practice.

Kingship and the city-states

The Yoruba built cities, and the cities built an empire. From Ifẹ̀ the tradition sends out Òrànmíyàn, youngest of Odùduwà's line, who founds — in the tradition's telling — both the Edo kingdom of Benin and, turning home, the kingdom of Ọ̀yọ́, which by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries had grown into the greatest empire of the forest-savanna edge: cavalry on the northern grass, tribute from neighbours including, for a time, Dahomey, and a capital at Òyó-Ilé whose king, the Aláàfin, ruled checked by the Ọ̀yọ́ Mèsì, a council of seven that could, at the limit, present the king with the parrot's eggs that ordered him to die. Sacred kingship, in other words, with constitutional teeth. And standing alongside the councils was Ògbóni, the society of elders bound to the earth, which checked kings, judged capital matters, and embodied — across Yorubaland — the counterweight of the earth to the king's heaven. Ọ̀yọ́'s greatest royal gift to the religion was one of its own kings: Ṣàngó, the Aláàfin whose reign ended in storm and accusation and who was remembered — deified — as the thunder itself. The empire broke in the early nineteenth century under civil war and the Fulani jihad from the north; Òyó-Ilé was abandoned by the 1830s, and the Yoruba wars that followed filled the slave ships of the trade's final, brutal decades — which is precisely why so much of Yorubaland's religion, and not another region's, took root so deeply in Cuba and Brazil. The drumbeat under all of it: kings are made by ritual, restrained by councils, judged by ancestors, and remembered, if they are great or terrible enough, as gods.

Ritual and the round of the year

This is a religion you can hear. Its public life is festival: drummers calling the orishas down with talking drums whose tones carry Yoruba speech itself, dancers in sequined cloth, devotees through whom — the tradition teaches — the orisha may come and be present among their people, mounted on the "horse" of the trance. Its private life is the offering: kola nut, gin, palm oil, a chicken, a goat — ẹbọ scaled to the matter at hand, given at shrines that range from a shelf in a family compound to the great annual gatherings. Twice the year turns on the dead and the mothers. At Egúngún festivals the ancestors return, whirling in layered cloth from head to foot, blessing, teasing, and disciplining the town in the persons of its own grandfathers. At Gẹlẹdẹ — proclaimed by UNESCO in 2001 and inscribed on its Representative List in 2008 — masked dances honour àwọn ìyá wa, "our mothers," the women, living and ancestral, whose spiritual power the community flatters, thanks, and carefully keeps sweet-tempered. And at Òṣogbo, each August, the Osun-Osogbo festival draws multitudes to the sacred grove of Ọ̀ṣun on the river's bank — one of the last surviving sacred forests of Yorubaland, filled since the 1950s with the monumental shrines of the New Sacred Art movement gathered around the Austrian artist and devotee Susanne Wenger, and protected since 2005 as a UNESCO World Heritage site. Ilé-Ifẹ̀ keeps its own signature feast, Olojo, centred on Ògún and on the Ọọ̀ni's sacred crown. Kingship festivals, hunters' nights for Ògún, the Ṣàngó festival at Ọ̀yọ́ (itself UNESCO-listed in 2023): the calendar is crowded because the world it serves is crowded — with gods, with ancestors, with obligations that are also celebrations.

Across the water

No African religion crossed the Atlantic more completely. The Yoruba arrived in the Americas late and in great numbers — many of them prisoners of the very wars that destroyed Ọ̀yọ́ — and they arrived, crucially, in cities, where Cuba's cabildos and Brazil's brotherhoods let nation-based communities reassemble in the open what slavery had scattered. What grew there is told in Chapter 40: Cuban Santería, the Regla de Ocha, where the orishas — orichas — wear the faces of Catholic saints and Ifá survived in the hands of bàbáláwo who guarded its verses across the ocean; Brazilian Candomblé of the Ketu nation, which keeps Yoruba as a liturgical language in the terreiros of Bahia; Trinidad's Orisha tradition; and the Lucumí chants still sung in Havana by people who may speak no other word of Yoruba. In the twentieth century the current began to flow back: diaspora seekers travelling to Yorubaland for initiation, Nigerian priests travelling out, and experiments like Oyotunji African Village in South Carolina, founded in 1970 as a deliberate Yoruba kingdom on American soil. Scholars argue over how much of the diaspora faiths is survival and how much brilliant reinvention; what is not argued is the source. When a Havana bàbáláwo opens the tray, he is doing what was done at Ifẹ̀ — and he knows it, and says so.

Under pressure, and the living faith

The tradition at home has lived for two centuries between two missionary religions. Islam reached Yorubaland along the trade roads long before the Europeans came by sea; Christianity arrived in the nineteenth century with missions that built schools and condemned the shrines — and produced, in Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a Yoruba ex-slave who became the first African Anglican bishop and led the translation of the Bible into Yoruba, fixing the written language in the process. Colonial rule demoted the kings and their cults; the new faiths won the towns. By affiliation, most Yoruba today are Muslim or Christian, and the old religion's own numbers are honestly hard to state — practice runs wider than affiliation, in families that pray in mosque or church on Friday or Sunday and still pour out a little for the ancestors, still consult Ifá at a child's naming, still crowd Òṣogbo in August. Since Nigerian independence the tradition has also known revival and recognition: festivals as public heritage, UNESCO listings, scholars and priests publishing the corpus, and a global diaspora for whom "Yoruba religion" names not an abandonment but a root. What the pressure never achieved is what slavery could not achieve either: the system bent, hid, travelled, translated itself — and did not break.

The believer's lens

From inside, this is not a "belief system" but a working relationship with a populated world. The orishas are not symbols to the devotee; they are persons — fed, praised, argued with, and known by their tempers and their mercies. Ifá is not fortune-telling but counsel from the one witness who saw your destiny chosen; an ẹbọ is not a bribe but a restitution, energy returned to a world knocked off balance. The ancestor under the Egúngún cloth is grandfather, present and accountable. Practitioners point, reasonably, to the system's fruits as they experience them: illnesses carried to shrine and hospital alike, disputes settled, children named, kings restrained, a moral order whose first proverb puts character above beauty and above fate. And they note what the tradition asks of them is not credulity but conduct — ìwà — a demand that survives every argument about the rest.

The skeptical lens

From outside, the same record reads as human history all the way down. The creation cycle, the deified kings, the diviner's verses can be read — have been read, by scholars within Yorubaland as well as outside it — as the accumulated literature of a gifted civilisation: law, medicine, statecraft, and psychology encoded in story, centuries before print. The 256 odù work, on this reading, because a vast case-law of human situations, recited by a trained counsellor, will fit almost any trouble brought to it; sacrifice works because communities that feast and reconcile together are, measurably, communities repaired. The skeptical lens also keeps the honest questions open: how old, really, are the verses, and how much of Ifá's present form is nineteenth-century standardisation? Where does memory end and invention begin around Odùduwà? The tradition's own scholars ask these questions too, and the archive counts that a strength, not a scandal: a system confident enough to house its own critics.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The system's signs are few, strong, and endlessly recombined. The diviner's tray, the opón Ifá, is a carved wooden cosmos — its rim populated by animals, faces, and Èṣù himself, its dusted centre the page on which the odù are written and wiped away at every cast, scripture as performance rather than object. Ṣàngó's double axe, the ọ̀ṣẹ́, rides thunderstones — Neolithic axe-heads dug from the earth and kept as the god's fallen weapons. White is Ọbàtálá's colour and the colour of coolness, purity, and the elder gods; the orishas' bead colours — worn in strands called ìlẹ̀kẹ̀ — announce allegiance at a glance, in Lagos as in Havana. The head is the master-symbol: seat of orí and of àṣẹ, which is why Ifẹ̀'s sculptors lavished their genius on heads, why crowns are the most sacred objects a kingdom owns, and why the beaded crown of a Yoruba king hangs veiled with a fringe — a face that has become more than a face. And the crossroads, Èṣù's address, is the system's diagram of itself: every road of sacrifice, prayer, and consequence passes one junction, and someone must keep it.

Connections

Yoruba religion is this archive's missing keystone between two chapters that already exist. Behind it stands African Traditional Religion, the continental family — high god, intermediaries, ancestors, divination — of which the Yoruba system is the most elaborated and best-documented member. Ahead of it, across the water, stands African Diaspora Religions: Vodou's Legba is Èṣù's kin — strictly his Dahomean cousin, descended from Fon Legba, the two figures converging in the Atlantic world rather than being one and the same; Santería's orichas and Candomblé's orixás are this chapter's gods under Atlantic skies, and the Ifá of Havana is this chapter's library, carried in memory through the Middle Passage. Its sacred kingship — divine, and yet answerable to council and ancestor — belongs beside the archive's wider study of Sacred Kingship, and its creation, earth scattered on the first waters, beside Creation & the First Order. Its nearest royal neighbour, the Edo kingdom of Benin — bound to Ifẹ̀ by the Òrànmíyàn tradition and famed for its own bronzes — has its own entry in the Vault: The Benin Bronzes. Few single traditions let a reader watch a religion be born in a city, crown kings, survive an ocean, and argue with two world faiths — and remain, through all of it, recognisably itself. This one does.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The existence, structure, and living practice of the Yoruba religious system — Olódùmarè, the orishas, the ancestors, àṣẹ, orí — documented by two centuries of scholarship and by practitioners today; the Ifá corpus of 256 odù and its verses, and its UNESCO recognition (proclaimed 2005, inscribed 2008); the art and urban civilisation of Ilé-Ifẹ̀, its naturalistic heads dated to the twelfth–fifteenth centuries by archaeology and art history; the rise and fall of the Ọ̀yọ́ empire (imperial peak seventeenth–eighteenth centuries, capital abandoned by the 1830s); the Gẹlẹdẹ and Osun-Osogbo traditions and their UNESCO listings (Gẹlẹdẹ proclaimed 2001, inscribed 2008; the grove World Heritage 2005), and the Ṣàngó Festival's (2023); and the Yoruba foundations of Santería, Candomblé, and Trinidad Orisha, among the best-documented religious survivals of the Atlantic slave trade.

Not supported by the evidence

The claim — made by the German explorer Leo Frobenius after encountering Ifẹ̀'s art in 1910 — that the heads must be the work of ancient Greeks, or of Atlantis: a colonial-era refusal of African authorship, contradicted by the archaeology, the local casting tradition, and the heads' own Yoruba features, and now retained only as evidence about its makers. The literal historicity of the creation account and of Odùduwà's descent, which the tradition holds as sacred truth but which no evidence dates or documents as events. The reading of Èṣù as "the devil" — a missionary mistranslation that the tradition's own theology refutes: Èṣù is messenger and enforcer within one God's order, in a system with no evil god at all. And any precise census of the faith's antiquity or of its present-day practitioners: the tradition is certainly old and certainly widespread, but specific numbers offered for either are not supported by evidence of the same quality.

Genuinely open

Who Odùduwà was — god, first king, deified ancestor, or a figure in whom all three readings have fused (some recorded tellings make Odùduwà female, a goddess or consort figure; a standing scholarly thesis, associated with Obayemi and Afigbo, reads the historical Odùduwà as a conqueror or immigrant ruler from the east whose arrival reorganised Ifẹ̀ — disputed, and unproven either way) — and when the Ifẹ̀ traditions took their classical shape; the true age and layering of the Ifá corpus, an oral library still growing, whose oldest verses cannot be dated the way a manuscript can; the exact nature of the early Ifẹ̀–Benin relationship behind the Òrànmíyàn tradition; how possession practice in Yorubaland itself has changed under diaspora influence flowing back; and the real demography of the living faith, where affiliation, practice, and heritage overlap in ways no survey has yet cleanly separated. What is not open is the tradition's depth, its documentation, or its dignity.
