Early Modern · African Traditional Religion

African Traditional Religion

In the archive

The vast family of indigenous religions of sub-Saharan Africa — a Supreme God above a world of intermediary spirits and living ancestors, read through divination and danced in masks — and the extraordinary story of how, in the colonial and slave-trade centuries, these faiths were carried across the Atlantic in the memories of the enslaved and survived, hidden inside the saints of their captors.

The seed that would not die

When a Yoruba man or woman was chained into the hold of a slave ship on the Bight of Benin in the seventeenth or eighteenth century, they were stripped of nearly everything — name, home, language, kin. But one thing could not be taken: what they carried in the mind. They remembered the orishas, the deities of thunder and river and iron; they remembered the wisdom of Ifá divination and the reverence owed the ancestors. On the far shore, forbidden their gods and driven toward the cross, they did something quietly astonishing: they matched their orishas to the Catholic saints — Shango, lord of thunder, to Saint Barbara; Ogun, god of iron, to Saint Peter with his keys — and worshipped the old powers behind the new faces. Out of that concealment were born Santería, Candomblé, and Vodou, and the African gods crossed an ocean that was meant to erase them.

This chapter is about the religions those people carried, and it must begin with a caution: "African traditional religion" is not one thing but hundreds, spread across a continent, mostly oral, and known to us largely through the records of the colonizers, missionaries, and anthropologists who often despised or romanticized what they described. What follows traces the deep family resemblances while remembering how varied, and how imperfectly recorded, the reality was.

A shared shape

Beneath the immense diversity, many sub-Saharan traditions share a recognizable structure. Above all stands a Supreme Creator God — Olodumare among the Yoruba, Nyame among the Akan, Nzambi in the Kongo — who is often remote, having withdrawn from the daily running of the world. Between that high God and humanity move a crowd of lesser divinities and spirits who are close and active: the Yoruba orishas, the Fon vodun, the powers of rivers, mountains, forests, and storms. Closest of all are the ancestors — the honored dead, who remain members of the family, watching, helping, and requiring remembrance. The living stay in right relation with all of them through offering, festival, and, above all, divination — the reading of the hidden will of the spirits through ritual specialists. There is, characteristically, no scripture: the tradition lives in memory, proverb, song, mask, and the trained diviner's art.

The great traditions

  • The Yoruba of what is now Nigeria and Benin built the most elaborate of these systems: hundreds of orishas, each with its own colors, foods, drums, and character, and the sophisticated Ifá divination overseen by the wisdom-orisha Orunmila — a corpus of hundreds of verses read from a binary system of signs. It is the Yoruba cosmos, more than any other, that crossed the Atlantic.
  • The Fon of Dahomey worshipped the vodun, the spirits whose name became "Vodou."
  • The Akan of the Gold Coast honored Nyame the sky-god and Asase Yaa the earth, and encoded their proverbs in the Adinkra symbols.
  • The Kongo and wider Bantu peoples of Central Africa held a cosmology of the two worlds — the living and the dead — divided by water, and of powerful ritual objects (the minkisi) that concentrated spirit; their ideas would shape the diaspora as deeply as the Yoruba's.

The southern and eastern reaches

The Atlantic story centers on West Africa, but the traditional religions run the length of the continent, and the same deep shape recurs in different dress. Among the Zulu and wider Nguni peoples of Southern Africa stands uNkulunkulu, “the great-great one,” the creator, above the ever-present amadlozi — the ancestors, the “living-dead,” who are the family’s nearest link to the spirit world and are honored, not worshipped as gods. The Zulu diviner, the isangoma, is “called” to the vocation through an initiation illness (ukuthwasa) and thereafter speaks for the ancestors, reads dreams, and heals with herbs. Older still are the San (“Bushmen”) of the Kalahari, whose central rite is the trance dance: the community claps and sings through the night while healers dance into trance, feeling the boiling healing energy (num) rise up the spine, and channel it to cure the sick and mend the community — a practice many scholars connect to the trance imagery painted on Southern African rock for thousands of years. And among the Nilotic peoples of the upper Nile — the Nuer and Dinka of South Sudan, famously studied by Evans-Pritchard and Godfrey Lienhardt — a lofty sky-and-rain High God (Nuer Kuoth, Dinka Nhialic) is approached through sacrifice and the spirit-powers, in one of the purest of Africa’s monotheistic-tending systems.

Across the water

The transatlantic slave trade — which tore an estimated 12.5 million people from West and West-Central Africa between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries — became, against every intention, one of the great engines of religious transmission. Forbidden their rites and pressed to convert, the enslaved practiced a strategic syncretism: they hid their orishas and vodun behind the masks of Catholic saints, folded African drumming and possession-trance into the calendar of the Church, and preserved the essentials of divination, initiation, and ancestor-honor. From this concealment emerged the enduring Afro-Atlantic religions — Santería (Regla de Ocha–Ifá) in Cuba, Candomblé in Brazil, Vodou in Haiti, and the traditions of New Orleans — living faiths in which the African gods are worshipped still. (Their modern forms are treated more fully among the diaspora syntheses of the next era.)

Ritual and the body

Where these religions are most themselves is in ritual, and ritual here is danced, drummed, and embodied. The defining act of worship across much of the family is spirit possession: through rhythmic drumming and dance a deity "mounts" a devotee, who becomes for a time the living presence of the god, speaking and moving as the orisha or vodun. Initiation binds a person to a particular deity for life; sacrifice and offering feed the spirits and the ancestors; and masks and masquerade make the invisible powers visible in festival. This is a religion not primarily of belief stated in creeds but of relationship enacted with the body and the community.

Witchcraft and the reading of misfortune

For many of these traditions, misfortune is never merely bad luck — it has a cause, and often that cause is witchcraft. The classic account is E. E. Evans-Pritchard’s study of the Azande of Central Africa (Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande, 1937), still one of anthropology’s most influential books. For the Azande, witchcraft (mangu) was thought to be a physical substance in the body that could harm others even without the witch’s conscious intent; it explained not how a granary collapsed — termites did that — but why it fell on this person at this moment. To find the responsible witch the Azande consulted oracles, above all the poison oracle (benge): a substance was fed to a chicken and the bird’s living or dying answered the question put to it. This was not superstition floating free but a coherent, self-checking system of explanation, and it named a real social role — the diviner who detects witchcraft and the specialist who counters it. It must be held with the same care as the neighboring witch-trials chapter: to describe a people’s belief in witchcraft is to document what they held, not to endorse that anyone actually bewitched anyone — and, unlike Europe’s hunts, African anti-witchcraft was usually aimed at healing the affliction rather than mass burning.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Africa's traditional religions are rich in encoded systems — and one of them is among the most remarkable in the world.

  • Ifá divination is a genuine information system. The diviner casts palm nuts or a divining chain and reads the result as one of 256 figures (the odu), each a distinct pattern of marks that is, in effect, an eight-bit binary signature — and each of which unlocks a vast body of memorized verses. It is a sacred encoding of the cosmos in binary, a corpus so significant that UNESCO named it a masterpiece of human heritage.
  • The Akan Adinkra symbols are a visual language of proverbs: each stamped emblem — Gye Nyame, "except God"; Sankofa, the backward-looking bird — encodes a maxim of wisdom and belief.
  • The Nsibidi ideograms of the Cross River peoples formed an indigenous script of signs, some sacred and secret, known to initiates.
  • In the diaspora, these encodings became the vèvè of Vodou — intricate ground-drawings that summon a particular lwa — and the color-and-attribute codes by which each orisha is known.

Connections

African traditional religion belongs, in its structures, to the oldest layer of human belief — the ancestor-cult and spirit-world that the Prehistory chapter reaches toward, still living. In the colonial centuries it collided with, resisted, and partly fused with the expanding Christianity and Islam of the age (much of the Sahel had already turned to Islam; the coasts and the Americas met the cross). Its transplantation across the Atlantic makes it the direct parent of the diaspora religions of the modern era — Santería, Candomblé, Vodou, and, further on, Rastafari — and one of the deepest and least acknowledged roots of the religious life of the whole Americas.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The broad shared structures of sub-Saharan traditional religion (a high creator God, intermediary spirits, powerful ancestors, divination, and possession); the specific great systems (Yoruba orisha and Ifá, Fon vodun, Akan, Kongo); the scale and route of the transatlantic slave trade (~12.5 million people); and the historical formation of the Afro-Atlantic religions through syncretism with Catholicism. The Southern and Eastern traditions (Zulu uNkulunkulu and the amadlozi, the isangoma; the San trance dance; the Nilotic Nuer and Dinka High Gods) and the well-studied role of witchcraft belief and oracular divination (Evans-Pritchard’s Azande) are firmly attested in the ethnographic record.

Not supported — colonial distortions

The old labels of "fetishism," "animism," and "ancestor worship" as European verdicts often carried contempt and misunderstanding; the ancestors, for instance, are honored and consulted, not worshipped as gods. And the picture of a single, static "African religion" is a colonial simplification of enormous diversity.

Genuinely open

Because these traditions were oral and are known largely through outside and often hostile observers, the pre-colonial detail of many is genuinely uncertain; reconstructing what was believed before the missionaries and anthropologists arrived — and how much the act of writing it down changed it — is a standing difficulty. The exact African origins of particular diaspora practices are still actively debated.
