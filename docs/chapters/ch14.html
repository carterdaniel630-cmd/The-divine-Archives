Axial Age · Celtic & Germanic

Celtic & Germanic

In the archive

The Celtic and Germanic peoples left cauldrons and bog bodies, gods’ names and sacred groves, and an art of extraordinary power — but wrote down almost none of their own beliefs. They are, more than any tradition so far, religions without a book, and reconstructing them is an exercise in honesty about how much we cannot know.

A cauldron in a bog, a body in the peat

In 1891, Danish peat-cutters lifted out a great silver cauldron, dismantled and laid down with care two thousand years before. Its panels show an antlered figure sitting among the beasts holding a torc and a ram-horned serpent, a giant hand plunging a man head-first into a vat, processions and a wheel-god. The Gundestrup cauldron is one of the richest religious objects to survive from the ancient Celts — and we do not know for certain which gods it shows, what the scenes mean, or who made it. A century later, in a Cheshire bog, cutters found Lindow Man — a young Iron Age Briton struck on the head, garroted, and his throat cut before he was laid face-down in the water: three deaths in one, an overkill many read as ritual.

These finds are the doorway into this chapter, because they show both the vividness and the silence of what it covers.

The problem of the sources (read this first)

The method governs everything. For Celtic and Germanic religion we have three kinds of evidence, all treacherous. First, the outsiders — nearly all contemporary testimony comes from Greek and Roman writers (Caesar, Tacitus, Posidonius, Strabo, Lucan, Pliny) describing enemies, often to justify conquest, usually without the language, fitting foreign gods into Roman categories; Caesar’s account of druids burning victims in a giant wicker man may be propaganda or truth. Second, the much later native literature — the Irish Ulster Cycle and the Welsh Mabinogi, written by Christian monks a thousand years or more after the Iron Age; they preserve old material, but filtered through Christianity and literary reshaping. Third, archaeology — our most direct evidence, but mute: it shows swords thrown into rivers and bodies laid in bogs, but not the beliefs behind them. Where these witnesses fall silent — which is often — the honest answer is that we do not know.

The Celts: druids, groves, and a religion of memory

The peoples the Greeks and Romans called Keltoi and Galli, carriers of the La Tène culture, administered their religion through a learned class the sources call the druids. They were not merely priests but the judges, teachers, philosophers, and loremasters of society, training up to twenty years — and, Caesar reports, they held it forbidden to commit their learning to writing, memorizing vast quantities of verse instead. That is precisely why this is a religion without a book. They taught, he says, that the soul does not perish but passes from one to another at death. Worship was largely outdoors, in sacred groves called a nemeton (Strabo records a Galatian Drunemeton, “oak-sanctuary”), and lavish with votive deposit: swords, cauldrons, and treasure cast into lakes and bogs — the sword in the water that echoes down to Excalibur.

The Celtic gods

The Celtic divine world is a crowd of mostly local names: Lugh/Lugus (a shining god of many skills, marking places from Lyon to Leiden), the Dagda (the “good god”), Brigid (poetry, healing, smithcraft), Cernunnos (the antlered lord of beasts, likely the Gundestrup figure), Taranis (the thunderer with his wheel), Teutates and Esus (named by Lucan as demanding bloody sacrifice), and Epona (the horse-goddess, adopted even by Roman cavalry). Two patterns matter: a love of triplication — gods, especially goddesses, in threes, like the Matres — and the fusion of Celtic and Roman gods under Rome (Apollo Grannus, Mars Lenus). There is no tidy Celtic pantheon; there is a vast, regional, overlapping family of powers.

The Germanic peoples: Tacitus’s witness

For early Germanic religion our key source is Tacitus, whose Germania (98 CE) reports — from the outside — that the Germani did not confine their gods within walls or in human form, but consecrated groves and woods. He names their gods only through Roman equivalents: they worship Mercury above all, with human sacrifice on certain days, and Hercules and Mars with animal offerings. Behind these masks stand the gods we know better in later Norse form: Mercury is Wodan/Odin, Mars is Tiwaz/Tyr the war-god, Hercules is Thunor/Thor the thunderer. Tacitus also preserves the worship of Nerthus, “Mother Earth,” whose veiled image was drawn in a cart through the tribes in peace, then washed in a secret lake — after which the slaves who washed it were drowned.

An important boundary. The rich mythology most people associate with this world — Odin on the world-tree, Ragnarök, the Eddas — comes from medieval Iceland, after the conversion, and belongs to the later Norse paganism chapter. Reading it back onto Tacitus’s tribes is exactly the error the sources warn against.

Blood in the water: sacrifice and the bog

Both traditions practiced sacrifice. Classical writers accuse the Celts of human sacrifice (Caesar’s wicker man, Lucan’s Teutates and Esus, Pliny on Britain); these hostile reports are suspect and most scholars judge such sacrifice occasional rather than routine — but the archaeology will not let the question be dismissed, for the bog bodies (Lindow Man above all) show individuals killed with ritual excess and deposited in water. On the Germanic side, the great weapon deposits — thousands of destroyed swords and shields thrown into bogs at Illerup Ådal and Nydam, the sacrificed gear of defeated armies — are among Europe’s most spectacular sacrificial finds. Water, in both worlds, was a doorway to the divine.

The otherworld and the festival year

The Celts believed in an Otherworld, reachable through mounds, caves, and lakes, and especially close at certain turnings of the year. The (late, carefully-used) Irish sources preserve four great festivals that have passed into modern life: Samhain (1 Nov, the dark threshold when the Otherworld drew near — ancestor of Halloween), Imbolc (1 Feb, of Brigid and early spring), Beltane (1 May, fire-festival of summer’s start), and Lughnasadh (1 Aug, harvest festival of Lugh). How much is genuinely Iron Age and how much medieval elaboration is debated — but the sense of a year hinged on the turns of light and season is real.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Two sacred scripts — with an honest caveat about their date. Each world produced a writing system freighted with sacred meaning, but both are later than the core Axial Age. The Germanic runes (the Elder Futhark) appear from about the 2nd century CE; Norse tradition made them a divine gift won by Odin on the world-tree, and they served as objects of magic and divination, not just an alphabet — the word rune itself carries a sense of “secret.” The Celtic ogham, an alphabet of notches along a stem-line associated with trees, appears later still, around the 4th century CE. Neither was the vehicle of Iron Age religion, which stayed oral by design; both show how, once writing came, the letter itself was treated as charged with power.

The art of the sacred spiral. Long before either script, La Tène art encoded a sensibility: endless interlacing spirals, triskeles, and shape-shifting forms in which faces emerge and dissolve. This refusal of fixed representation is itself a kind of theology, and the triple motif points to the deep Celtic reverence for threeness. The severed head was, to the Celts, the seat of the soul and a potent sacred object — carved stone heads and skull-niches appear at sanctuaries like Roquepertuse, and the head recurs through Celtic art and Irish story as a source of power and prophecy.

A calendar in bronze. The most remarkable Celtic sacred artifact is the Coligny calendar, a fragmentary bronze tablet from Roman Gaul (2nd century CE, encoding an older system) laying out a five-year lunisolar cycle in Gaulish, reconciling moon and sun and marking days as MAT (auspicious) or ANM (not) — a druidic ritual almanac frozen in metal, our best window into how the Celts encoded sacred time.

Connections

Both are branches of the great Indo-European family running through this archive — their sky-gods, thunder-gods, priestly loremasters, and fire-and-water sacrifices are cousins of the Vedic (Chapter 5), Greek (Chapter 8), and Roman (Chapter 13) worlds; ancient writers themselves compared the druids to the brahmins and magi. Within the Axial Age these are the traditions of Europe’s edge — non-literate, tribal, grove-worshipping — the oldest-feeling counterpoint to the era’s textual transformations. Forward, the Germanic thread flowers into the fully-recorded Norse paganism of the early medieval north; the Celtic thread survives folded into Irish and Welsh literature and folk practice; and both are reborn in the modern era as revival Druidry and Heathenry — later chapters of this work.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • A non-literate, largely outdoor religion centered on sacred groves and waters, with votive deposition of weapons and treasure (abundantly confirmed by archaeology) and a learned Celtic priesthood, the druids.
  • The reality of sacrifice, including at least occasional human sacrifice — the bog bodies (Lindow Man) and mass weapon deposits (Illerup, Nydam) — and the named gods (Lugh, Cernunnos, Taranis, Epona; Wodan, Tiwaz, Thunor, Nerthus).
  • The Coligny calendar as a genuine Gaulish lunisolar ritual calendar; the later appearance of runes (c. 2nd c. CE) and ogham (c. 4th c. CE); the Celtic reverence for threeness and the head.

What’s not supported

  • Confident reconstructions of Celtic or Germanic theology and myth for this period — the vivid “Celtic/Germanic mythology” often presented draws heavily on much later Irish, Welsh, and Norse texts.
  • Reading the medieval Icelandic Eddas back onto the Axial-Age Germani (that belongs to the later Norse chapter).
  • The classical claims of routine, mass human sacrifice (the wicker man) as reliable fact; and the popular image of druids as builders of Stonehenge (millennia older than the Celts).

What’s genuinely open

  • How much of the later Irish and Welsh literature preserves authentic pre-Christian religion versus medieval Christian reshaping.
  • The frequency, meaning, and context of human sacrifice and the bog bodies.
  • Whether there was any single “Celtic religion” or “Germanic religion” at all — and how meaningful the label “Celtic” itself is, now much debated; and the true antiquity of the four festivals.

This is the archive’s most source-critical chapter by necessity: it foregrounds the unreliability of the three witnesses (hostile classical writers, much-later Christian-era vernacular texts, mute archaeology), keeps the well-attested (groves, votive deposits, bog bodies, named gods, the Coligny calendar) separate from the reconstructed (systematic myth), reserves the Norse Eddic material for the early-medieval chapter, honestly dates the runes and ogham to later centuries, and flags the genuinely open questions — including whether a single “Celtic religion,” or even the label “Celtic,” is meaningful. Written to be educational, with attention to how much we cannot know about religions that, by design, kept no book.
