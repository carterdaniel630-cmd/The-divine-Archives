Celtic & Germanic

In the archive

Tradition chapter · Era 04 — Axial Age · DRAFT — READY FOR REVIEW ---

A cauldron in a bog, a body in the peat

In 1891, peat-cutters in a Danish bog lifted out a great silver cauldron, dismantled and laid down with care nearly two thousand years before. Its panels, worked in gleaming detail, show a world of gods: an antlered figure sitting cross-legged among the beasts, holding a torc and a ram-horned serpent; a giant hand plunging a small human head-first into a vat; processions of warriors, a wheel-god, women with braided hair. The Gundestrup cauldron is one of the richest religious objects to survive from the world of the ancient Celts — and almost everything about it is uncertain. We do not know for sure which gods it shows, what the scenes mean, who made it, or how it came to be sacrificed into a northern bog far from the Celtic heartland.

A century later and a few hundred miles west, in a Cheshire peat bog, other cutters found Lindow Man — a young Iron Age Briton, his last meal of charred bread still in his gut, who had been struck on the head, garroted, and had his throat cut before being laid face-down in the water. Three deaths in one: overkill that many archaeologists read as ritual, a human being given to the bog.

These two finds are the perfect doorway into this chapter, because they show both the vividness and the silence of the traditions it covers. The Celtic and Germanic peoples of Iron Age Europe left behind cauldrons and bog bodies, gods' names and sacred groves, and an art of extraordinary power — but they wrote down almost none of their own beliefs. They are, more than any traditions in this archive so far, religions without a book, and reconstructing them is an exercise in honesty about how much we cannot know.

The problem of the sources (read this first)

Before a single god is named, the method has to be stated plainly, because it governs everything. For Celtic and Germanic religion in this era we have three kinds of evidence, and all three are treacherous.

First, the outsiders. Nearly all our contemporary written testimony comes from Greek and Roman authors — Julius Caesar, Tacitus, Posidonius, Strabo, Lucan, Pliny — who were describing enemies and barbarians, often to justify conquest, usually without the language, and frequently by fitting foreign gods into Roman categories. Caesar's Gauls conveniently have Roman gods; his account of the druids burning victims in a giant wicker man may be propaganda, borrowed hearsay, or true. These sources are indispensable and must be read with deep suspicion at once.

Second, the much later native literature. The great vernacular texts — the Irish Ulster Cycle and Mythological Cycle, the Welsh Mabinogi — were written down by Christian monks a thousand years or more after the Iron Age. They preserve genuinely old material, but filtered through Christianity, literary reshaping, and centuries of change; using them to reconstruct pre-Roman religion is legitimate only with great care, and scholars disagree sharply about how much weight they bear.

Third, archaeology — the sacred sites, votive deposits, bog bodies, coins, and art. This is our most direct evidence, but it is mute: it shows us that swords were thrown into rivers and bodies laid in bogs, but not the prayers said over them or the beliefs behind them.

Everything that follows is built from these three unreliable witnesses, cross-checked where possible. Where they fall silent — which is often — the honest answer is that we do not know.

The Celts: druids, groves, and a religion of memory

The peoples the Greeks and Romans called Keltoi and Galli spread across much of Iron Age Europe, carriers of the La Tène culture and its swirling art. Their religion, as far as we can see it, was administered by a learned class the sources call the druids — and here Caesar is our fullest witness. The druids were not merely priests; they were the judges, teachers, philosophers, and loremasters of Celtic society, adjudicating disputes, presiding over sacrifices, and training for as long as twenty years. Crucially, Caesar reports that they held it forbidden to commit their learning to writing, memorizing vast quantities of verse instead — which is precisely why their religion is a religion without a book. They taught, he says, that the soul does not perish but passes from one to another at death, a doctrine ancient writers compared (rightly or not) to the transmigration taught by Pythagoras.

The classical sources actually describe a threefold learned class, not a single one. Strabo, drawing on Posidonius, distinguishes the druids proper (the philosophers, judges, and overseers of sacrifice), the bards (poets and singers who kept genealogy, law, and praise alive in memorized verse), and the vates (Gaulish velets, the seers and diviners who read the sacrifices) — a division that modern revival Druidry, in the final era of this archive, would consciously restore as its three grades.

Worship was largely outdoors, in sacred groves the Celts called a nemeton — Strabo records a Galatian assembly-place named Drunemeton, "the oak-sanctuary." There were also temples and shrines, especially later under Roman influence, but the grove, the spring, and the river were the primal holy places. And Celtic religion was lavish with votive deposit: swords, shields, cauldrons, and treasure cast deliberately into lakes, rivers, and bogs as offerings — the sword in the water that echoes, distantly, down to the legend of Excalibur.

The Celtic gods

The Celtic divine world is a crowd of names, most of them local. Classical and later sources, and hundreds of inscriptions, give us figures such as Lugh (the Irish Lugh, the Gaulish Lugus, a shining god of many skills, whose name marks places from Lyon to Leiden), the Dagda (the "good god," a club-wielding father-figure of Irish myth), Brigid (goddess of poetry, healing, and smithcraft), Cernunnos (the antlered lord of beasts, likely the figure on the Gundestrup cauldron), Taranis (the thunderer, with his wheel), Teutates and Esus (named by Lucan as gods demanding bloody sacrifice), Epona (the horse-goddess, so appealing that Roman cavalry adopted her), Belenus, Sucellus, and Maponos. Two patterns recur and matter: a love of triplication — gods and especially goddesses appearing in threes, like the Matres or "Mothers" carved across the Roman-Celtic world — and the fusion, under Rome, of Celtic and Roman gods (Apollo Grannus, Mars Lenus) through interpretatio. There is no Celtic pantheon in the tidy Greek sense; there is a vast, regional, overlapping family of powers.

The Germanic peoples: Tacitus's witness

East and north of the Celts lay the Germanic tribes, and for their early religion our single most important source is the Roman historian Tacitus, whose Germania (98 CE) describes their gods and rites — again, from the outside, and again to be read warily. Tacitus reports that the Germani did not confine their gods within walls or represent them in human form, but consecrated groves and woods and worshipped in the open. He names their gods only through Roman equivalents (interpretatio romana): they worship Mercury above all, to whom on certain days they offer human sacrifice, and Hercules and Mars with animal offerings. Behind these Roman masks stand the Germanic gods we know better in their later Norse forms: Mercury is Wodan/Odin, Mars is Tiwaz/Tyr the war-god, Hercules is Thunor/Donar/Thor the thunderer.

Tacitus also preserves one unforgettable scene: the worship of Nerthus, "Mother Earth," a goddess whose veiled image was drawn in a cart through the tribes in a time of peace and rejoicing, then washed in a secret lake — after which the slaves who performed the washing were drowned. Whatever its exact truth, it captures the character of the religion the Romans met at their frontier: earth-goddesses and war-gods, sacred groves, processions, and blood.

Tacitus adds one more revealing detail: the Germani believed their women held something holy and prophetic, and heeded their counsel. He names the seeress Veleda of the Bructeri, who during the Batavian revolt against Rome lived secluded in a tower, delivered oracles no petitioner was allowed to approach directly, and was revered almost as a goddess — and an earlier prophetess, Aurinia (or Albruna). The venerated Germanic seeress is one of the very few religious roles the Roman sources let us see with any clarity, and it points forward to the völva, the staff-bearing prophetess of the later Norse world.

An important boundary. The rich mythology most people associate with the Germanic world — Odin hanging on the world-tree, Ragnarök, the Nine Worlds, the Eddas — comes from medieval Iceland, written down after the conversion, and belongs to the later Norse paganism chapter of this archive. Reading it back onto the tribes of Tacitus's day is exactly the error the sources warn against. What we can responsibly say of the Axial-Age Germani is far sparser: gods under Roman names, groves, sacrifice, and votive deposit.

Blood in the water: sacrifice and the bog

Both traditions practiced sacrifice, and both are linked to one of archaeology's most haunting classes of evidence. Classical writers accuse the Celts of human sacrifice — Caesar's wicker man, Lucan's blood-hungry Teutates and Esus, Pliny on Britain — and while these hostile reports are suspect and most modern scholars judge human sacrifice to have been occasional rather than routine, the archaeology does not let the question be dismissed: the bog bodies of northern Europe, of whom Lindow Man is the most studied, show individuals killed with ritual excess and deposited in water, exactly the kind of act the texts describe. On the Continent, excavation has put flesh on the classical accusations: at the great war-sanctuaries of northern Gaul — Gournay-sur-Aronde and Ribemont-sur-Ancre in Picardy, excavated by Jean-Louis Brunaux — archaeologists found ditched enclosures heaped with thousands of ritually destroyed weapons and, at Ribemont, the decapitated remains of hundreds of young men, apparently the war-dead of a defeated enemy displayed and offered to the gods. Whatever the precise rite, these northern Gallic sanctuaries are among the most direct evidence of Celtic sacrificial practice we have. On the Germanic side, the great weapon deposits — thousands of destroyed swords, spears, and shields thrown into lakes and bogs at sites like Illerup Ådal and Nydam, apparently the sacrificed gear of defeated armies — are among the most spectacular sacrificial finds in Europe. Water, in both worlds, was a doorway to the divine, and offerings were sent through it.

The otherworld and the festival year

The Celts believed in an Otherworld — a realm of the dead and the gods, reachable through burial mounds, caves, lakes, and the mist, and especially close at certain turnings of the year. The Irish sources (late, and to be used with care) preserve a ritual calendar of four great festivals that has passed into modern life: Samhain (1 November, the year's dark threshold when the Otherworld drew near — the distant ancestor of Halloween), Imbolc (1 February, associated with Brigid and the first stirrings of spring), Beltane (1 May, fire-festival of the summer's start), and Lughnasadh (1 August, the harvest festival of Lugh). How much of this festival year is genuinely Iron Age and how much is medieval Irish elaboration is debated — but the underlying sense of a year hinged on the turning points of light and season is real.

The believer's lens

Reconstructed carefully from fragments, the world of the Celtic and Germanic peoples was one saturated with the sacred in the land — in groves and springs, rivers and bogs, the thresholds between this world and the Otherworld that lay always close by. Religion was bound up with reciprocity and the local: gifts to the powers (offerings cast into water, the great deposits of weapons and treasure — and, at times, of lives) to secure victory, fertility, and protection; festivals turning the wheel of the year; and a learned class — the Celtic druids, bards, and vates — who held the memory, law, and lore that were never written down but carried in trained memory. Fate loomed large, and honor with it: to die well, to keep faith, to feast in the hall. It was not a religion of scripture or salvation but of place, ancestor, and season, of a world alive with presences to be honored and a boundary to the Otherworld that the right rites, in the right places, could cross.

The skeptical lens

No tradition in this book must be handled more warily, because the Celts and Germans wrote almost nothing down, and nearly all our testimony comes from hostile or interested outsiders — Julius Caesar, who fought the Gauls and had reasons to paint their druids as savage; Tacitus, moralizing about noble Germans to shame his fellow Romans; and, for the myths, medieval Christian scribes (Irish monks, Icelandic antiquarians) writing centuries after conversion. So the lurid set-pieces (wicker-man burnings, mass human sacrifice) are exactly where skepticism must bite hardest, even as archaeology — the bog bodies, the war-booty sanctuaries of Gournay and Ribemont, the votive deposits — confirms that sacrifice, including occasional human sacrifice, was real. The "Celtic religion" of popular imagination is largely a modern Romantic and Neo-pagan construction. The honest scholar affirms what the ground and the (biased) sources jointly support — sacred groves and waters, votive offering, a learned priesthood, a cult of the head, seasonal festivals — and leaves the rest, including most of the "druid theology," frankly uncertain.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Two sacred scripts — and an honest caveat about their date. The Celtic and Germanic worlds each produced a writing system freighted with sacred meaning, but both are later than the core Axial Age and must be dated honestly. The Germanic runes (the Elder Futhark) appear in inscriptions from about the 2nd century CE; Norse tradition later made them a divine gift, won by Odin through his self-sacrifice on the world-tree, and they served not only as an alphabet but as objects of magic and divination, carved for protection, cursing, and casting — the very word rune carries a sense of "secret." The Celtic ogham — an alphabet of notches and strokes along a stem-line, associated in Irish tradition with trees and used in a body of name-lore — appears later still, around the 4th century CE. Neither script, then, was the vehicle of Iron Age religion (which stayed oral by design); both show how, once writing did come, these cultures treated the letter itself as charged with power.

The gods hidden in the week. One piece of this old encoding sits on every calendar. When the Germanic peoples adopted the Roman seven-day planetary week, they translated its gods into their own by interpretatio, and English still carries the result: Tuesday is Tiw's (Tyr's) day, Wednesday Woden's (Odin's) day, Thursday Thunor's (Thor's) day, and Friday the day of the goddess Frig — the war-god, the sky-and-wisdom god, the thunderer, and the love-goddess, each matched to the Roman deity they most resembled (Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus). The names of the Axial-Age Germani's gods survive, unnoticed, in the ordinary words for the days.

The art of the sacred spiral. Long before either script, La Tène art encoded a sensibility rather than a message: endless interlacing spirals, triskeles (the three-armed whirl), and shape-shifting animal and vegetal forms, in which faces emerge and dissolve. This restless, ambiguous style — refusing fixed representation — is itself a kind of theology, and the triple motif (the triskele, the three-faced heads, the triadic goddesses) points to the deep Celtic reverence for threeness.

The severed head. To the Celts the head was the seat of the soul and a potent sacred object: classical writers describe warriors taking and preserving enemies' heads, carved stone heads and skull-niches appear at Celtic sanctuaries (as at Roquepertuse in southern Gaul), and the head recurs throughout Celtic art and later Irish story as a source of power, prophecy, and even conversation after death.

A calendar in bronze. The single most remarkable Celtic sacred artifact is the Coligny calendar, a fragmentary bronze tablet from Roman Gaul (2nd century CE, but encoding an older system) that lays out a five-year lunisolar cycle in the Gaulish language, reconciling moon and sun with intercalary months and marking days as MAT ("good," auspicious) or ANM(ATU) ("not good") — a druidic ritual almanac frozen in metal, and our best direct window into how the Celts encoded sacred time.

Connections

Celtic and Germanic religion are branches of the great Indo-European family that runs through this archive: their sky-gods, their thunder-gods, their priestly loremasters, and their fire-and-water sacrifices are cousins of the Vedic world (Chapter 5), the Greek (Chapter 8), and the Roman (Chapter 13) — ancient writers themselves compared the druids to the brahmins and the magi, and the comparison is apt. Within the Axial Age, these are the traditions of Europe's edge — non-literate, tribal, grove-worshipping — standing beside the era's great textual and philosophical transformations as their oldest-feeling counterpoint. Forward, their stories run deep into this archive: the Germanic thread flowers into the fully-recorded Norse paganism of the early medieval north; the Celtic thread survives Christianization folded into Irish and Welsh literature and folk practice; and both are reborn, in the modern era, as self-conscious new religious movements — revival Druidry and Heathenry — that belong to the final chapters of this work.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What's well supported by the evidence

- That the Iron Age Celtic and Germanic peoples practiced a non-literate, largely outdoor religion centered on sacred groves and waters, with votive deposition of weapons and treasure (abundantly confirmed by archaeology) and a learned priestly class among the Celts, the druids. - The reality of sacrifice, including at least occasional human sacrifice, evidenced by the bog bodies (Lindow Man), the excavated northern-Gallic war-sanctuaries (Gournay-sur-Aronde, Ribemont-sur-Ancre), and the mass weapon deposits (Illerup, Nydam), and described (with bias) by classical writers. - The classical sources' threefold Celtic learned class — druids, bards, and vates (Strabo/Posidonius) — and the Germanic reverence for prophetic seeresses (Tacitus names Veleda and Aurinia). The Germanic gods survive in the English weekday names (Tiw, Woden, Thunor, Frig). - The existence of the named gods (Lugh/Lugus, Cernunnos, Taranis, Epona; the Germanic Wodan, Tiwaz, Thunor, Nerthus) from inscriptions and Tacitus, and the reverence for threeness and the head in Celtic art. - The Coligny calendar as a genuine Gaulish lunisolar ritual calendar, and the later appearance of runes (c. 2nd c. CE) and ogham (c. 4th c. CE).

What's not supported by the evidence

- Detailed, confident reconstructions of Celtic or Germanic theology and myth for this period — we do not have it; the vivid systems often presented as "Celtic mythology" or "Germanic mythology" draw heavily on much later Irish, Welsh, and Norse texts. - Reading the medieval Icelandic Eddas and Norse cosmology back onto the Axial-Age Germani (that material belongs to the later Norse chapter). - The classical claims of routine, mass human sacrifice (e.g. Caesar's wicker man) as reliable fact rather than possibly propagandistic hostile report. - The popular image of the druids as builders of Stonehenge (millennia older than the Celts) or as keepers of a unified pan-Celtic "faith."

What's genuinely open

- How much of the later Irish and Welsh literature preserves authentic pre-Christian religion, and how much is medieval Christian reshaping — a live and contested question. - The frequency, meaning, and context of human sacrifice and the bog bodies. - Whether there was any such thing as a single "Celtic religion" or "Germanic religion" at all, versus a patchwork of local cults loosely related — and indeed how meaningful the ethnic label "Celtic" itself is, now much debated among archaeologists. - The true antiquity of the four Celtic festivals and much of the ritual calendar known only from late sources.
