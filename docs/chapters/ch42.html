Prehistory · The Neolithic

The Neolithic

In the archive

The age when humans settled, farmed, and raised the first monuments to the sacred — a hilltop sanctuary older than agriculture, towns that buried their dead beneath the floor and kept their plastered skulls, and the great stones aligned to the turning sun — and the honest reckoning with a religion we can read only in architecture, never in words.

The temple that came first

On a barren hill in southeastern Turkey called Göbekli Tepe, archaeologists uncovered something that rewrote the story of religion. Rings of massive T-shaped limestone pillars, some sixteen feet tall and weighing tons, carved with foxes, snakes, boars, scorpions, and vultures, raised in great enclosures — and dated to around 9600 BCE, more than six thousand years before Stonehenge, before pottery, before writing, and, most astonishingly, at the very dawn of farming. Göbekli Tepe was built by people still largely living as hunter-gatherers. Its excavator, Klaus Schmidt, drew a revolutionary conclusion: that the old assumption — first agriculture, then surplus, then temples — might be exactly backward. Perhaps the need to gather in great numbers for ritual was itself one of the forces that drove people to settle and farm. First the temple, then the city.

We now know Göbekli Tepe was not alone. Across the same region — the "Taş Tepeler," the Stone Hills — sister sites are emerging, above all Karahan Tepe, with its own T-pillars and a startling chamber of carved phalluses and a watching human head. And the picture has grown more complex: the archaeologist E. B. Banning has argued that people may have lived at Göbekli Tepe after all, blurring the neat line between "temple" and "settlement," and the enclosures were deliberately backfilled and rebuilt over centuries. The "temple before the city" remains a powerful, genuinely revolutionary idea — and a debated one.

The Neolithic revolution

The Neolithic, the New Stone Age (roughly 10,000 to 3500 BCE in the Near East, later elsewhere), was the deepest transformation in human history before the modern age: the shift from wandering and hunting to farming, herding, and settled village life. It remade everything — diet, property, population, the shape of society — and it remade religion too, giving it, for the first time, permanent architecture: shrines, tombs, and monuments that stood in one place and gathered a community around the dead and the divine. We still cannot read its beliefs, for it left no writing. But for the first time, we can walk through its sacred spaces.

The cult of the skull

One practice binds the early Neolithic Near East together and speaks, more clearly than almost anything else, of an ancestor cult. At Jericho — one of the world's oldest towns — and across the Levant at sites like 'Ain Ghazal and Tell Ramad, people of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic (c. 9000–7000 BCE) practiced a startling rite: they buried their dead, then later dug up the skulls, modeled the faces back onto them in plaster, set shells or paint in the eye-sockets, and kept these plastered skulls among the living. Some sixty survive. At 'Ain Ghazal the same people made haunting plaster statues with staring eyes — among the oldest large-scale human images ever found. Whether the skulls honored beloved ancestors, venerated powerful lineages, or served some purpose we cannot name, they show a religion centered on the continuing presence of the dead.

Çatalhöyük: the sacred in the home

If Göbekli Tepe shows the monumental, Çatalhöyük in Anatolia (c. 7400–6000 BCE) shows the domestic. One of the world's first true towns, it was a dense honeycomb of mud-brick houses packed wall to wall, entered through holes in the roof. Its religion was woven into the home rather than housed in any public temple: households installed the horns of wild bulls (bucrania) into their walls and benches, painted their rooms with vultures pecking at headless human bodies (an image, perhaps, of excarnation — sky-burial), and — most strikingly — buried their dead beneath the floors of their own houses, keeping the ancestors literally underfoot. The archaeologist Ian Hodder, who excavated the site for decades, found no evidence of a ruling class or a priesthood, but rather a network of ritually charged "history houses" that accumulated burials and symbols over generations. Here the sacred was not a separate temple but the house itself.

The megaliths

Across Neolithic Europe and beyond rose the megaliths — the "great stones." At Stonehenge (built in stages c. 3000–2000 BCE) the sarsens were aligned to the solstices, so that the sun rose and set along the monument's axis; nearby Durrington Walls hosted the great midwinter feasts of the people who built it. At Newgrange in Ireland (c. 3200 BCE), a passage tomb was built so precisely that at winter-solstice sunrise a beam of light creeps down the passage to flood the burial chamber. At Carnac thousands of standing stones march across the Breton landscape; on Malta, the temples of Ġgantija (c. 3600 BCE) are among the oldest free-standing buildings on earth, and the underground Hypogeum of Ħal Saflieni held the bones of thousands with the reclining "Sleeping Lady." In the far north, the Heart of Neolithic Orkney — Skara Brae, the Ring of Brodgar, the solstice-aligned tomb of Maeshowe, and the vast temple-complex of the Ness of Brodgar — reveals a monumental sacred landscape at the edge of the world. Again and again the pattern repeats: monuments for the dead, aligned to the sky — an ancestor-cult married to the careful watching of the sun, the first stone calendars.

The Mother Goddess debate

No question about Neolithic religion is more famous, or more contested, than the Mother Goddess. The excavator of Çatalhöyük in the 1960s, James Mellaart, interpreted its many female figurines — above all the seated woman flanked by leopards — as a Great Goddess of fertility; the archaeologist Marija Gimbutas built this into a sweeping vision of a peaceful, matriarchal "Old Europe" worshipping a single Goddess, later overthrown by patriarchal, sky-god-worshipping Kurgan invaders from the steppe. It is a powerful and influential vision, and it must be reported honestly: mainstream archaeology largely rejects it. Hodder's long re-excavation of Çatalhöyük, and the systematic study of its figurines by Lynn Meskell and Carolyn Nakamura, found the figures to be predominantly not female at all, found no evidence of a female-dominated or goddess-centered society, and suggested an essentially egalitarian community. The single universal Goddess is now generally seen as a modern reconstruction projected onto a far more varied and unknowable past. The debate matters here precisely because the Goddess hypothesis went on to shape the modern Pagan and Goddess movements of the archive's final era (Chapter 38).

The believer's lens

No Neolithic voice survives, so this lens too is a careful reconstruction from architecture and analogy — what the sacred may have felt like from inside. The recurring evidence points toward a world organized around two poles: the ancestors and the sky-marked year. The plastered skulls of Jericho, the under-floor burials of Çatalhöyük, and the great passage-tombs all suggest a religion in which the dead did not depart but remained present, powerful, and tended — kin who had to be housed, fed, and consulted. The solstice alignments of Newgrange, Maeshowe, and Stonehenge suggest a sacred calendar in which the turning of the sun, the death and rebirth of the year, was watched and ritually met — fittingly, in an age newly dependent on the seasons for the harvest. And the wild bull's horns built into the walls of Çatalhöyük hint at a potent, dangerous natural world brought deliberately into the home. Read this way, the Neolithic sacred is the ancestor-and-season religion of settled farmers: the dead beneath the floor, the sun in the doorway, the bull on the wall.

The skeptical lens

The disciplining truth is that we possess the architecture of Neolithic religion in abundance and its theology not at all. Every reading above is inference, and the field's confident interpretations have repeatedly collapsed: Mellaart's and Gimbutas's Mother Goddess is the cautionary tale, a vivid, widely believed reconstruction that the evidence does not support. Schmidt's "temple before the city" is a hypothesis under active challenge (Banning). We do not know what the T-pillars represented, what the figurines were for, what the plastered skulls meant to those who kept them, or what was believed at the monuments whose alignments we can measure precisely. The honest skeptic affirms only what the stones compel — that these people built enduring sacred spaces, tended their dead with elaborate care, and oriented great monuments to the sun — and marks everything past that, however plausible, with a question mark. In prehistory more than anywhere, the archive can show what people did, and must confess how little it can prove of what they believed.

Symbology and sacred encoding

The Neolithic encoded the sacred in stone, bone, and orientation.

  • The T-pillars of Göbekli Tepe and Karahan Tepe are not blank slabs: several bear carved arms, hands, and belts, so that the pillar is a stylized, faceless being — perhaps an ancestor, perhaps a god — the first monumental image of the more-than-human, standing twelve feet tall in the round.
  • The plastered skull and the installed bull's horns encode the presence of the ancestors and the potency of the wild in the very fabric of the house — the human face rebuilt in plaster so the dead could keep their place among the living.
  • The astronomical alignments of Stonehenge, Newgrange, and Maeshowe are encoded time: the solstice sunrise built into the architecture, so the monument itself is a machine for marking the sacred turning of the year — the same impulse that would raise the sky-aligned ziggurats and pyramids of the coming age.
  • And the megalithic tomb — the dark stone passage opening to a chamber, entered by the dead and by the solstice light alike — encodes the oldest of religious ideas: that death is a passage, and the stone can be a womb as well as a grave. Its walls, at Newgrange and in Orkney, are carved with spirals and lozenges whose meaning is lost but whose intention is unmistakable.

Connections

The Neolithic grows directly out of the Paleolithic of the previous chapter — the same ancestor-honor and provisioned dead, now housed in permanent stone — and it leads directly into the Bronze Age that opens the archive's second era, when writing at last let religion speak for itself and the temple-mounds of Göbekli Tepe found their heirs in the ziggurats of Mesopotamia and the pyramids of Egypt (Chapters 2–3). Its sky-aligned monuments prefigure the temple-astronomy of the Aztec, Maya, and Inca (Chapter 29 and its companions); its contested Goddess became a charter for the modern Goddess movement and Wicca (Chapter 38). Chronologically first, written last, these two prehistoric chapters close the circle of the Nine Ages: everything in this book grows from the painted cave and the standing stone.

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The sites and their dates — Göbekli Tepe (c. 9600 BCE) and the wider Taş Tepeler (Karahan Tepe) as monumental early-Neolithic ritual architecture; the Levantine plastered-skull cult (Jericho, 'Ain Ghazal); Çatalhöyük with its under-floor burials, bucrania, and figurines; and the great megaliths (Stonehenge, Newgrange, Carnac, Ġgantija, the Ness of Brodgar) with their genuine, measurable solstice alignments. The architecture is real, dated, and unambiguous.

Not supported (as established fact)

The universal Neolithic "Mother Goddess" and peaceful matriarchy of Mellaart and Gimbutas — a contested hypothesis largely rejected by mainstream archaeology (Hodder, Meskell). Schmidt's "temple before the city" is an influential but actively debated interpretation (Banning). And the specific meanings of the pillars, figurines, skulls, and spirals are inferred, not known.

Genuinely open

What Göbekli Tepe was actually for, and who its carved beings were. What the figurines and the plastered skulls meant to those who made them. The beliefs behind the aligned monuments — we possess the architecture of Neolithic religion in abundance, but its theology is a silence we fill, honestly, with question marks. In prehistory more than anywhere, the archive can show what people did, and must confess how little it can prove of what they believed.
