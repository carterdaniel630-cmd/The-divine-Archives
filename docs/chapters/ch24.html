Early Medieval · Tantra

Tantra

In the archive

The esoteric revolution that swept across early-medieval India and Buddhism alike — a ritual technology of mantra, diagram, and the subtle body that made the human being a map of the universe — and the careful work of separating what the tradition teaches, what history can date, and what a century of Western reinvention has laid on top of it.

The circle of the sixty-four

On a low rise at Khajuraho, in central India, stands a temple unlike the famous carved spires nearby: an open-air ring of stone shrines, roofless, arranged in a great rectangle open to the sky. It is the Chausath Yogini temple — “the sixty-four yoginis” — and it is the oldest surviving building at the Chandela capital, dated to about 885 CE. Once it held sixty-four cells for the images of the yoginīs, fierce and powerful goddesses attendant on the Great Goddess, with a sixty-fifth shrine for the deity herself. Thirty-five cells survive. Here, the tradition holds, rites were performed under the yoginis’ gaze that were never meant for the ordinary temple crowd.

The open circle of yoginis is one of the few things Tantra left standing in plain sight, and it is a fitting emblem — because Tantra is a religion of the hidden. Its central texts are written in code, its practices are transmitted only from teacher to initiate, and much of what the modern world thinks it knows about it is a recent invention. This chapter is about a genuine and enormously influential current in early-medieval religion — and about the unusual care needed to describe it.

What “Tantra” is — and is not

“Tantra” (from a Sanskrit root meaning “to weave,” hence “loom,” “system,” “continuity”) names, most narrowly, a class of ritual scriptures. But “Tantrism” as a single -ism is a scholarly construct — a category assembled by Western Indologists in the nineteenth century to group diverse Hindu and Buddhist esoteric practices that their own practitioners never gathered under one name. That guards against the commonest error: treating “Tantra” as one doctrine rather than a family resemblance among many.

What those traditions share is an approach. Tantra arose in India no later than the fifth century CE, flowered from roughly 600 to 1100, and appears in both Hinduism and Buddhism. It seeks not only liberation (mokṣa or nirvāṇa) but also power (siddhi), through the ritual channeling of divine energy — śakti — and it works by aligning the human microcosm with the cosmic whole through mantras (sacred sound), yantras and mandalas (sacred diagrams), visualization, and above all initiation (dīkṣā) under a guru. Its scriptures are written in a veiled “twilight language” (sandhābhāṣā) that, the tradition insists, cannot be read without a teacher.

The textual record

The Hindu tantric scriptures are the Āgamas (where Śiva instructs the Goddess) or Nigamas, treating four domains — metaphysical knowledge (jñāna), yoga, ritual (kriyā), and conduct (caryā). The Shaiva Agamas, the Shakta Tantras of the Goddess, and the Vaishnava Pancharatra Samhitas each form vast bodies of text, most surviving in manuscripts of the seventh to tenth centuries.

The Buddhist tantras were later sorted into a rising series — Kriyā, Caryā, Yoga, and Anuttarayoga (highest) tantras. The most advanced, the Mahāyoga and Yoginī tantras, emerged between the eighth and tenth centuries: the Guhyasamāja, Cakrasaṃvara, and Hevajra tantras, the last composed in eastern India in the late eighth to tenth century. They introduced the coded language of advanced practice — deity visualization, charnel-ground meditation, wrathful deities, and the ritual use of substances and sexual yoga — hedged in secrecy within. Dating any of this precisely is hard, and honestly so: the texts are esoteric, often undated, orally transmitted, and deliberately obscure.

The body as a map of the cosmos

Tantra’s most influential idea is the subtle body. Beneath the gross physical body lies an energetic one threaded by channels (nāḍī) — said to number 72,000, with three chief ones: the central suṣumnā up the spine and the iḍā and piṅgalā spiralling around it. Where they meet sit the chakras, “wheels,” each imagined as a lotus with a seed-syllable (bīja mantra) at its heart. Coiled at the base sleeps kuṇḍalinī, the serpent-power, a form of Śakti; the goal of much tantric yoga is to wake her and draw her up the central channel to union with Śiva at the crown — the microcosmic image of the reuniting of God and Goddess, consciousness and energy.

It must be said plainly: the subtle body is a contemplative and experiential map, not a claim about anatomy. No dissection finds a chakra or a nāḍī, and the tradition, at its most careful, does not say one would. It is a religious model of inner experience — powerful, coherent, hugely influential — and it is a category error, common in modern popular writing, to treat it as physiology.

The deities and the schools

Tantric Hinduism is above all a religion of Śiva and the Goddess — Śiva in his fierce form as Bhairava; the Goddess as dark Kālī, as the serene Tripurasundarī of the Śrī Vidyā tradition, or as the ring of yoginīs. In nondual Shaiva theology Śiva is pure consciousness and Śakti his inseparable power, and the aim is to realize one’s identity with that divine ground. In tantric Buddhism, the practitioner visualizes and identifies with a chosen deity (yidam) — often a wrathful cremation-ground figure like Hevajra or Cakrasaṃvara — as a technique for realizing emptiness.

  • Kashmir Shaivism (Trika), arising after c. 850 CE, found its great synthesizer in Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1020), whose encyclopedic Tantrāloka (some 5,800 verses in 37 chapters) wove every available Shaiva stream into a single nondual philosophy of consciousness — one of the towering intellects of Indian thought, squarely of this era.
  • The Kāpālikas (“skull-bearers”) and Kaulas, radical ascetics who wrote the Bhairava Tantras, imitated their ferocious god: smeared with cremation ash, carrying a skull-bowl, propitiating their deities with the impure and deliberately breaking caste and purity taboos. Their descendants include the Aghoris.
  • The Śākta traditions of the Goddess, especially Śrī Vidyā around the Śrī Yantra, carried tantric worship into more “right-handed” forms; the Nāth yogis systematized the subtle-body yoga that later fed haṭha yoga.
  • In Buddhism, the Vajrayāna (“thunderbolt vehicle”) descended from the wandering mahāsiddhas, the “great adepts.”

Ritual and the two hands

After dīkṣā from a guru, the initiate works with mantra, yantra, nyāsa (ritually installing mantras and deities onto the body by touch), mudrā (gesture), and elaborate visualization. Tradition drew a famous distinction between the “right-hand” path (dakṣiṇācāra), which kept the transgressive elements symbolic, and the “left-hand” path (vāmācāra), which enacted them.

The left-hand rites center on the pañcamakāra, the “five Ms”: madya (wine), māṃsa (meat), matsya (fish), mudrā (parched grain), and maithuna (ritual sexual union) — the five things orthodox purity most forbade, consumed in a tightly bounded ritual frame to dissolve the ego’s divisions of pure and impure. Whether particular lineages performed the fifth M literally or read the whole set symbolically is a genuine and unresolved scholarly question; the sources range from the plainly literal to the frankly allegorical, and the secrecy around the rite means the reality is often not recoverable. What must be resisted is both the old outsider’s caricature of orgy and the modern sanitizing that pretends the literal rites never existed.

Buddhist Vajrayāna and the road to Tibet

Tantric Buddhism grew from the mahāsiddhas of eastern and northwestern India; its texts appeared from perhaps the sixth or seventh century, with the term Vajrayāna first attested in the eighth. Its method is deity yoga: a vivid identification with an enlightened being to actualize buddhahood swiftly. In 747, tradition holds, the master Padmasambhava carried these teachings into Tibet, anchoring the Nyingma lineage; after a persecution, a second diffusion in the late tenth and eleventh centuries reintroduced the later cycles — the Hevajra and Cakrasaṃvara tantras among them — and set Tibetan Buddhism on the tantric path it has followed since. These centuries are precisely the age of that transmission.

Material record: temples of the yoginis

Tantra’s clearest physical trace is the yoginī temple — around thirteen survive across India, eight in Madhya Pradesh, most built between the ninth and twelfth centuries. Their open, circular, roofless plan, unique in Indian temple architecture, set the sky itself as the ceiling over the ring of goddesses, marking them as spaces for rites outside the ordinary temple order; they are associated with the Kāpālika and Kaula streams. At Khajuraho more broadly, the Chandela temples (c. 950–1050) carry the famous erotic sculpture — a fraction of the total carving, and one whose exact relation to tantric ritual is debated, but which belongs to the same world in which the sacred and the sexual were not held apart.

Symbology and sacred encoding

Tantra is, more than almost any tradition in this book, a religion of encoded form — of sound and diagram treated as the very body of the divine.

  • Mantra: the tantric mantra is not merely a prayer but a sonic form of the deity. The bīja or “seed” syllables — oṃ, hrīṃ, śrīṃ, aiṃ — carry no dictionary meaning; they are held to be the deity in the form of sound, one to each chakra. To speak the seed is to invoke the power.
  • Yantra: a sacred diagram, the deity in geometric form. The supreme example is the Śrī Yantra — nine interlocking triangles (four “male,” pointing up, and five “female,” pointing down) radiating from a central point, the bindu, enclosed in lotus petals and a square frame, the bhūpura, with four gates. The practitioner meditates inward from the outer frame (earth and body) through the petals (the senses) and the triangles (the play of Śiva and Śakti) to the bindu, the dimensionless point of pure consciousness — a map of the cosmos and of the path drawn as one figure.
  • Mandala: the Buddhist counterpart, a circular palace of the deity into which the initiate is symbolically led.
  • Nyāsa and mudrā: the ritual writing of mantras onto the body by touch, and the coded language of hand-gestures, encode the divine directly into flesh and posture.
  • Twilight language (sandhābhāṣā): the double-coding of the tantras themselves, in which a word means one thing to the uninitiated and another to the initiate — a lock built into scripture against the unprepared.

A firm line must be drawn here, as the tradition’s own honesty demands. The classical system above is well attested in medieval texts. But the familiar modern seven-chakra rainbow, with its fixed colors and one-to-one map to glands and psychology, is substantially a twentieth-century synthesis — shaped by early translators and Western esoteric and New Age reworking — not a straight transcription of the medieval sources, which vary in the number and character of the centers. And the popular Western equation of “Tantra” with sacred sex is a modern distortion: classical Tantra is overwhelmingly mantra, visualization, and ritual, in which sexual yoga is one bounded, secret, contested element of some left-hand lineages, not the heart of the thing.

Connections

Tantra grew out of the Vedic and Puranic Hinduism and the Mahayana Buddhism of earlier eras and reshaped both from within. It fed directly into Tibetan Buddhism (the neighboring transmissions of this very period), into the Nāth yogis and thence haṭha yoga, into the devotional Bhakti movements of the next era, and into the Śrī Vidyā worship still living today. Its coded diagrams rhyme with the sacred geometry of Islam and the Kabbalah of the same centuries. And in the twentieth century its vocabulary — chakras, kundalini, mantra, mandala — became, in reworked form, part of the global language of yoga and the New Age, which is exactly why separating the medieval reality from the modern overlay matters so much here.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • A vast tantric scriptural corpus in both Hinduism and Buddhism from roughly the fifth century on; the historical reality of Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1020) and the Tantrāloka.
  • The Kāpālika/Kaula ascetic traditions and their transgressive reputation; the yoginī temples and their distinctive open plan (Khajuraho c. 885).
  • The rise of Vajrayāna and its transmission to Tibet across these centuries; and the core technology of mantra, yantra, mandala, initiation, and the subtle-body model.

What’s not supported (as history or science)

  • That the chakras and nāḍīs are anatomical structures — they are a contemplative model of inner experience, not physiology.
  • That the popular seven-chakra color system is the unaltered medieval teaching — it is largely a modern synthesis.
  • That “Tantra” means sacred sex — a modern Western distortion of a mostly ritual and meditative tradition.

What’s genuinely open

  • The precise dating and authorship of most tantric texts, given their esoteric, oral, deliberately obscure transmission.
  • How literally particular lineages performed the pañcamakāra, especially the fifth M — the sources genuinely differ and the secrecy is real.
  • The exact relationship between Khajuraho’s erotic sculpture and tantric ritual, and how much inner practice was ever written down at all.

Because Tantra is unusually overlaid by modern reinvention, the chapter separates what the medieval tradition teaches, what history can date, and what later Western reworking added: the classical mantra/yantra/subtle-body system is presented as attested medieval teaching, while the seven-chakra color scheme and the popular “Tantra = sacred sex” image are flagged as largely 20th-century constructions, and the subtle body is treated as a contemplative model, never as anatomy. Transgressive left-hand practice is described with the same descriptive rigor as any other tradition — neither sensationalized nor sanitized — and live debates (text dating, how literal the five Ms were, the meaning of Khajuraho’s sculpture) are left open. Tibetan Buddhism, Nāth/haṭha yoga, Bhakti, and modern global yoga are reserved for their own chapters.
