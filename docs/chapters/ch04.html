Bronze Age · Indus Valley

Indus Valley

In the archive

A one-inch seal shows a horned figure seated among wild animals. A great archaeologist called it a proto-Shiva. A century later we still cannot say what it is — because the people who made it left writing we have never been able to read.

A god that may not be a god

Sometime around 1928, in the ruins of Mohenjo-daro on the plain of the Indus, excavators turned up a small steatite seal, barely an inch and a half square. It shows a figure seated with legs folded beneath it, in what looks unmistakably like a yogic posture, wearing a tall horned headdress and surrounded by animals — a tiger, an elephant, a rhinoceros, a water buffalo, and two deer. Sir John Marshall, directing the excavation of the newly discovered Indus civilization, looked at this seal and saw something breathtaking: a proto-Shiva. Here, he argued, three thousand years early, was the Hindu god in embryo — Pashupati, “Lord of Animals,” the horned yogi, master of the beasts.

It is one of the most famous objects in the archaeology of religion, and nearly a century later scholars still argue about almost every word of that description. Is the figure male? A god at all, or a chieftain or shaman? Are those three faces, or a wide horned mask? Is it Shiva’s ancestor, or a version of the “Master of Animals” motif found from Mesopotamia to the Aegean in the same era? We cannot settle it — and the reason is the single most important fact about Indus religion, the fact that governs this whole chapter: their writing has never been read.

This is a civilization we can see in extraordinary material detail and cannot hear in its own voice at all. What follows therefore carries more honest uncertainty than any chapter so far — and that is exactly as it should be.

The silent civilization

The scale is easy to underestimate. The Indus (or Harappan) civilization of its mature phase, roughly 2600 to 1900 BCE, was the largest of the Bronze Age — larger in area than Egypt and Mesopotamia combined, spread across more than a thousand sites. Its great cities — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, Lothal — were laid out on grids, built of standardized fired brick, and served by the most advanced water and drainage engineering anywhere in the ancient world. Its system of weights barely varied across the entire territory for centuries.

And then the strangeness. For a civilization this sophisticated, the Indus cities are eerily quiet about the things other Bronze Age powers shouted. There are no securely identified temples. No great palaces. No royal tombs stuffed with gold like Ur’s, no pyramids, no monumental inscriptions boasting of kings, no reliefs of rulers smiting enemies. Where Egypt and Mesopotamia broadcast a hierarchy of god-kings in stone, the Indus is, by comparison, faceless — and that absence is itself one of the most intriguing pieces of evidence we have.

The wall we cannot read

Everything would be different if we could read the Indus script. We have it in abundance — some four thousand inscribed objects, mostly the little seals — carrying about four hundred distinct signs. And it is, after a century of effort and dozens of confident “decipherments,” still completely undeciphered. No bilingual key like the Rosetta Stone has ever been found.

There is not even agreement that the signs encode language at all. The dominant view, argued by Asko Parpola and Iravatham Mahadevan, holds that the script is true writing, most likely an early Dravidian language. But in a provocative 2004 paper, Farmer, Sproat, and Witzel argued the opposite: that the inscriptions are too short (averaging about five signs), contain too many once-used signs, and lack the repetition real languages produce — and so may be non-linguistic emblems rather than writing. The debate is unresolved. Until it is, the Indus people keep their theology to themselves, and every claim about their gods is an inference from things, not texts.

What the objects suggest — held loosely

With that warning fixed in place, here is what the remains have been taken to suggest. Every item is an interpretation, and the honest ones are offered as such.

The seated horned figure. Marshall’s “proto-Shiva” (seal M-420) rests on later features read backward: Shiva as lord of animals, as archetypal yogi, as bull-associated. Critics such as Doris Srinivasan have argued the figure may be a bull or buffalo deity and that the “three faces” are questionable; Shubhangana Atre has proposed the central Indus deity was in fact a goddess, a “Lady of Beasts.” No reading commands consensus.

A goddess, or goddesses. Excavators recovered many terracotta female figurines, some with soot marks suggesting offerings burned before them — widely read as a fertility or mother-goddess cult. But without texts we cannot be sure which were cult objects, which votive, and which toys.

Water and purity. The most striking structure is the Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro, a watertight sunken tank of fitted brick sealed with bitumen. It looks built for communal immersion and is often read as ritual bathing — a possible ancestor of later Indian purity concerns — but the reading is attractive and unprovable.

Trees, animals, and stones. Seals show a figure standing in a pipal (sacred fig) tree, still sacred in later India; cattle imagery is everywhere, above all the enigmatic single-horned “unicorn” bull. Cones and ring-stones have been read (first by Marshall) as lingam and yoni prefiguring Shaiva worship, though many archaeologists see ordinary objects. At Kalibangan, pits with ash have been claimed as fire altars, but whether ritual or hearth is disputed.

The quiet dead. Harappan burials are generally modest — extended inhumations with some pottery and ornaments, and no lavish royal tombs. Compared with the death-pits of Ur or the tombs of Egypt, the Indus dead were sent off with striking restraint.

Symbology and sacred encoding

For a civilization defined by an unread script, sacred encoding is not a side-topic — it is the whole predicament in miniature.

The great cipher. The Indus script is the largest undeciphered corpus of the ancient world, and it sits mostly on seals whose practical job was almost certainly administrative — yet the same seals carry the richest religious imagery: the horned figure, the tree spirit, composite animals. The encoded and the sacred are literally combined on one object, and both halves resist us. It is the purest case in this archive of a symbol system that is demonstrably there, plainly systematic, and still locked.

The unicorn and the altar-object. The most common seal image is the “unicorn” — a bull-like animal shown with a single horn — standing before a mysterious object usually called the “standard” or “manger,” possibly a ritual filter or burner. What it meant is unknown; that it meant something, repeated on thousands of seals, is hard to doubt.

The swastika. Among Indus motifs is the swastika, a hooked cross that was for thousands of years across Eurasia a benign symbol of good fortune, and remained sacred in Hindu, Jain, and Buddhist use long before its twentieth-century appropriation by Nazism inverted its associations in the West.

The order in the weights. There is one Indus “code” we can read, and it is not religious: the standardized cubical stone weights, uniform across the whole territory. It is a reminder that not every encoded system is sacred — and a caution that, precisely because we cannot read the script, the temptation to pour mystical meaning into Indus symbols must be resisted.

Connections

The Indus was not isolated. Sumerian texts speak of trade with a distant land called Meluhha, widely identified with the Indus, and the archaeology confirms it: Indus-style seals and etched carnelian beads have been found at Ur. The Indus and the Mesopotamia of the previous chapter were contemporaries and trading partners — two utterly different religious worlds, one loud with gods and kings, the other silent.

The forward connection is more fraught. Around 1900 BCE the Indus cities declined — through a drawn-out combination of climate change, shifting rivers, and the fading of their trade and urban systems. In the centuries after, the record of northwest India is increasingly that of the Vedic culture and its Indo-Aryan language. Recent ancient-DNA work, including a genome from a Harappan individual at Rakhigarhi, indicates that steppe-pastoralist ancestry associated with Indo-European speakers was absent among the Harappans and arrived largely after the Indus decline (c. 2000–1500 BCE), as admixture layered onto the existing population, not a replacement. Whether, and how much, Indus religion survived that transition to shape later Hinduism is the great open question the next chapter, on the early Vedic world, must take up.

✦ The evidence, honestly

What’s well supported

  • The scale, urban planning, standardized weights, and water engineering of the mature Harappan civilization (c. 2600–1900 BCE).
  • The Indus script exists as a systematic sign-system and remains genuinely undeciphered; no proposed decipherment is accepted.
  • The material religious remains are real: seal M-420, the female figurines, the Great Bath, pipal-tree and bull/“unicorn” imagery, modest burials.
  • The near-total absence of identified temples, palaces, royal tombs, and ruler/war art, unlike Egypt and Mesopotamia.
  • Long-distance trade with Mesopotamia (“Meluhha”; Indus seals and beads at Ur).
  • Ancient DNA indicating steppe ancestry was absent among the Harappans and entered South Asia mainly after the decline, as admixture not replacement.

What’s not supported

  • The confident identification of seal M-420 as Shiva; “proto-Shiva” is one reading among several, none proven.
  • Any claim that the Indus script has been read, or a specific myth recovered from it.
  • The assertion that Harappan religion simply is early Hinduism; the parallels are suggestive, not established.
  • Certainty about the figurines or the Great Bath as specifically cultic.

What’s genuinely open

  • Essentially the entire religious content: deities, myths, priesthood (if any), and cosmology.
  • The identity and sex of the horned figure, and whether the central deity was god, goddess, both, or neither.
  • Whether the script encodes a language at all, and if so which (Dravidian hypothesis vs. non-linguistic thesis).
  • The function of the Great Bath and the meaning of the “unicorn” and its altar-object.
  • The degree of continuity with later Hinduism, and the Harappan relationship to the incoming Indo-Aryan/Vedic culture — a debate entangled with modern politics, to be reported, not adjudicated.
