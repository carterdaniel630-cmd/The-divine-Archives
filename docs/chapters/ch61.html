Prehistory · Aboriginal Australian Dreaming

Aboriginal Australian Dreaming

In the archive

An elder sings a sequence of verses, and the song is also a map. Sung in the right order, it names every waterhole, ridge, and rock across hundreds of miles of desert, so that a person who knows the song can cross a country they have never seen — following the exact path an Ancestral Being walked at the beginning, when the world was sung into shape. This is a songline, and it belongs to what may be the oldest continuous religious tradition on earth.

The oldest living tradition

Aboriginal Australians have maintained their spiritual traditions for more than sixty-five thousand years — a continuity beside which every other religion in this archive is young. But it is a mistake, and one this chapter must flag at the outset, to speak of "the" Aboriginal religion, for there was never one: the continent held hundreds of distinct peoples, languages, and traditions, each with its own Ancestral Beings and its own Dreaming. What they share is not a single creed but a common grammar — the world shaped by ancestral travellers, those journeys written into the land, and every person bound by totem to a particular place and living thing. And crucially, this is not a religion of the dead past: it is alive today, practiced by living communities, even as it reaches back into deep prehistory. The archive places it here, at the book's chronological root, precisely because it is the closest thing we have to a living window onto the religious mind of the deep human past — while insisting it is a present, not a past, faith.

The Dreaming

At the center of Aboriginal belief is what English calls the Dreaming or Dreamtime — an inadequate translation of words like the Western Desert Tjukurpa and the Arrernte Altjira. It is not a "time" in the ordinary sense, and not a dream. It is better rendered as the "everywhen": an eternal, ongoing creative reality that is past, present, and future at once. In the Dreaming the great Ancestral Beings — part human, part animal, part landform — emerged into a featureless world and travelled across it, and in their journeys and deeds they shaped the land: this ridge is where an ancestor lay down, that waterhole is where one dug, that rock is an ancestor's body turned to stone. They did not then depart; they remain present in the country they made, in its features and its species. And they laid down the Law (also called Tjukurpa) — the pattern of kinship, ceremony, land-custody, and right living that people are bound to keep. To be Aboriginal in the traditional sense is to live inside a landscape that is at once map, scripture, ancestor, and law.

Songlines and Country

The travels of the Ancestral Beings are inscribed in the land as songlines (dreaming tracks) — routes across the country, each carried in a song-cycle that names its features in sequence. The songline is simultaneously a myth, a navigational map, a property register, and a liturgy; songlines cross the boundaries of many languages, each people singing its own stretch, so that a single track may run for a thousand miles through a dozen tongues. Bound up with this is the concept of Country — not "land" as property but a living, conscious, sacred web of which people are part. In the Aboriginal understanding a person does not own Country; a person belongs to it, is kin to it, and carries a sacred duty to "care for Country." Every individual is born with a totem — an ancestral being and an associated species and place — that fixes their identity, their kin obligations, and their responsibilities to a particular tract of the sacred land.

The Ancestral Beings

Among the countless Ancestral Beings, some are widespread. The most famous is the Rainbow Serpent, a great creator-being associated with water, rain, and fertility, known across much of the continent under many names, who carved rivers and gorges and remains in deep waterholes. In the Kimberley, the Wandjina are the supreme creator and rain beings of the Worrora, Ngarinyin, and Wunambal peoples — depicted with great dark eyes "like the eye of a storm" and, strikingly, no mouth, for a mouth would make them too powerful; they control the seasons and the rains. Across Arnhem Land, the desert, and every region, distinct beings shaped distinct countries. There is generally no single supreme "God" of the Western kind; there is the Dreaming, and the Ancestral Beings who fill and sustain it.

Ceremony and the sacred object

Aboriginal religion is transmitted entirely orally and through performance — there is no scripture. Its life is ceremony: the gatherings often called corroboree, the great initiation rites that make boys and girls into adults and open the sacred knowledge to them stage by stage, and the mortuary and increase rites that keep the land and its species fertile. Much of this knowledge is restricted — divided into "men's business" and "women's business," and graded by initiation, so that the deepest truths are held only by the fully initiated and are not to be shared with outsiders or the uninitiated. Sacred objects carry ancestral power, above all the incised stone or wooden tjurunga (churinga), regarded as holding the very essence of an ancestor and kept hidden from ordinary sight. Body painting, the bullroarer, the didgeridoo, and elaborate designs all serve the ceremonial re-enactment of the Dreaming — for in ceremony, the everywhen becomes present again.

The record in stone

Aboriginal Australia holds the oldest continuous painting tradition on earth. Its rock art spans tens of thousands of years: the enigmatic, elegant Gwion Gwion figures (the "Bradshaw" art) of the Kimberley, estimated at over twenty thousand years old; the great Wandjina images, repainted across generations; and the "X-ray" art of Arnhem Land, which shows animals and beings with their inner organs and bones — the life-essence made visible. Crucially, much of this art is not a relic but a living practice: certain images are ritually repainted, their power renewed by the act, so that a Wandjina figure on a rock wall may be at once ancient and freshly made — a tradition of continuous renewal rather than preservation.

Dispossession and survival

Colonization from 1788 brought catastrophe — dispossession, massacre, disease, the forced removal of children, and the deliberate destruction of language and ceremony. Sacred sites have been lost even in living memory: in 2020 the 46,000-year-old Juukan Gorge rock shelters were destroyed by mining, an act that caused national outrage. Yet the tradition endures. The struggle for land rights, for the protection of sacred sites, and for the repatriation of sacred objects and ancestral remains is itself now part of the living religious life of Aboriginal Australia — the defense of Country against those who would treat it as mere ground.

Symbology and sacred encoding

  • The songline. The single most remarkable encoding in this archive: a song that is simultaneously a map, a law, and a title-deed, so that the land itself is a text sung into legibility. Knowledge, navigation, and religion are one thing.
  • The concentric circle. The iconography of Central and Western Desert painting reads the land from above: concentric circles for waterholes and sacred sites, connecting lines for the ancestors' paths, U-shapes for people seated — an aerial map of Country and Dreaming at once. And the famous dot-painting style both reveals a design's surface and conceals the restricted sacred knowledge beneath it, showing outsiders the pattern but not its inner meaning.
  • The Rainbow Serpent and the Wandjina. The serpent encodes water, fertility, and the creative-destructive power that carved the land; the mouthless, storm-eyed Wandjina encodes a power so great it must be shown without a mouth.
  • X-ray art encodes the belief that the true reality of a being is its inner life-essence — the bones and organs — not merely its outward form.

Connections

Aboriginal religion opens the archive's deepest chronological horizon, standing beside the Paleolithic and Neolithic (ch41–ch42) as a living tradition that may illuminate the religious world of the deep human past — though the archive resists the temptation to treat a present people as a museum of prehistory. Its totemism became, through Émile Durkheim's Elementary Forms of the Religious Life, the foundation of the modern sociology of religion — the theory that the sacred is society worshipping itself. Its "everywhen" is a conception of sacred time unlike either the line or the wheel of ch50; its Rainbow Serpent belongs with the world's creation beings (ch46); its purely oral transmission parallels the Vedic (ch05) and the Baltic dainos (ch59); and its sacred bond to Country connects to the reverence for sacred land found throughout this book. It is joined in the next chapters by the other great living oral traditions of the Americas and Oceania (ch62, ch63).

✦ The evidence, honestly

Well-supported by evidence

The extraordinary antiquity and continuity of Aboriginal presence and culture in Australia (more than 60,000 years, archaeologically established), making it among the oldest continuous living traditions on earth; the reality of the core concepts — the Dreaming/Tjukurpa, songlines, totemism, connection to Country, the Ancestral Beings including the Rainbow Serpent and the Wandjina; the ceremonial and oral transmission and the restricted grading of sacred knowledge; and the rock-art record as the oldest continuous painting tradition, including living repainting. The colonial catastrophe and the destruction of sites such as Juukan Gorge (2020) are documented fact.

Not supported (as established fact) / to be rejected

The idea of a single Aboriginal religion is false — the traditions are many and diverse. "Dreaming/Dreamtime" is an imperfect colonial-era translation and should not be taken literally as "a dream" or "a past age." Sensational appropriations — such as the pseudo-archaeological claim that the Wandjina depict "ancient aliens," or New Age repackagings of Aboriginal spirituality — are distortions and are rejected here. The precise dating and authorship of the oldest rock art (e.g. the Gwion Gwion) are debated.

Genuinely open — and deliberately closed

Much of the deepest content of these traditions is restricted sacred knowledge, not to be published, and the archive reports only what is already public and respects that boundary rather than treating it as a gap to be filled. The exact meanings of the most ancient rock art, and the full pre-contact diversity of belief across the continent, are in many cases unrecoverable or rightly held only by their custodians. The archive documents what is openly known, refuses the appropriations, and honors what is not ours to tell.
