Not every flood is the same story

The Mesopotamian-to-Genesis line is a documented literary chain — Ziusudra becomes Atrahasis becomes Utnapishtim becomes Noah. Greek and Hindu deluges share the shape but are not obviously borrowed. And two famous “floods” differ in kind: China’s Yu the Great tames the waters by engineering, and Egypt’s Book of the Heavenly Cow floods the fields with beer to stop a slaughter.

✦ Where the pattern breaks

Two shapes, independently reached

Two motifs recur — order carved from a watery chaos, and a world built from a slain primordial body (Tiamat, Ymir, Purusha). These are real cross-cultural shapes, but the traditions are largely independent, and their theologies differ sharply. The Vedic Nasadiya Sukta is remarkable precisely for refusing certainty — “who really knows?” And the Norse cosmogony survives only in Christian-era Icelandic texts, so how much is genuinely pagan is debated.

✦ Where the pattern breaks

One grim shadow — and one exception

For most of the ancient world the dead shared a single, undifferentiated shadow, without reward or punishment — the Mesopotamian Kur, the Greek Hades, the Hebrew Sheol, the Norse Hel. Egypt is the striking exception, with a moral judgment and a blessed afterlife for the justified. Heaven, hell, judgment, and resurrection are largely later developments — in Second Temple Judaism and after — and how much they owe to Zoroastrian Persia is genuinely debated.

✦ Where the pattern breaks — read this one carefully

The category itself is contested

“The dying-and-rising god” is a disputed scholarly category. Since James Frazer’s Golden Bough grouped these figures together, many scholars — notably Jonathan Z. Smith — have judged the pattern loose and misleading: Osiris rules the dead rather than returning to life, several “resurrections” are seasonal or ambiguous rather than personal, and lumping them together flattens real differences. And the popular claim that Christianity simply copied a dying-and-rising pagan god is rejected by mainstream scholarship — some parallels post-date Christianity, some (a “resurrected” Mithras) are modern inventions, and the resemblances more likely reflect a shared religious atmosphere than borrowing. The Roman Mystery Cults chapter weighs this in full.
