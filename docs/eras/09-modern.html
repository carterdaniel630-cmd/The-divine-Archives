Era IX · c. 1800 – present

The Modern Age

Faith did not fade; it multiplied and remade itself. New religious movements, Spiritualism and the séance, the revival of Paganism and Wicca, Theosophy and the occult, and the living syntheses of the diaspora.

✦

Traditions of this age

New Religious MovementsRecently added · pending review

The explosion of brand-new religions in the last two centuries — from Joseph Smith's golden plates to the global movements of today. The neutral term that replaced 'cult'; the millenarian ferment of 19th-century America (the Latter-day Saints, Adventists, Jehovah's Witnesses, Christian Science); the global 20th century (Baha'i, Scientology, Falun Gong, Hare Krishna) and its tragedies (Jonestown); the 'cult' question judged by conduct not novelty; and a symbology of new scriptures and staged initiation — taking each movement seriously as religion while keeping a clear eye on harm.

 SpiritualismRecently added · pending review

The religion of the talking dead — born from two girls and a knocking sound in 1848, swept through a grieving Victorian world of darkened parlors and rapping tables. The Fox sisters and their 1888 confession; the séance and its wonders (rapping, table-tipping, automatic writing, spirit photography, the Ouija board); the appeal to a war-bereaved age and to women; the exposures and the Society for Psychical Research; and a symbology of the coded rap and the 'spiritual telegraph' — keeping the real consolation distinct from the never-verified communication.

 Theosophy & the Occult RevivalRecently added · pending review

The late-19th-century rebirth of Western esotericism — a claimed ancient wisdom behind all religions, hidden Masters, and a magical order that fused Kabbalah and tarot into a system of the soul. Blavatsky and the Theosophical Society, the Secret Doctrine and the root races, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Crowley's Thelema, and Steiner; a symbology section on the tarot mapped onto the Tree of Life and the Hermetic 'as above, so below' — reckoning honestly with the fraudulent Mahatma letters, the invented pedigrees, and the dark later misuse of the root-race doctrine.

 Wicca & Modern PaganismRecently added · pending review

The 20th-century rebirth of Pagan religion — a nature faith of Goddess and Horned God, coven and sabbat, magic and moon. Gerald Gardner and the (false) claim of an ancient surviving witch-cult; the honest origins in ceremonial magic, Romanticism, and folk custom; the Wheel of the Year and the Wiccan Rede; the wider revival (Druidry, Heathenry, the Goddess movement); and a symbology of the pentacle and the Book of Shadows — told as a real, living modern religion that need not be old to be genuine.

 SatanismRecently added · pending review

Two completely different things wearing the same frightening name: the imaginary Devil-cult that persecutors invent (the witch trials, the blood-libel, the 1980s Satanic Panic — investigated and found baseless) and the real, mostly atheistic philosophy people actually choose. LaVey's Church of Satan and the Satanic Bible (Satan as symbol, not deity), The Satanic Temple's non-theistic church-state activism, and the small theistic fringe; a symbology of the inverted pentagram, Lévi's Baphomet, and the largely-literary Black Mass — described with the same rigor as any tradition and none of the shock.

 African Diaspora ReligionsRecently added · pending review

The living Afro-Atlantic faiths that grew from the African religions carried into slavery — Haitian Vodou, Cuban Santería, Brazilian Candomblé, and the newer fire of Rastafari. From the oath at Bois Caïman and the Haitian Revolution, through the lwa and orichas doubled behind Catholic saints, possession and drumming, to Haile Selassie, Babylon and Zion; a symbology of the vèvè, the bead-codes, the calling drums, and concealment itself — seeing them as the rich, healing, community faiths they are, behind the racist 'voodoo' caricature. Completes the story begun in African Traditional Religion and closes the Modern age.

 Pentecostalism & Global ChristianityIn the archive

The fire and the southern shift — the fastest-growing movement in modern religion, and the transformation of Christianity into a Global-South faith. The Azusa Street Revival (1906) under William Seymour and the Holiness roots (Parham, 1901); the baptism in the Holy Spirit and the restored gifts — speaking in tongues, prophecy, and divine healing, modeled on Acts 2; the three waves (classical Pentecostal denominations, the 1960s Charismatic movement into the mainline and Catholic churches, and the neo-charismatic independents); the prosperity gospel, reported honestly and contested; and the demographic shift of the Christian majority to Africa, Latin America, and Asia. A symbology of the tongues of fire, the raised hands, and the laying on of hands — an aniconic Christianity of direct experience, with its theological claims marked as faith.

 Bahá'í & New FaithsIn the archive

One God, one religion, one humanity — the archive’s closing chapter, on the faith that tries to hold all the others in a single embrace. The Bahá'í Faith: the Báb (executed 1850) and Bahá'u’lláh (1817–1892), exiled to ʾAkká; the three onenesses and the doctrine of progressive revelation, which gathers Abraham, Krishna, Moses, Zoroaster, the Buddha, Jesus, Muhammad, the Báb, and Bahá'u’lláh into one unfolding succession of Manifestations; the strikingly modern social teachings (equality of the sexes, harmony of science and religion, world peace); a clergy-less order under the Universal House of Justice in Haifa; and the systematic persecution of Bahá’ís in Iran. Plus the wider modern ‘new world faiths’ — Caodaism, the Ahmadiyya, Tenrikyo. A symbology of the nine-pointed star, the Greatest Name, and the terraced mountain — with progressive revelation flagged as the faith’s own framework, not neutral history.

Comparative themes across this age

The FloodIn the archive

Why nearly every people who kept records remembers the world ending in water — the Mesopotamian lineage from Ziusudra to Utnapishtim, its relationship to Genesis, and what the archaeology will and won't support.

 Creation & the First OrderIn the archive

How the world began, told side by side: the watery chaos of Tiamat, Nun, and tehom; the combat myth (chaoskampf) in which Marduk, Baal, and Zeus win order by battle — and Genesis's pointed refusal of the fight; the world built from a slain primordial body (Ymir, Purusha) and the contested Indo-European 'cosmic giant'; creation by word from Ptah to 'let there be'; the later doctrine of creation from nothing and the Genesis 1:1 translation debate; and the astonishing Nasadiya Sukta, the ancient hymn that looks at the beginning and admits it cannot be sure. With a symbology of the cosmic egg, sacred number, the dividing line, and the creative word.

 Journeys to the UnderworldIn the archive

The descent to the land of the dead — one of the oldest and most widespread of all sacred stories — and what a civilization reveals when it weighs its dead. Inanna stripped at the seven gates; the Greek katabasis (Odysseus, Orpheus, Aeneas, Persephone); Egypt’s Duat and the Weighing of the Heart against the feather of Maat; the judged afterlives (Zoroastrian Chinvat bridge, the Tibetan bardo, Chinese Diyu) set against the un-judged pits of Sheol and Norse Hel; the god who goes down (Izanagi, Baldr, the Harrowing of Hell); and a symbology of the gate, the ferryman, the scale, and the guidebook written for the dead.

 The Great GoddessIn the archive

The divine feminine — and the modern myth built on top of it. The goddesses we can actually document (Inanna/Ishtar, Isis, Asherah and the ‘Yahweh and his Asherah’ inscriptions, Cybele, Demeter, the living Mahādevī of India, Guanyin, Amaterasu); the ancient temptation to unify them into one; and Marija Gimbutas’s Great Goddess of a peaceful Neolithic matriarchy, reported honestly as largely rejected by mainstream archaeology. The sublimation of the goddess into Wisdom/Sophia and the Virgin Mary; the 20th-century Goddess movement and thealogy; and a symbology of the figurine, the triple moon, and the Black Madonna — a study in keeping what is revered separate from what is proven.

 Sacred KingshipIn the archive

The ruler as a holy figure between heaven and earth — god, god’s son, god’s chosen, or god’s hostage. Frazer’s priest of Nemi and the dying-king thesis; the king as god (the Egyptian pharaoh, the Sapa Inca son of the Sun, the Japanese emperor, the deified Roman emperor); the king answerable to Heaven (the Chinese Mandate of Heaven, the prophet-checked king of Israel); the documented dying-king rites (the Shilluk reth, the Mesopotamian substitute king); and anointed kingship in Christendom (divine right, the royal touch, messiah/Christ). A symbology of regalia, anointing oil, and the king’s two bodies — with Frazer’s grand unifying thesis flagged honestly as rejected even where the specific cases hold.

 The End of DaysIn the archive

How the world ends — and the deep divide between the line and the wheel. The Qumran War Scroll and the apocalyptic mind; the Persian blueprint (Zoroastrian dualism, the Saoshyant, resurrection, and Frashokereti, the ‘making wonderful’); the apocalyptic turn in Second Temple Judaism (Daniel, Enoch) and the contested question of Zoroastrian influence; the Book of Revelation, the Antichrist, and the millennium; the Islamic Day of Resurrection, the Mahdi, and the Dajjal; and the cyclical counter-vision — Norse Ragnarök, the Hindu Kali Yuga and Kalki, Buddhist Maitreya, the Aztec Fifth Sun — where destruction is one turn of an eternal wheel. A symbology of 666, Armageddon, and the world made new.

 Sacrifice & the ScapegoatIn the archive

The gift of blood — one of the most universal and most troubling of religious acts. What sacrifice was (korban, ‘drawing near’; yajna into fire; thysia, ‘making smoke’; do ut des) and the gift/communion/feeding theories; blood on the altar; the substitution of animal for human (the Aqedah, the Passover lamb); human sacrifice reported honestly (Aztec, the contested Carthaginian tophet, Shang retainers); the scapegoat of Leviticus and the Greek pharmakos; Girard’s mimetic scapegoat mechanism and Burkert’s Homo Necans (flagged as contested); and the sacrifice that ends sacrifice — the Lamb of God and the Eucharist, the bloodless turns of Judaism, Hinduism, Jainism, and Buddhism. A symbology of altar, smoke, substitute, and shared meal.
