Angkor Wat

A Buddhist shrine for centuries; a pilgrimage shrine today

Angkor Wat, near Siem Reap in Cambodia, is the largest monument of Angkor, the region of the capitals of the Khmer empire from the 9th to the 15th century. King Suryavarman II, who reigned from 1113 until his death about 1149, built it in the first half of the 12th century as a temple of Vishnu. It became a Buddhist shrine, and for centuries Buddhist pagodas stood within it. Today it is, in Britannica’s words, ‘one of the most important pilgrimage shrines in Southeast Asia’.

This walk crosses the moat and the western gate, follows the raised way to the temple, walks the gallery of bas-reliefs, climbs through the cruciform cloister to the second level and ends on the platform of the five towers. The temple is shown as it stands, simplified; the reliefs are our own renderings, and the stone is drawn in code.

The Sacred Tooth Relic (for comparison)

Sri Dalada Maligawa, Kandy · In Sri Lanka since the 4th century CE (tradition)

Not from Angkor. The casket of the Buddha’s tooth in Kandy, Sri Lanka, shown as a rendition; the relic itself is shown only rarely and is not shown here. For a thousand years whoever held it held the right to rule. It is here for two comparisons: Angkor Wat, too, joined a king to the sacred, built by Suryavarman II for Vishnu and perhaps as his mausoleum; and, like the tooth’s temple, it became a place of Buddhist worship.

I · The western approach

Through the western gate and along the raised way, with the five towers ahead: the place where the spring equinox sun rises over the central tower.

A temple for Vishnu

Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate Shown as it stands today, simplified

Angkor Wat was built for Suryavarman II, in the first half of the 12th century, as a temple of Vishnu; after his death the king was known by the name Paramavishnuloka. The five towers ahead stand on a stepped pyramid of three levels. The central tower is 42 metres high and rises 62 metres above the way you stand on.

Commaille cleared the temple from 1908 to 1911. Glaize notes that the temple had always sheltered Buddhist pagodas and been a place of pilgrimage for the Khmer.

Why west?

Contested

Most temples of Angkor face east, to the sunrise. Angkor Wat faces west, and why is debated. Louis Finot, George Cœdès, Jean Przyluski and F. D. K. Bosch took it for a funerary temple, built in the king’s lifetime and perhaps meant for his ashes: Bosch found the westward orientation typical of Indo-Javanese funerary monuments, and in funerary rites the ritual walk round the shrine was reversed. Cœdès also pointed out that Vishnu, to whom the temple was dedicated, is usually associated with the west. Others point to a practical cause, the stone brought by water from the Kulen hills; Glaize found that last reason not enough on its own; he himself wrote that the funerary reading ‘seems proven’, but others disagree, and none of these is settled.

The equinox sunrise

The alignment is observed; its intent is not recorded

Around the spring equinox, from about 20 to 23 March and most exactly on the 21st, the sun seen from the western way rises over the top of the central tower. It is a public event: in 2023 the tourism authorities counted 8,726 people at dawn on 21 March. It happens again at the autumn equinox in September.

The archaeologist Im Sokrithy of the APSARA National Authority, which manages Angkor, calls the alignment deliberate, a sign of the builders’ skill in astronomy. Eleanor Mannikka reads it as deliberate too (see the next card), but the builders left no inscription saying so. The model’s sun is aimed from this point over the tower top; its place in the sky is our estimate.

A calendar in stone?

Proposed by single authors · not established

Eleanor Mannikka argued, in Angkor Wat: Time, Space and Kingship (1996), that the temple is laid out as a solar and lunar calendar: converted into an old unit of length, the cubit, the temple’s distances give numbers she reads as solar, lunar and calendrical, with recurring figures such as 54 and 108. Subhash Kak (2001) took the same approach with a Khmer cubit of about 0.435 m and read distances along the axis as the lengths of the Hindu ages of the world, the yugas, while disputing some of her numbers.

The alignments appear only after the survey’s metres are converted into cubits, and small mismatches are put down to error or settling. No independent test of these readings was found for this build; they are best taken as individual scholars’ proposals, not an established result.

Sources: D. Heywood, review of E. Mannikka, Angkor Wat: Time, Space and Kingship (University of Hawaii Press, 1996), Times Higher Education (2 January 1998); S. Kak, “Time, Space and Astronomy in Angkor Wat” (Louisiana State University, 2001).

The moat

Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

With its moat the temple fills a rectangle of about 1,500 by 1,300 metres, nearly 200 hectares. The moat is 190 metres wide and about 5.5 kilometres round; on the west it is crossed by this causeway, 200 metres long and 12 metres wide, and on the east by a bank of earth. Inside, about 30 metres from the water, runs a laterite wall enclosing 1,025 by 800 metres.

Sources: M. Glaize, The Monuments of the Angkor Group (1944), English translation at theangkorguide.com, “Angkor Wat”.

The western gate

Shown as it stands today, simplified Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

The western entrance is a colonnade 235 metres long, its middle a gate in three parts, each crowned by a tower whose upper tiers have fallen. At each end a pavilion let elephants pass at ground level.

Ground-penetrating radar found the foundations of eight towers buried at the western entrance; excavations in 2010 and 2012 confirmed them, and radiocarbon dates suggest they were raised as building began. Till Sonnemann, who led the radar survey, thinks they were deliberately torn down once the outer wall and gate were finished. Their purpose is unknown; one suggestion is a temporary shrine to Vishnu.

Sources: M. Glaize, The Monuments of the Angkor Group (1944), English translation at theangkorguide.com, “Angkor Wat”; B. Bower, “Lasers unveil secrets and mysteries of Angkor Wat”, Science News (29 April 2016), on the studies in Antiquity 89 (December 2015).

The raised way

Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

From the gate to the temple runs a way 350 metres long and 9.4 metres wide overall, raised 1.5 metres above the ground, its edge a balustrade in the form of a naga, a serpent; six stairs go down from each side. Towards its middle stand two buildings known as the ‘libraries’, and nearer the temple it passes between two square pools; the northern one keeps its stone steps and holds water.

Sources: M. Glaize, The Monuments of the Angkor Group (1944), English translation at theangkorguide.com, “Angkor Wat”.

A town under the grass

In April 2012 Damian Evans and colleagues flew lidar, a laser survey from the air, over about 370 square kilometres around Angkor. Inside the moat it showed a grid of roads in four blocks, with mounds and an estimated 250 to 300 small ponds; digs led by Miriam Stark in 2010 and 2013 found modest wooden houses, apparently of the temple’s workers. Just beyond the moat to the south lie six great spiral mounds of earth, each about a kilometre long, whose purpose is unknown. None of this is shown.

Sources: B. Bower, “Lasers unveil secrets and mysteries of Angkor Wat”, Science News (29 April 2016), on the studies in Antiquity 89 (December 2015).

II · The gallery of bas-reliefs

Walk the first gallery past the battle of Kurukshetra, the army of Suryavarman II and the judgement of the dead, then climb through the cruciform cloister to the second level.

The first gallery

Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

The lowest level is ringed by a gallery 187 by 215 metres, its outer side open between pillars. On its inner wall, 2 metres high and more than 1,000 square metres in all, runs a series of reliefs, followed from the west door heading south, anticlockwise round the temple. That is the reverse of the usual walk, which keeps a shrine on one’s right; Glaize connects it with funerary rites.

The reliefs drawn here are our own simple renderings, not copies, and only three of the eight great panels are drawn.

The battle of Kurukshetra

Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified

The southern half of the west gallery shows the great battle of the Mahabharata at Kurukshetra: the Kauravas advance from the left, the Pandavas from the right, and they meet in the middle. Among them Bhishma, the Kauravas’ commander, lies dying, pierced with arrows, and the hero Arjuna rides with his four-armed charioteer, Krishna. Before this battle, in the epic, Krishna teaches Arjuna the Bhagavad Gita.

The army of Suryavarman II

Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified

The western part of the south gallery, about 90 metres, is the only great panel with a historical subject. Above, the king holds audience on his throne; below, his army marches with its chiefs standing on elephants, and the king himself, taller than the rest, under his fifteen parasols. A procession of royal priests and allied troops from Siam follows. Twenty-eight short inscriptions name the chiefs.

Sources: M. Glaize, The Monuments of the Angkor Group (1944), English translation at theangkorguide.com, “Angkor Wat”.

Heavens and hells

Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified

The eastern part of the south gallery, about 60 metres, shows the judgement of the dead. Yama, the many-armed judge, mounted on a buffalo, sends them up to the heavens, rows of palaces with their dwellers, or down to the hells, where they are punished; Glaize counts 37 heavens and 32 hells, and 36 short inscriptions name them. The model draws the hells only as falling figures.

The churning of the sea of milk

Sources differ

Round the corner, in the southern half of the east gallery (not drawn here), is the most famous panel, nearly 50 metres long: gods and demons pull on the serpent Vasuki wound round Mount Mandara, which rests on a tortoise, one of Vishnu’s forms, to churn the ocean of milk for the nectar of immortality. Vishnu presides over the work, and a frieze of apsaras runs along the panel.

The figures are counted differently. Glaize counts 92 demons and 88 gods; Im Sokrithy of the APSARA National Authority counts 91 demons and reads both numbers as days of the solar year between equinoxes and solstices. That calendar reading is his interpretation.

The cruciform cloister

A Buddhist shrine for centuries Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

Between the first and second levels lies a cloister in the form of a cross, with four sunken tanks with carved sides and a single stair in the middle of each, which may have been pools or courtyards. Its southern part is known as Prah Pean (Preah Poan), ‘the thousand Buddhas’: when the temple became Buddhist, monks set Buddha images here, and many have been put back. The model leaves the images out and draws the cloister as a single passage.

From Vishnu to the Buddha

A Buddhist shrine for centuries

Hindu ceremonies went on here into the 13th century; by the 14th or 15th the temple was Buddhist. Monks took away the Brahmanic image from the central sanctuary, walled up its doorways and carved standing Buddhas on them, and replaced missing pillars and lintels with columns taken from other parts of the temple. A long inscription of the early 18th century on the east gate records a funerary monument. Glaize, writing in 1944, says it had always sheltered Buddhist pagodas; the model shows no images of the Buddha or of Vishnu.

III · The central sanctuary

The platform of the five towers, the highest level of the temple, around the central tower.

The five towers

Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

The highest level, a square of towers 60 metres wide at its base, stands on a substructure about 30 metres high, reached by stairs as steep as 70 degrees. Four towers stand at the corners and the fifth, the central one, over the sanctuary. As early as 1866 the Englishman Thomson saw in Angkor Wat the symbol of Mount Meru, the mountain at the centre of the universe, and matched the seven circles of the central tower to the seven mountain chains round Meru (as Glaize reports, after Madame de Coral-Remusat). The towers’ tiered outline, ribbed with recesses and crowned with rows of lotus petals, is simplified here.

Gold under the tower

At the bottom of a well under the central sanctuary, 23 metres down, excavators found the temple’s sacred foundation deposit: two leaves of gold, each about 18 centimetres across, set in laterite. The sanctuary room itself is only about 5 metres wide.

Sources: M. Glaize, The Monuments of the Angkor Group (1944), English translation at theangkorguide.com, “Angkor Wat”.

World Heritage

Angkor was listed as a World Heritage Site in 1992 and at once placed on the list of sites in danger, until 2004. The protected area covers about 400 square kilometres.

Sources: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Angkor” (site 668, inscribed 1992).

About this model

How close is this to the real thing?

From published figures: the moat 190 m wide and the rectangle it encloses about 1,500 by 1,300 m; the enclosure 1,025 by 800 m; the moat causeway 200 by 12 m; the western colonnade 235 m; the raised way 350 m long, 9.4 m wide and 1.5 m high; the first gallery 187 by 215 m with reliefs 2 m high; the second gallery 100 by 115 m; the quincunx of towers 60 m at its base; the central tower 42 m above its platform and 62 m above the way.

Approximate: the heights of the three levels, the places and sizes of the libraries, pools and cruciform terrace, the shapes of the towers and gates, the steepness of the stairs and the sun’s place in the sky. No measured plan could be opened for this build. The reliefs are our own renderings, and only three panels are drawn.

Not shown: the inner galleries of the second and third levels and the cross-shaped galleries of the summit, the cloister’s tanks, the carved apsaras and other carving, the statues and Buddha images, the town and spiral mounds found by lidar, the trees inside the enclosure, the modern stairs and walkways, and the people.
