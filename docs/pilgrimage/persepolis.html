Persepolis

Persepolis, in Persian Pārsa, stands on a great stone terrace at the foot of the Kuh-e Rahmat, the ‘Mountain of Mercy’, on the plain of Marvdasht in Fars, south-western Iran. Darius I began it about 518 BCE; Xerxes I and Artaxerxes I went on building for decades after him. It was a seat of the Achaemenid kings and, above all, a place for royal receptions and festivals. Alexander burned it in 330 BCE.

This walk climbs the Great Stairway, passes through Xerxes’ Gate of All Nations, follows the carved front of the Apadana’s staircase and ends among the columns of its hall. The ruins are shown as they stand, simplified; the reliefs are our own renderings, and the stone is drawn in code.

The Cyrus Cylinder (for comparison)

British Museum, London (BM 90920) · After 539 BCE

Not from Persepolis. A clay cylinder written in Babylonian cuneiform for Cyrus the Great, founder of the Achaemenid empire, after he took Babylon in 539 BCE. It is shown here because the kings who built Persepolis, beginning with Darius a generation later, ruled the empire Cyrus founded, and spoke of it, as he did, in inscriptions of royal power and divine favour.

I · The stairway and the Gate

Climb the Great Stairway onto the terrace and pass between the guardian bulls of Xerxes' Gate of All Nations.

The terrace

Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

Darius chose a spur of the hill for his citadel about 518 BCE and built it up into a platform of about 125,000 square metres, roughly 300 by 455 metres, half rock and half fill. Its western and northern walls rise about 12 metres above the plain. Water channels and drains were cut into the rock, and there was a well 26 metres deep.

Ernst Herzfeld excavated here for the Oriental Institute of Chicago from 1931 to 1934, followed by Erich Schmidt until 1939.

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016); UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Persepolis” (site 114, inscribed 1979).

The Great Stairway

Sources differ Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate Step count and size published; the model's risers adjusted to reach the terrace

The stairway is double and reversed: two flights rise away from each other from the foot of the wall to two landings, then turn and climb back to meet at the top. They have 63 and 48 steps. Each step is 6.7 metres long but only about 10 centimetres high and 38 deep, so the climb is slow and easy. Some writers say that royalty could ride up it on horseback (Mark); Shahbazi rejects this as a popular belief and takes the shallow steps to be for a slow, ceremonial climb. Neither is recorded by the builders.

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016); J. J. Mark, “Persepolis”, World History Encyclopedia (2019, updated 2022).

The Gate of All Nations

Shown as it stands today, simplified Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

At the top of the stairway Xerxes built a square hall of about 612 square metres, its roof carried on four columns over 16.5 metres tall, with three doors: west to the stairway, south towards the Apadana, and east. Great bulls guard the west door; on the east door they are winged and have the heads of bearded men. They follow Assyrian guardians, but stand on four legs, not the Assyrian five.

The walls are gone; the stone door frames with their guardians survive. How many columns stand is simplified here.

Sacred Kingship (comparative chapter)

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016); J. Lendering, Livius.org, “Persepolis, Gate of All Nations”.

Xerxes speaks

A royal claim, in the king's words

On the door jambs, in Old Persian, Babylonian and Elamite, Xerxes names the building. The inscription opens with Ahuramazda: the great god who created the earth, the sky above, man and happiness for man, and who made Xerxes king. Xerxes says that by Ahuramazda’s favour he built this Gate of All Nations, and much else besides, and asks the god to protect him and his kingdom.

The kings’ inscriptions here and at Behistun are the best evidence for how they spoke of their god; how far their faith matched what later Zoroastrians taught is a question for the chapter.

Read the Zoroastrianism chapter · The Behistun Inscription (Vault)

Sources: Livius.org, “XPa” (the inscription of Xerxes on the Gate of All Nations), translation; J. Lendering, Livius.org, “Persepolis, Gate of All Nations”; A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016).

Names in the stone

Later visitors carved their own names on the gate. The Dutch artist Cornelis de Bruijn, who made the first accurate drawings of the ruins in the winter of 1704, cut his here; so did the journalist Henry M. Stanley and others. The model leaves them out.

Sources: J. Lendering, Livius.org, “Persepolis, Gate of All Nations”.

II · The Apadana stairs

The east staircase of the great audience hall, carved with guards, nobles and the gift-bearers of twenty-three nations.

Twenty-three nations

Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

The front of the staircase is carved in three registers. On one wing, guards and Persian and Median dignitaries file towards the centre; on the other, twenty-three delegations from the peoples of the empire bring gifts, eighteen in three rows and five more in a slanting line up the stair, each led by an official and set apart by a cypress tree. In the centre a winged disc hovers between sphinxes.

The figures here are our own simplified outlines, not copies of the carving, and the delegations are not meant to be identified.

Sacred Kingship (comparative chapter)

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016).

The lion and the bull

Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified Contested

At the ends of the staircase a lion leaps onto a bull’s back and bites. The scene is repeated again and again at Persepolis. What it meant is not recorded: Shahbazi suggests it may be astrological, the lion standing for the sun and the bull for the constellation Taurus; no reading can be confirmed.

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016).

Once painted

Shown as it stands today, simplified

The grey stone was not left bare. Small traces of paint survive at Persepolis: blue made from a copper compound and red from ochre, both to be had in Iran. Alexander Nagel and colleagues have studied them on site, and protective glass now covers some of the best-preserved patches. The model shows the stone as it is today.

Sources: Smithsonian Freer|Sackler Archives blog, “A Colorful Past” (10 July 2012), on A. Nagel’s work at Persepolis.

Built for the New Year?

Contested

The processions on these stairs have suggested a festival. Ernst Herzfeld, Friedrich Krefter, Kurt Erdmann, Roman Ghirshman and Edith Porada argued that Persepolis was built for the spring New Year, Nowruz, when the peoples of the empire brought their gifts to the king. Others, among them Carl Nylander, Peter Calmeyer and Ali Mousavi, answer that there is no evidence for a Nowruz celebration in the Achaemenid period at all. The question is open.

Read the Zoroastrianism chapter

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016).

The winged figure

Contested Reliefs: our own rendering, simplified

Above royal scenes at Persepolis hovers a winged disc; in its best-known form a bearded figure rises from it. Who or what it shows is debated. It has been taken for Ahuramazda, for the king’s guardian spirit (the fravashi), and for the divine glory that gave a king his right to rule (the farr), which Shahbazi argues is how the Persians of the time understood it. Today it is the best-known emblem of Zoroastrianism, read by many believers as a summary of their ethics; whether the kings who carved it were Zoroastrians in the later sense is itself debated. The model shows a plain winged disc, simplified, on the stairs’ centre panel.

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016); the archive’s chapter on Zoroastrianism (ch06).

III · The Apadana

Among the columns of the audience hall, nearly twenty metres tall, and the view over the terrace to the royal tombs in the hill.

The audience hall

Shown as it stands today, simplified Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

The Apadana was the largest building on the terrace, raised on its own podium about 3 metres above the court. Its hall, some 60 metres square, had 36 columns; three porticoes of twelve columns each opened to the north, west and east, with towers at the four corners. The columns stood about 19.5 metres tall, slender, UNESCO says, because the roof they carried was of timber, and the walls were more than five metres thick. Thirteen of the 72 columns were still standing in 1977.

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016); UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Persepolis” (site 114, inscribed 1979).

Bulls on the columns

Shown as it stands today, simplified

Each column ended in a capital of two animals joined back to back, bulls or other beasts, on which the roof beams rested. None is shown here: the model’s columns stop where the standing shafts end today.

Sources: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Persepolis” (site 114, inscribed 1979); J. J. Mark, “Persepolis”, World History Encyclopedia (2019, updated 2022).

Gold and silver in the corners

Darius set stone boxes into the corners of the walls. Two were found in 1933 by Friedrich Krefter, each holding a gold and a silver plaque about 33 centimetres square, inscribed in three languages with Darius’s description of his empire.

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016).

The clay archive

Sources differ on the year of the loan (1936 or 1937)

In 1933 the Chicago excavators found tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments in two small rooms of a bastion in the fortification wall. Most are in Elamite and date from around 500 BCE; they record the storage and issue of food, from workers’ rations to supplies for the king’s family. There are Aramaic tablets too (the Chicago project has imaged more than six hundred), and one each in Greek, Phrygian and Old Persian. Wouter Henkelman has used them to study the gods worshipped in the Persian heartland, Elamite and Iranian side by side.

Iran lent the tablets to Chicago for study in the 1930s. Objects from Persepolis held in Chicago were later claimed in a US lawsuit against Iran; in 2018 the Supreme Court ruled that they could not be seized.

The burning

Contested

In 330 BCE, after the Persian treasurer surrendered the citadel, Alexander of Macedon looted Persepolis and burned it. Ancient writers disagree about why. Some tell of a drunken feast at which an Athenian woman, Thaïs, urged the fire; others present it as revenge for the Persians’ burning of the Athenian Acropolis in 480 BCE. Shahbazi argues from the evidence that the fire was deliberate: the precious-metal fittings set into the carved reliefs had been removed first, and the water of the moat was not used to put it out. Its motive is not settled.

Sources: A. Sh. Shahbazi, “Persepolis”, Encyclopaedia Iranica (online, 2009, updated 2016); J. J. Mark, “Persepolis”, World History Encyclopedia (2019, updated 2022).

The tombs in the hill

Layout after published descriptions · positions approximate

Cut into the face of the hill behind the terrace are two royal tombs, usually attributed to Artaxerxes II and Artaxerxes III. Darius I and his successors were buried in rock-cut tombs at Naqsh-e Rostam, a few kilometres away, whose fronts copy a palace front like Darius’s own Tachara, the smaller palace south of this hall.

About this model

How close is this to the real thing?

From published figures: the terrace’s length, 455 m, and its 12 m height (its published width is about 300 m; the model’s is 275 m); the Great Stairway’s 63 and 48 steps, each 6.7 m long; the Gate of All Nations about 612 m² with columns over 16.5 m; the Apadana’s hall 60 m square with 36 columns, its three porticoes of 12, columns about 19.5 m high and 1.6 m thick, its podium about 3 m high and its staircases about 82 m long; the Hall of a Hundred Columns about 68.5 m square.

Approximate: where each building stands and how far apart they are, the shapes of the stairs, which columns stand, the Tachara and the Treasury (outlines only), and the hill. The excavators’ plans could not be checked for this build. Only the northern half of the Great Stairway can be walked; the reliefs are our own simplified outlines.

Not shown: the Palace of Xerxes, the Tripylon, the Harem and other buildings, the mud-brick walls and fortifications, the roofs, the column capitals, the colour, the modern shelters and walkways, and the people.
