Göbekli Tepe

Göbekli Tepe, ‘Potbelly Hill’, is a mound some 15 metres high on a limestone ridge above the Harran plain in south-eastern Turkey. Inside it are round enclosures of stone walls set with T-shaped limestone pillars, among the earliest known monumental architecture, raised from about 9500 BCE by people who, as far as the evidence shows, had no domesticated plants or animals and lived by hunting and gathering.

This walk starts on the rim of the main excavation, goes down among the pillars of Enclosures D and C, and ends at the quarry where the pillars were cut. The layout is modelled after published plans at approximate size; the carvings are our own simplified renderings, and the stone is drawn in code.

I · The hill and the hollow

From the walkway on the rim, look down into the main excavation area: Enclosures A, B, C and D, sunk in the mound.

A hill full of stones

Layout after published plans · positions approximate

A survey by Istanbul and Chicago universities noted the mound in 1963, its slopes clustered with flint, but took the stones on top for an Islamic cemetery. In 1994 Klaus Schmidt of the German Archaeological Institute recognised the ‘tombstones’ as Neolithic T-shaped pillars; excavation began in 1995 and continues under a Turkish–German team. Only a small part of the mound has been dug. Geophysical survey points to at least twenty enclosures, more than ten of them large, most still buried.

Schmidt’s 2003 report (“a minimum of 20 enclosures”); “FAQ”, The Tepe Telegrams (dainst.blog/the-tepe-telegrams/faq/) (“only a small part of the site has been excavated”).

How old?

Radiocarbon dates place the use of the site between about 9500 and 8000 BCE, in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, thousands of years before writing, metal or the wheel, and some six thousand years before the first stage of Stonehenge. Dates from the mortar of the walls show the enclosures were used, rebuilt and altered for centuries; the excavators gave up the original three-layer scheme of the site in favour of this longer, overlapping story.

Enclosures A and B

Layout after published plans · positions approximate

The enclosures are lettered A to D: A, nearest the walkway, was the first found, in 1996; B beyond it was the second, and both of its central pillars, 9 and 10, bear a carved fox. Each enclosure is a ring of dry-stone wall with pillars set into it facing inwards, round a central pair that stand taller than the rest.

Planned as one?

One study's argument Layout after published plans · positions approximate

In 2020 Gil Haklay and Avi Gopher argued that Enclosures B, C and D were laid out together, on a plan: the centres of the three enclosures form a nearly equilateral triangle, with each central pair set on its enclosure’s centre point, and an axis drawn square to one side runs through the centre of D. If they are right, the builders planned with geometry before they farmed. It is one study’s argument, not an agreed result, and the triangle here is drawn on our approximate layout, so it shows the idea, not a measurement.

Read the Neolithic chapter

Sources: G. Haklay and A. Gopher, “Geometry and Architectural Planning at Göbekli Tepe, Turkey”, Cambridge Archaeological Journal 30.2 (2020), 343–357.

Temple, or houses?

Contested

Klaus Schmidt’s team first read Göbekli Tepe as a purely ritual site: a mountaintop sanctuary raised by hunter-gatherers, the ‘world’s first temples’. In 2011 Edward Banning argued that the round buildings might be houses, rich in symbolism but domestic, since in this period sacred and everyday life were not kept apart; in 2017 the excavators answered that the evidence still points to special buildings for cult and ritual. Since 2015 they have found buildings that were probably dwellings, many grinding tools used mostly for cereals, a burial under a floor, and a rock-cut pit and channels that may have gathered water. Lee Clare has written that a revision of the popular ‘temple’ picture is now unavoidable.

II · Enclosure D

A very well preserved enclosure: the two 5.5-metre central pillars with arms, hands and belts, and the Vulture Stone in the wall.

Enclosure D

Layout after published plans · positions approximate

D is in an excellent state of preservation, and a 2012 report in Current World Archaeology calls it the largest: a ring of rough stone wall with a bench along its inner face, eleven pillars found so far set into the wall, and two taller pillars standing free in the middle on the smoothed bedrock. Foxes and snakes are the commonest animals carved here, with boar, aurochs, gazelle, wild ass, birds and others.

Göbekli Tepe's Pillars (Vault) · Read the Neolithic chapter

Sources: Current World Archaeology 53 (2012), “Turkey: Göbekli Tepe”; German Archaeological Institute (DAI), Göbekli Tepe project, The Tepe Telegrams (dainst.blog).

The central pair: Pillars 18 and 31

Carvings: our own rendering, simplified Height published; other sizes approximate

Each of the two central pillars is about 5.5 metres tall and weighs some eight to ten tonnes. They are not just posts. Arms are carved down their broad sides, bent at the elbow, and the hands meet across the narrow front above a belt, with a loincloth below it. Pillar 18 also appears to carry a fox under its arm. The T-shaped top is the head, without a face. The excavators read the T-shape as an abstract human body seen from the side.

Who these beings are is not known. The excavators have asked whether they are ancestors or even gods and leave it open; nothing written survives to say.

The Vulture Stone (Pillar 43)

Carvings: our own rendering, simplified Not supported by the evidence

Pillar 43, set in the wall, is crowded with carving: a vulture with a raised wing beside a disc, birds and beasts above, and, lower down, smaller creatures and a headless man. In 2017 two engineers, Martin Sweatman and Dimitrios Tsikritsis, read the animals as constellations and the pillar as a record of a comet strike about 10,950 BCE.

The excavators answered that the earliest date for Enclosure D is some 700 to 1000 years later than that, that the reading picks a few pillars out of more than sixty, ignores the carving on the pillar’s narrow sides, and assumes that hunters of the tenth millennium BCE saw the same constellations that Egyptian, Arabian and Greek scholars named thousands of years later.

Were the enclosures aimed at the stars?

Contested

Several writers have looked for sky alignments here. In 2016 the astronomer Giulio Magli suggested that the enclosures were laid out to watch the rising of Sirius, a brilliant star that, by the slow wobble of the earth’s axis, had just begun to appear above the southern horizon in this period.

The excavators have not, as far as this archive found, answered Magli directly. Writing in 2017 about another sky reading (the Vulture Stone, above), they said that no convincing proof of a celestial orientation had been put forward, and that the older enclosures were sunk into the ground and possibly roofed, which would make poor observatories. The question remains open, but no alignment has been demonstrated.

III · Enclosure C

Rings of wall inside rings of wall, and the broken central pillars on their pedestals cut from the bedrock.

Enclosure C

Layout after published plans · positions approximate Carvings: our own rendering, simplified

C is ringed by three, perhaps four, concentric walls, set with pillars; a 2012 report in Current World Archaeology calls it the most impressive of the enclosures. Its two central pillars, numbered 35 and 37, were found broken; they stood on pedestals carved out of the smoothed bedrock floor. Pillar 35, pieced together from laser scans of its fragments, was about five metres tall. Wild boar dominate the carvings here. On Pillar 27 a predator in high relief, carved from the same block, crouches beside a boar in flat relief.

Buried on purpose?

Contested

The enclosures were found full of rubble, flint and animal bone. For years the excavators read this as deliberate backfilling, a ritual burial of each building at the end of its life, and the archive’s Neolithic chapter still says so. In its summary of the 2015–2019 work, the excavation team argued instead that the fill slid in from the higher slopes round about, which would explain why it mixes material of different ages. The question is now contested.

Read the Neolithic chapter

Sources: L. Clare, “Göbekli Tepe, Turkey: a brief summary of research at a new World Heritage Site (2015–2019)”, e-Forschungsberichte des DAI 2020.2, 81–88; the archive’s Neolithic chapter (ch42).

Open to the sky, or roofed?

Contested

No roof survives. Some reconstructions show open rings; the excavators now see a ‘significant possibility’ that the enclosures were roofed, with the walls and pillars carrying the load. Like everything about how these buildings were used, it is a reading of the evidence, not a finding.

Sources: J. Notroff, O. Dietrich, L. Clare and others, “More than a vulture: a response to Sweatman and Tsikritsis”, Mediterranean Archaeology and Archaeometry 17.2 (2017), 57–63 (“significant possibility”); “Archaeoastronomy, meteor showers, mass extinction: What does the fox say?”, The Tepe Telegrams (21 April 2017).

IV · The quarry

The limestone plateau where the pillars were cut, and the seven-metre pillar that was never finished.

Where the pillars came from

Size published; position approximate

The pillars were cut from the limestone of the plateau round the mound, part of the protected site. The workers used flint: no metal was known. On the plateau’s western edge lies a pillar that was never finished: about seven metres long and one and a half thick, it would have weighed some fifty tonnes, larger than any found standing. It may have cracked as it was cut, and was left where it lay.

Göbekli Tepe's Pillars (Vault) · Read the Neolithic chapter

Sources: O. Dietrich, “How did they do it? Making and moving monoliths at Göbekli Tepe”, The Tepe Telegrams (3 May 2016).

How were they moved?

Not supported by the evidence

The quarries lie close by: the pillars travelled less than 100 metres at best and about 500 at most. How pillars of eight to ten tonnes were moved and raised into their sockets is not recorded. The excavators suggest wooden beams and wedges to lift each piece from the bedrock and, from parallels in Indonesia, sledges and wooden planks to haul it, with many hands. Claims that the work needed a lost advanced civilisation are not supported: the tools, the debris and the unfinished pillar all point to people working stone with flint.

Sources: O. Dietrich, “How did they do it? Making and moving monoliths at Göbekli Tepe”, The Tepe Telegrams (3 May 2016); German Archaeological Institute (DAI), Göbekli Tepe project, The Tepe Telegrams (dainst.blog).

About this model

How close is this to the real thing?

From published figures: the central pillars of Enclosure D at about 5.5 m and the wall pillars at up to about 4 m; eleven pillars in D’s wall round the central pair; the round enclosures at roughly 10 to 30 m across, with three or four rings of wall in C; and the unfinished pillar in the quarry, about 7 m long and 1.5 m thick.

Approximate: how big each enclosure is (a 2012 report in Current World Archaeology calls D the largest; here C’s outer ring is drawn wider), where each lies and how far apart they are, the place of every pillar, the shapes of the walls and the depth of the hollow. The carvings are our own simplified renderings in the manner of the reliefs, not tracings of any pillar, and most pillars here are left plain.

Not shown: the modern shelter roof and its supports, the later rectangular buildings, the excavated houses, the cisterns, the other buried enclosures, and the people. The ramp into the hollow is the model’s own.
