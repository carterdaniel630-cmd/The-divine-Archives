# Chapter 29 — Aztec, Maya & Inca: The Temple Religions of the Americas

Three great civilizations of the pre-Columbian Americas — the Maya of the rainforest, the Aztec of the Mexican lake, and the Inca of the Andes — and the temple religions of blood, calendar, and divine kingship they built, reconstructed from ruins, deciphered glyphs, frozen mummies, and the records of the conquerors who destroyed them.

## The child on the summit

In 1999, on the frozen summit of **Llullaillaco**, a volcano on the border of Argentina and Chile more than 22,000 feet high, archaeologists uncovered three children. They had been placed there some five hundred years before, drugged with maize beer and coca, and left to die in the cold — and the cold had kept them so perfectly that the eldest, a girl of about thirteen, looks less like a corpse than like someone asleep, an ember of tissue still in her lungs. She is a **capacocha**, an Inca offering of the most precious thing the empire had: a chosen, perfect child, sent to live among the gods on the roof of the world.

She is also a warning about how to tell this story. Human sacrifice was real in the religions of the ancient Americas — the ground proves it beyond dispute. But almost everything else we think we know came to us through the hands of the Spanish who conquered these peoples, burned their books, and had every reason to paint them as monsters. To describe the Aztec, the Maya, and the Inca honestly is to hold the reality and the propaganda apart, and to see three of the most sophisticated religious civilizations the world has produced.

## Three worlds

These were not one people but three, in two very different lands. In **Mesoamerica** — the tropical world of present-day Mexico and Guatemala — rose the **Maya**, whose great cities flourished from roughly 250 to 900 CE and whose descendants endured, and the **Aztec** (properly the **Mexica**), whose empire crested from 1428 until the Spanish came in 1519. In the **Andes** of South America rose the **Inca**, whose vast empire, the largest in the pre-Columbian Americas, ran from about 1438 until its fall in 1533. They never knew one another. Yet they built strikingly parallel worlds: monumental **temple-pyramids**, elaborate **calendars** wedded to astronomy, **divine or solar kingship**, and the conviction that the cosmos ran on a debt of **blood** the living were bound to pay.

## The Maya: the people of the maize and the glyph

The Maya achieved what no other people of the ancient Americas did: a **complete writing system**. Their **hieroglyphs** — a logo-syllabic script of several hundred signs — recorded history, myth, and astronomy on stone monuments and in bark-paper books; their **decipherment**, largely a triumph of the twentieth century, reopened a literature the conquest had nearly erased. Only **four** of their books survive the Spanish burnings.

Their sacred epic, the **Popol Vuh** — transcribed from older sources into the Latin alphabet around 1554 — tells how the gods, after failed attempts with mud and wood, made human beings at last from **maize**; and how the **Hero Twins** descended into **Xibalba**, the underworld, to outwit its lords of death. The Maya cosmos was ruled by **Itzamna**, the creator and patron of writing, **Kʼinich Ajaw** the sun, **Chaac** the rain, and **Kukulkan** the feathered serpent. Its kings were **kʼuhul ajaw**, "holy lords," who bound their people to the gods through **bloodletting**: a king or queen would draw a thorned rope through the tongue or genitals, and from the rising smoke of the burnt blood the **Vision Serpent** appeared, an ancestor or god speaking from its jaws. Humans, made of maize, owed a **body debt**, repaid in blood, that kept the gods — and so the world — alive.

And the famous **calendar**: the **Long Count**, a cycle of some 5,125 years begun in 3114 BCE, whose thirteenth *bakʼtun* closed on 21 December 2012. It did not, in any Maya text, mean the end of the world — only the turning of a great cycle, like an odometer rolling over. The "**2012 apocalypse**" was a modern Western invention, made by non-Maya, that the Maya themselves never held.

## The Aztec: the debt of the Fifth Sun

The **Mexica** of **Tenochtitlan** built their island capital around the **Templo Mayor**, a great twin pyramid crowned by two shrines — to **Huitzilopochtli**, the war-and-sun god who led them to their promised land, and to **Tlaloc**, the rain god. Their cosmology was one of **cyclical catastrophe**: four previous worlds, four "**Suns**," had each been created and destroyed, and the present **Fifth Sun** was itself doomed. To make it move at all, the myth said, the gods had **sacrificed themselves**; and so the sun, and the fragile order of the world, had to be sustained by human blood in return. Sacrifice, for the Mexica, was not the appeasement of an angry god but the **repayment of a cosmic debt** — the maintenance of a universe that would otherwise run down.

It happened, and archaeology confirms it: excavations at the Templo Mayor have uncovered the **Huei Tzompantli**, the great skull rack, with hundreds of skulls of sacrificed men, women, and children. But the scale must be told honestly. Spanish chroniclers claimed **80,400** people were killed in four days at the temple's rededication in 1487 — a figure that is **physically impossible** (it would require a killing every few seconds around the clock) and served the conquerors' need to cast the Mexica as savages worth destroying. The real toll, modern scholars estimate, ran to the **low thousands a year** across the empire: terrible, systematic, and real — but not the cartoon of the propaganda.

## The ballgame: the court between the worlds

Across Mesoamerica, from the Maya cities to Aztec Tenochtitlan, ran a ritual older than either: the **ballgame** (Maya *pitz*, Aztec *ōllamaliztli*), played for at least three thousand years on great I-shaped stone courts with a heavy rubber ball driven by the hips. It was sport, but it was never only sport. The bouncing rubber ball was read as the **sun**, and the walled court as the seam between the earth and the underworld, so that the game re-enacted the cosmic contest at the heart of the **Popol Vuh** — the **Hero Twins**, Hunahpu and Xbalanque, playing ball against the lords of death in **Xibalba** and defeating them, to be reborn as sun and moon. To play was to perform that struggle of light against darkness, life against death, and so to keep the cosmos in motion. And the game could end in **sacrifice**: reliefs at Chichén Itzá and El Tajín show a decapitated player, and in some rites the losers — or, some evidence suggests, the winners — were offered to the gods, their blood feeding the same cosmic order the game itself sustained.

## The Inca: the empire of the Sun

The **Inca** ruled their immense Andean empire from **Cusco**, whose golden **Coricancha**, the Temple of the Sun, was the empire's holiest place. At the head of the pantheon stood **Inti**, the sun, from whom the emperor — the **Sapa Inca** — claimed direct descent, making him a living divinity; behind Inti stood **Viracocha**, the remote creator, and **Pachamama**, the earth mother. The land itself was alive with **huacas** — sacred places and objects: a spring, a peculiar stone, a mountain, or a mummy, each inhabited by a spirit and given offerings. The Inca kept the **mummies of their dead emperors** as the holiest huacas of all, feeding them, dressing them, and carrying them out to be consulted. They recorded their world not in writing but in the **quipu**, an intricate system of knotted and colored cords — an accounting and record technology, much of it still undeciphered. And at the great turning points of the imperial life they performed the **capacocha**, sending chosen children to die on the high peaks, as the girl of Llullaillaco died.

## Common threads

Across these separate worlds run deep rhymes. The **feathered serpent** — Kukulkan to the Maya, **Quetzalcoatl** to the Aztec — is worshipped across all Mesoamerica. **Solar and divine kingship** binds ruler to sun in every case. Time is **cyclical**, not linear, and read from the sky. Temples are **mountains of stone** aligned to solstice and equinox. And everywhere the cosmos is understood as something that must be actively **fed and maintained** by ritual and blood, not a machine that runs on its own. Where they differ is as telling: the Maya wrote, and the Inca — alone among great empires — did not, entrusting their memory to the knotted cord instead.

## The gods that would not die

The conquest shattered the temples, but it did not simply erase the religions beneath them. In the Andes in the **1560s**, a generation after the fall, a movement called **Taki Onqoy** ("dancing sickness") swept the highlands: its preachers taught that the **huacas** — the old sacred powers, led by Pachacamac and Titicaca — were rising, entering the bodies of the faithful, and massing to defeat the Christian God and drown the Spaniards in the returning sea, restoring the Andean world. It was crushed, yet the archaeology of its centers shows something subtler than defiance: the same people were buried in Christian churches *and* kept the old rites — **entangled** in both religions at once, not simply choosing one. That entanglement is the real story of survival across the Americas. In Mexico the veneration of the **Virgin of Guadalupe**, appearing in 1531 on the hill of Tepeyac where a mother-goddess had been honored, gathered indigenous devotion into a Christian form; the offerings to **Pachamama**, the eating of maize-dough figures, and the festivals of the dead did not vanish but wove themselves into the calendar of the Church. The temple religions were broken as institutions, but their substance survived — hidden, blended, and alive — in the folk Catholicism of Latin America to this day.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

These were civilizations of dense, deliberate **encoding**.

- **Maya writing** is the great achievement: a true logo-syllabic script in which signs stand for whole words or syllables, carved on stelae and painted in the codices, whose modern **decipherment** let the ancient Maya speak again. The **Dresden Codex** records the motions of Venus with startling precision.
- The **calendars** are encoded time itself: the Maya **Long Count** and the interlocking Aztec **tonalpohualli** (260 days) and **xiuhpohualli** (365 days), meshing into a 52-year "**Calendar Round**." The great Aztec **Sun Stone** (*Piedra del Sol*) is a carved cosmogram of the Five Suns — popularly, and wrongly, called "the Aztec calendar."
- The Inca **quipu** encodes data in three dimensions — the type of knot, its position on the cord, the cord's color and ply — a recording system unlike any other, and one we can still only partly read.
- And the temples encode the heavens in stone: at **Chichén Itzá**, the pyramid of Kukulkan is built so that, at the equinoxes, the afternoon light casts a serpent of shadow slithering down its stair.

A necessary honesty, as this book requires: the **2012 doomsday** was never Maya; the **80,000-in-four-days** sacrifice was never real; and the tidy, total pictures we are often given of these religions are reconstructions, built from partial decipherment, fragmentary survivals, and the testimony of enemies.

## Connections

The temple religions of the Americas developed in complete isolation from the traditions in the rest of this book — which is precisely what makes their parallels so striking: **divine kingship**, **cyclical cosmic time**, **blood offering to sustain the world**, and **temple-mountains** aligned to the sky arose here entirely independently, as they had in the Bronze Age **Egypt** and **Mesopotamia** of the first era. They are the New World's own answer to the oldest religious questions, reached without a single borrowed idea — and, within a generation of the Spanish arrival, they were shattered, their books burned, their temples pulled down, and their peoples forced toward the cross.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The cities, temples, and monuments (Tenochtitlan's Templo Mayor, the Maya cities, Cusco's Coricancha); the Maya **writing system** and its decipherment, the **Long Count** and the meshing Aztec calendars, and the **quipu**; the **Popol Vuh** (via its 1554 transcription); and the **reality of human sacrifice**, archaeologically confirmed by the Templo Mayor skull rack and the Inca mountain mummies. The **Mesoamerican ballgame** and its stone courts, its link to the Popol Vuh Hero Twins, and its ties to sacrifice; and the post-conquest **survival** of these religions — the **Taki Onqoy** movement of the 1560s (attested in colonial records and in the archaeology of its centers) and the syncretic devotion (the Virgin of Guadalupe, continued Pachamama offerings) that carried the old substance into folk Catholicism.

**Not supported — propaganda and modern myth.** The **inflated sacrifice numbers** of the Spanish chroniclers (the 80,400 of 1487 is logistically impossible and served the conquest); and the "**Maya 2012 apocalypse**," a modern Western fabrication with no basis in any Maya source.

**Genuinely open.** Much Maya myth is still reconstructed from **partial decipherment** and iconography; the **Popol Vuh** and most of what we know reach us only through post-conquest, often hostile, transcription (Bishop Landa burned the Maya books even as his own account became the key to reading them); the **quipu** remains largely unread; and the inner theology behind many practices is seen, unavoidably, mostly through the eyes of the people who destroyed these religions.
