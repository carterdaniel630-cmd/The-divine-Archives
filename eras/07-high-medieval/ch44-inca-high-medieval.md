# Chapter 44 — The Inca: The Empire of the Sun

*Recently added — pending full review.*

The religion of the largest empire the Americas ever knew — a living sun-emperor, a landscape wired with sacred lines and shrines, the mummified dead who still ruled, and children carried to die on the roof of the world — reconstructed from stone, frozen bodies, knotted cords, and the records of the conquerors who overthrew it, with a steady eye on how much of it we can actually read.

## The child on the summit

In 1999, on the frozen summit of **Llullaillaco**, a volcano on the border of Argentina and Chile more than 22,000 feet high, archaeologists uncovered three children who had been placed there some five hundred years before. Drugged with maize beer and coca and left in the cold, they had frozen so perfectly that the eldest, a girl of about thirteen, looks less like a corpse than like someone asleep. She is a **capacocha**, an Inca offering of the most precious thing the empire had: a chosen, perfect child, sent to live among the gods on the highest peaks of the world. She is also a lesson in how to tell this story — for almost everything we know beyond her frozen body reached us through the Spanish who conquered her people, and the honest task is to hold the archaeology and the chronicles carefully apart.

## Tawantinsuyu: the empire of the four quarters

The **Inca** ruled **Tawantinsuyu**, "the Four Quarters Together" — the largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas, running the length of the Andes from Colombia to Chile, welded together in barely a century (c. 1438–1533) from their capital at **Cusco**, before the Spanish under Pizarro shattered it. Their cosmos was divided into three worlds, the three **pachas**: **Hanan Pacha**, the upper world of the sun, moon, and gods; **Kay Pacha**, "this world," the middle realm of the living; and **Uku Pacha**, the inner/under world of the dead, of seeds, and of fertility, reached through caves and springs.

## The gods and the divine emperor

At the head of the pantheon stood **Viracocha** (Wiraqocha), the remote creator who made the world, the sun, and humankind at **Lake Titicaca**, then withdrew. Closer and blazing was **Inti**, the sun — giver of warmth and of the maize harvest, and the divine ancestor from whom the emperor, the **Sapa Inca**, claimed direct descent, which made him a **living god** and gave the empire its right to rule. Around them moved **Mama Killa**, the moon (Inti's sister-wife, who governed the calendar and festivals); **Illapa**, the thunder-and-weather god who brought the rains; **Pachamama**, the earth mother who fed the world; and **Mama Cocha**, the sea. The founding myth told that Inti sent his children **Manco Capac** and Mama Ocllo out of Lake Titicaca to found Cusco with a golden staff — the sun's own dynasty planted on earth.

## The Coricancha and the wired landscape

Cusco's holiest place was the **Coricancha**, the "Golden Enclosure," the Temple of the Sun, whose walls were once sheathed in sheets of gold to catch Inti's light and whose garden held life-size plants and animals worked in gold and silver. From the Coricancha radiated the most remarkable feature of Inca religion: the **ceque system**, some **forty-one invisible lines** running out across the landscape, strung with over **three hundred huacas** — sacred places and objects, a spring, a peculiar stone, a cave, a mountain, a battlefield — each assigned to a kin group (*ayllu*) that tended it and made its offerings on the proper day. The whole territory around Cusco was thus a vast, living **calendar and map of the sacred**, the city's social order written onto the land. And the land itself was alive with **huacas** everywhere: the Andes were a holy geography, its peaks (*apus*) powerful spirits in their own right.

## The dead who still ruled

Nowhere was Inca religion stranger, or more consequential, than in its treatment of the **dead emperors**. The Sapa Inca's body was mummified at death and became a **mallqui** — not a relic but a still-living person: fed, dressed, brought out to festivals, consulted for advice, and served by a dedicated kin-corporation, the **panaca**, that kept his palaces, lands, and servants *in his name forever*. Because a dead emperor kept everything he had owned, each new Sapa Inca inherited the throne but not the wealth, and had to conquer his own — a system of "split inheritance" that historians think helped drive the empire's furious expansion. Ordinary ancestors too were preserved and honored as mallquis, brought out and fed: the dead remained members of the community, the Andean ancestor-cult reaching all the way up to the god-kings.

## Capacocha, the oracle, and the calendar

Sacrifice was mostly of llamas, maize, chicha beer, and coca; but at the great crises and turning-points of imperial life — a coronation, a plague, a drought, the death of an emperor — came the **capacocha**, in which chosen, physically perfect **children** from across the empire were feted, married, paraded to Cusco, and then carried to the summits of the sacred mountains to die of cold and be received by the gods, as the girl of Llullaillaco died. The empire also maintained and consulted **oracles**, above all the ancient coastal shrine of **Pachacamac** near Lima — so powerful that the Inca, rather than destroy it, built beside it and folded it into their state religion. And the year turned on a calendar of monthly festivals, greatest among them **Inti Raymi**, the sun-festival of the June solstice, when Cusco feasted the sun with sacrifice, dance, and the parading of the royal mummies.

## The believer's lens

To the people of Tawantinsuyu, the world was saturated with the sacred and bound together by **reciprocity** (*ayni*). One did not merely believe in the gods; one *fed* them — the sun, the earth, the mountains, the ancestors — and they, fed, gave rain, harvest, and life in return. The Sapa Inca was not a symbol of the divine but its living presence, the sun's own son, whose rule and whose person guaranteed the cosmic order; to serve him was to serve Inti. The dead did not depart but remained, dressed and fed among the living, so that the community spanned the generations and the ancestors' power stayed available. The huacas and the ceque lines made the very land a body of the sacred, and tending one's assigned shrine on its day was to keep one's thread in the great weave that held Cusco and the cosmos together. Even the capacocha, terrible as it is to us, was understood as the highest honor and a gift of life, not its waste: the chosen child did not die but went to dwell with the gods on the mountain, becoming a guardian huaca for its people. Seen from inside, the Inca world was a single vast circulation of gift and return, binding sun, earth, dead, and living into one order.

## The skeptical lens

The Inca are, in one crucial respect, the hardest of the three American religions to know: **they had no writing**. Alone among the world's great empires they kept their records not in script but in the **quipu**, and though these encode numbers and, probably, more, we still cannot fully read them — so the Inca cannot speak to us directly. Almost everything narrative we have comes through the **Spanish chronicles** and the mestizo and native writers who followed them — Cieza de León, Betanzos, the part-Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, the indigenous Guaman Poma — each with his own agenda, writing decades after the conquest, often to justify it or to plead against it. The result is that the tidy pantheon and mythology in the books above are partly a **Spanish ordering** of a messier, more local reality; the neat elevation of Viracocha to a supreme creator, in particular, may owe something to missionaries seeking an Andean "God." What the archaeology confirms is stark and real: the gold-stripped Coricancha, the ceque huacas, the royal mummy cult (the Spanish hunted down and destroyed the imperial mallquis precisely because of their power), and the frozen capacocha children on the peaks. After the conquest, the **extirpation of idolatries** campaigns tried to stamp the huaca religion out — and the millenarian **Taki Onqoy** ("dancing sickness") movement of the 1560s, in which the huacas were said to be rising to drive the Spanish into the sea, shows how alive it remained.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

The Inca encoded their world not in writing but in **cord, line, and gold**.

- The **quipu** (khipu) is the great Inca encoding: a system of knotted, dyed, and plied cords in which the *type* of knot, its *position* on the cord, the cord's *color*, and its *ply* all carried meaning — a three-dimensional record technology, decimal in its numbers, read by trained specialists (the *khipukamayuq*), and still only partly deciphered. It is one of the world's few major information systems that is not a script.
- The **ceque system** encoded the sacred landscape itself: the 41 lines and 328 huacas radiating from the Coricancha wove together Cusco's geography, its social groups, its water and its calendar into a single diagram on the land — the empire's cosmology walked rather than written.
- **Gold** was "the sweat of the sun," silver "the tears of the moon" — not currency (the Inca had none) but the encoded substance of the divine, which is why the Coricancha blazed with it and why its loss to Spanish greed was a desecration, not merely a theft.
- Andean **textiles**, the most prized of all Inca goods, encoded rank, ethnicity, and meaning in their geometric *tocapu* designs — cloth as a bearer of identity and the sacred. (The stepped "Andean cross," the *chakana*, is often read today as an ancient Inca symbol of the three worlds, though how far that specific meaning is pre-Columbian rather than modern is genuinely uncertain.)

## Connections

The Inca are the third of the great temple religions of the pre-Columbian Americas in this book, beside the **Maya** (Chapter 29) and the **Aztec** (Chapter 43) — and the most different of the three, an Andean rather than Mesoamerican world, without their writing, ballgame, or 260-day calendar. Yet the deep parallels hold across all three and across the ocean: **divine kingship**, a **sun at the center**, **temple-shrines aligned to the solstices**, and the **ancestor cult** — the Inca mummy-kings a vivid extreme of the honored dead that runs from the Neolithic (Chapter 42) through Egypt (Chapter 2) to the African traditions (Chapter 33). That these forms arose in the Andes in complete isolation is, as with the Maya and Aztec, the strongest testimony in this book that the great shapes of religion recur wherever human beings face the same questions — and, within a generation of Pizarro's arrival, this one too was pulled down, its temples stripped and its mummies burned.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The empire and its capital; the **Coricancha** and the **ceque system** of huacas; the cult of **Inti** and the divine **Sapa Inca**; the **royal mummy (mallqui) / panaca** ancestor cult (attested archaeologically and by the Spanish who destroyed the mummies); the **capacocha** child sacrifice (the frozen mummies of Llullaillaco and other peaks); the **quipu**; the **Pachacamac** oracle; and the post-conquest **extirpation** campaigns and the Taki Onqoy movement.

**Not supported / heavily filtered.** Because the Inca left **no writing**, the narrative mythology and the tidy pantheon reach us only through post-conquest, often hostile or Christianizing **Spanish sources**; the elevation of **Viracocha** to a single supreme creator may be partly a missionary construction, and much "Inca theology" is a colonial ordering of a more local, various reality.

**Genuinely open.** The **quipu** remains largely unread, so the Inca's own records are still mostly closed to us. The precise **theology** behind the practices is seen through the eyes of the conquerors. And the antiquity of some now-popular symbols (the *chakana* "Andean cross") is uncertain. We possess the Inca's stones, cords, and frozen dead in abundance; their inner voice we have barely begun to hear.
