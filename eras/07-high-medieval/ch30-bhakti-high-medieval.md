The devotional revolution that swept across medieval India — a religion of passionate, personal love for God, sung in the mother tongue by weavers and washerwomen and untouchables, needing no priest, no temple, and no Sanskrit, only the heart — and the honest distinction between what the movement taught, what history can date, and where devotion has wrapped its saints in legend.

## The flowers under the shroud

When **Kabir** died, around 1518, the story goes that his followers fell to quarrelling over his body. He had been raised a Muslim and revered by Hindus, and now the Muslims wished to bury him and the Hindus to burn him. As they argued over the corpse, someone lifted the shroud — and found beneath it not a body but a heap of **flowers**. The Hindus took half to cremate and the Muslims half to bury, and the poet-saint who had spent his life mocking the boundary between the two slipped free of it even in death.

The tale is almost certainly a legend, and it is the right place to begin — because Bhakti is a tradition whose truth lives in its poetry and whose saints live, very often, in devout legend rather than firm history. What is beyond doubt is the movement itself: one of the great religious upheavals of the medieval world, a tide of love that broke the monopoly of priest and Sanskrit and caste, and remade the spiritual life of a subcontinent.

## What Bhakti is

**Bhakti** — from a Sanskrit root meaning "to share, to participate, to love" — is **devotion**: the path of intense, personal, self-giving love for a personal God, held to be superior to ritual sacrifice, to philosophical knowledge, and to ascetic works. Its claim was radical in its simplicity. To reach God one needs no **priest**, no **temple**, no **Sanskrit**, no ritual purity, and no high birth — only **love** (*prema*), **surrender** (*prapatti*), and the constant repetition of the divine **name**. That claim threw the doors of Hindu religious life open to everyone the old order had shut out: the low-caste, the untouchable, the illiterate, and women — many of whom became the movement's greatest saints.

## From the Tamil south

Bhakti's scriptural seed lay in the **Bhagavad Gita**, which had already named loving devotion as a path to God. But the movement itself was born in the **Tamil south**, between the sixth and ninth centuries, in the songs of two bands of poet-saints. The **Alvars** — twelve devotees of **Vishnu**, among them the woman saint **Andal** — poured out hymns of love gathered into the *Nalayira Divya Prabandham*. The **Nayanars** — some sixty-three devotees of **Shiva** — sang the hymns collected in the *Tevaram*. They sang not in Sanskrit but in **Tamil**, challenged caste and Brahmin privilege, and set the pattern the whole movement would follow. From the south it spread, over the following centuries, across the whole of India, cresting between the fourteenth and seventeenth.

## The philosophers of devotion

Bhakti found its intellectual defender in **Ramanuja** (1017–1137), who answered the austere non-dualism of Shankara — in which the individual self dissolves wholly into an impersonal Absolute — with **Vishishtadvaita**, "qualified non-dualism," in which God and the soul remain distinct enough that love between them is real and eternal. Crucially, Ramanuja taught that even **shudras and outcastes** could reach salvation by complete surrender. **Madhva** pressed further into outright dualism. Between them they gave the religion of the heart a rigorous philosophical spine — and preserved the one thing devotion cannot do without: a real Other to love.

## Saguna and nirguna

The movement divided over a deep question: does God have form? **Saguna** bhakti loves God **with** qualities and form — **Krishna** with his flute, **Rama**, **Shiva**, the **Goddess** — present in the image and the temple, approached through story and festival. **Nirguna** bhakti loves the **formless**, attributeless Absolute beyond all image, and in its boldest voices grew hostile to temple and mosque alike, to idol and to scripture, insisting that the God worth loving cannot be carved or caged. The line between them runs through the whole tradition.

## The love of Radha: Jayadeva's *Gita Govinda*

If Bhakti had a single supreme song of divine love, it was composed in this very era. Around 1190, the Bengali poet **Jayadeva** wrote the **Gita Govinda** ("Song of Govinda"), a Sanskrit lyric cycle of twelve cantos and twenty-four *ashtapadis* — songs meant to be sung and danced — on the springtime love of **Krishna** and the cowherd **Radha**: their longing, their jealousy, their separation, and their rapturous union. It is unashamedly **erotic**, and that is the theology: the anguish of the lovers apart (*vipralambha*) and the ecstasy of their meeting become the very image of the soul's separation from and reunion with God. From it flowered the exalted mood the tradition calls **madhurya bhava** — loving God as the beloved, the "sweet" and most intimate of all devotional relationships — and with it the raising of **Radha** into the supreme devotee, the human heart's own stand-in before the divine. The *Gita Govinda* passed into temple liturgy and classical dance across India and became, in the next era, the fountainhead of Chaitanya's ecstatic Bengali Krishna-devotion.

## The saints

The soul of Bhakti is its **saints** — a great, unruly company drawn from every corner of society.

- In **Karnataka**, **Basava** (Basavanna, twelfth century) founded the **Lingayat** (Virashaiva) movement, rejecting caste, temple, and Brahmin ritual, and pouring his teaching into terse **vachana** prose-poems in Kannada; the woman saint **Akka Mahadevi** sang beside him.
- In **Maharashtra**, **Jnaneshwar** (thirteenth century) wrote the *Jnaneshwari*, a luminous Marathi commentary on the Gita, and **Namdev** sang his **abhangs** to **Vithoba** of Pandharpur, building the enduring **Varkari** pilgrim tradition of communal song.
- In the **north**, **Ramananda** carried the movement's fire, and from his circle came its most famous voice: **Kabir** (fifteenth century), the weaver of Banaras, a **nirguna** poet who ridiculed the pretensions of Hindu and Muslim alike and sought the one God beyond both — whose verses were later gathered into the **Sikh scripture** itself. Beside him stood **Ravidas**, the leather-worker saint. The later blooms — **Mirabai**, **Surdas**, **Tulsidas**, and **Chaitanya** of Bengal — belong mostly to the following era.

## The women who sang

No feature of Bhakti is more striking than the number of its greatest voices who were **women** — and how radically their devotion defied the world that bound them. In a society that offered a woman marriage and the household as her whole horizon, love of God became a door out of it. The Tamil **Andal** refused every earthly husband, declaring herself the bride of Vishnu alone. The Kannada **Akka Mahadevi** walked out of her marriage and wandered clothed only in her own long hair, holding Shiva her only lord. **Lal Ded** of Kashmir left her home and roamed unclothed, singing her *vakhs*. The Rajput princess **Mirabai** abandoned her royal husband's palace to dance and sing for Krishna, enduring exile and, in legend, poison sent by her scandalized in-laws. To choose God as husband or lover was to slip the authority of father and husband and caste at once — which is why the women saints were watched, doubted, and punished more harshly than the men, and why their songs still read as some of the boldest claims to a woman's own soul in the pre-modern world.

## The practice of love

Bhakti's devotion took set forms — classically the **nine** (*navadha bhakti*): hearing God's stories, singing them, remembering, serving, worshipping, bowing, serving as a slave, befriending, and utterly surrendering the self. Its warmth came from the **bhavas**, the moods in which one loved God — as **master**, **friend**, **child**, or **lover** — each a different intimacy. Above all it sang. **Kirtan** and **bhajan**, communal devotional singing often set to ecstatic music, carried the movement into every village; and the endless repetition of the divine **name** — *Ram*, *Hari*, *Krishna* — was itself the shortest road to God, needing no learning at all. There was no priest between the devotee and the beloved, and that was the whole point.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Bhakti's genius was to hide nothing and give everything — yet it, too, encoded the sacred in distinctive ways.

- Its central sacred vehicle is the **vernacular song** — the *pada*, *abhang*, *vachana*, and *doha* &mdash; deliberately composed in the mother tongue so that anyone could receive the divine. In doing so the movement became **the single most powerful force in the shaping of the modern Indian languages**: Tulsidas made a scripture of Awadhi, Surdas of Braj, Tukaram of Marathi, Mirabai of Rajasthani, Chaitanya of Bengali, Narsinh Mehta of Gujarati.
- The **divine name** (*nama*) is itself sacred and sufficient: to repeat it (*nama-japa*) is to hold the deity present in sound — the Indian devotional cousin of the Sufi **dhikr**, the tantric **mantra**, the Kabbalistic divine names, and the Shinto **kotodama**, all met in one age.
- **Kabir**'s riddling **ulatbamsi**, "upside-down language" — deliberately paradoxical verses that turn the world inside out to jolt the hearer awake — is his own version of the coded speech that runs through the esoteric traditions of this book.
- And the Lingayats' **ishtalinga** — a small symbol of Shiva worn on the body, worshipped anywhere — encodes the whole radical claim of Bhakti in an object: the divine is carried in the heart and on the person, not housed in a temple that only some may enter.

## Connections

Bhakti grew from the Vedic and epic Hinduism of the earliest eras and from the devotional current already in the **Gita**, and it rhymes with the **Tantra** of the previous chapter as another intensely experiential path. Above all it met, in medieval India, the **Sufism** of this very era: the two love-mysticisms cross-fertilized for centuries, and **Kabir** stands squarely at their meeting point, claimed by Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs alike. It fed directly into the birth of **Sikhism** in the next era — Guru Nanak's hymns and Kabir's verses share a scripture — and, much later, carried Krishna-devotion around the modern world. With Bhakti the High Medieval age closes: five traditions, from the Kabbalah and Sufism of the Abrahamic west to the temple religions of the Americas and the love-religion of India, each in its own way seeking the hidden God through love, order, or blood.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The Bhakti movement and its spread from the Tamil south across India; the poetic corpora of the **Alvars** and **Nayanars**; the philosophers **Ramanuja** and **Madhva**; the saints **Basava**, **Jnaneshwar**, **Namdev**, **Kabir**, and **Ravidas** and their surviving verse; the plain **anti-caste, anti-clerical, vernacular** message of the poetry; and Bhakti's immense role in shaping the modern Indian languages. **Jayadeva**'s *Gita Govinda* (c. 1190) and its erotic Radha-Krishna theology (*madhurya bhava*); and the prominence of **women saints** — Andal, Akka Mahadevi, Lal Ded, Mirabai — and their documented defiance of marriage and caste.

**Not supported (as history) — the lives of the saints.** The **biographies** of most Bhakti saints are woven through with **miracle and legend** — Kabir's body turning to flowers, poison turning to nectar for Mirabai — devotional hagiography, not documented history. Firm **dates** for many saints are uncertain, and the poems attributed to them were largely transmitted orally and compiled later, so that a corpus like "**Kabir**'s" is layered and partly the work of others.

**Genuinely open.** How far the movement's radical **anti-caste** message actually reformed society, as against being spiritually revolutionary while socially absorbed — scholars genuinely disagree. The authorship and dating of many individual poems. And the question the flowers under the shroud pose directly: whether **Kabir** is best understood as a Hindu, a Muslim, or, as he himself insisted, as belonging to no such box at all.
