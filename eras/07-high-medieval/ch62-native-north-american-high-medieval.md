# Chapter 62 — Native North American Religion: The Sacred in All Things

*Recently added — pending full review.*

Around the year 1100, on the floodplain across the river from modern St. Louis, stood a city. At its heart rose **Monks Mound** — a flat-topped earthen pyramid built up over centuries in fourteen stages, its base larger than that of the Great Pyramid of Giza, crowned by a great temple and the house of a paramount chief. This was **Cahokia**, the largest city in North America north of Mexico, home to perhaps fifteen thousand people, aligned to the sun by a great timber circle. It is the most dramatic monument of a religious world that Europeans would later pretend did not exist — the vast, ancient, and staggeringly diverse spiritual life of Native North America.

## The problem of "one" religion

There is no such thing as *the* Native American religion. At the time of European contact, the peoples of North America spoke some **three hundred distinct languages** and lived in hundreds of nations, each with its own beliefs, ceremonies, and sacred stories — from the communal cyclic rituals of the Pueblo to the individual vision-seeking of the Plains, from the potlatch of the Northwest Coast to the mound-cities of the Mississippi. What follows traces *shared threads*, not a single creed, and it does so knowing two things: that these are **living** traditions, practiced today, not relics; and that many are **oral** and place-specific, and hold sacred knowledge that is not ours to tell.

## The sacred power in everything

If there is a common intuition, it is that a **sacred power pervades all things**. English flattened this into the "**Great Spirit**," but the native concepts are subtler. The Lakota **Wakan Tanka** — often translated "Great Spirit" but closer to "**Great Mystery**" or "the Sacred" — is an abstract, omnipresent creative power, never fully personified, the sacredness residing in everything. The Algonquian **Manitou** (Gitche Manitou, "Great Spirit") is a spiritual power that can dwell in a rock, a tree, an animal, a person, or even a dream. The Iroquois and Huron **orenda** is a transmissible spiritual energy present in varying degrees in all things — a successful hunter's orenda overcoming his quarry's. This is an essentially **animist** vision: the world is alive and personed, the line between the human and animal worlds is thin, and animals, plants, mountains, and winds are relatives and powers, not resources.

## Creation and the trickster

Two great patterns of creation recur (both treated in ch46). In the **earth-diver** myth, dominant across the north and east, the world begins as a limitless sea, and a creator sends an animal — muskrat, loon, beaver — diving to bring up a speck of mud from which the earth grows on the back of a great **Turtle**, so that this continent is "**Turtle Island**." In the Iroquois telling, **Sky Woman** falls from the sky-world and is caught on the turtle's back, where the earth is built for her. In the Southwest, the **emergence** myth prevails: the Pueblo and Diné (Navajo) peoples climbed up through a series of lower worlds into this one. And presiding over many creations is the **trickster** — **Coyote** in the West, **Raven** on the Northwest Coast, **Nanabozho** among the Anishinaabe — at once creator, culture-hero, fool, and cosmic disruptor, who shapes the world as much by blunder and appetite as by design, and who teaches by breaking every rule.

## The vision and the healer

Across much of the continent, and especially on the Plains, the deepest religious act was the **vision quest**: a seeker went alone to a high or sacred place and fasted, without food or water, for days and nights, praying for a **vision** and a **guardian spirit** to guide their life. Visions, dreams, and altered states were not marginal but central — the primary channel to the spirit world, and the foundation of most healing. The **medicine person** (often called, imperfectly, a "shaman") was the specialist who could enter those states to heal, to find game, to see the future, and to mediate between the community and the powers.

## The great ceremonies

Native ceremonial life was rich and various. The Plains **Sun Dance** renewed the world and the community through days of dancing, fasting, and — for some dancers — self-sacrifice and piercing, the suffering offered for the good of all. The **Sacred Pipe** (the calumet) was, for the Lakota and many others, the central holy object: given, the Lakota say, by **White Buffalo Calf Woman**, its rising smoke carried prayers between humans and the sacred, and its sharing sealed truth and peace. The **sweat lodge** purified; the Northwest Coast **potlatch** redistributed wealth and fixed status and rights in great gift-giving feasts; the Pueblo peoples danced the **kachinas** (ancestral spirit-beings) in their plaza and prayed in underground **kivas** on a communal, seasonal cycle; and the Southeastern peoples kept the **Green Corn** ceremony of renewal and forgiveness. Over much of the continent lay the shared grammar of the **four directions**, each with its color and power, gathered in the **medicine wheel**.

## The record in earth and stone

North America holds monuments to match its faiths. For millennia its peoples raised **mounds**: the **Adena** and **Hopewell** built vast geometric earthworks and effigy mounds (the quarter-mile **Serpent Mound** of Ohio) for burial and ceremony; the **Mississippian** culture built the pyramid-cities, of which **Cahokia** (c. 950–1350) was the greatest, with its aligned "**Woodhenge**" and the shared religious art historians call the **Southeastern Ceremonial Complex** — the falcon-warrior "**birdman**," the cross-in-circle, the weeping eye. In the Southwest, the **Ancestral Puebloans** raised the great houses of **Chaco Canyon**, precisely aligned to the movements of the sun and moon. (The old racist "Mound Builder myth" — that some vanished non-Indian race must have built these works — is false and long refuted: they were built by the ancestors of today's Native peoples.)

## Catastrophe and renewal

European colonization brought demographic collapse, dispossession, forced conversion, and the banning of ceremonies like the Sun Dance and the potlatch. Out of that crucible came powerful new movements. The **Ghost Dance**, born of the Paiute prophet **Wovoka**'s vision in 1889, promised that faithful dancing would bring back the dead, the buffalo, and a renewed earth free of the invaders — a messianic hope (ch50) that spread across the Plains and ended in the massacre of some 250 Lakota at **Wounded Knee** in 1890. The Iroquois prophet **Handsome Lake** founded the enduring **Longhouse religion** in 1799. And the **Native American Church**, crystallizing around 1885, built a new pan-Indian faith around the sacramental use of **peyote** — the vision-giving cactus used in the Americas for thousands of years — fused with Christian elements, and today the most widespread Indigenous religion in North America, its peyote sacrament now legally protected.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

- **The medicine wheel and the four directions.** The circle divided into four quarters, each with its color, animal, and power, encodes the whole cosmos and the human place within it — a map of the sacred order carried in a single figure.
- **The cross-in-circle and the birdman.** The Mississippian **cross-in-circle** encodes the four directions and the sacred fire/sun at the center; the **birdman** (the falcon-costumed warrior) encodes the union of the sky-power and the human ruler — the iconography of a whole religious civilization stamped on shell and copper.
- **The sacred pipe and the rising smoke.** The **calumet** encodes the covenant between the human and sacred worlds; its smoke, drawn in and breathed upward, is the visible carrier of prayer — the same intuition as incense across the archive.
- **The mound as sacred mountain.** The earthen pyramid encodes the **world-axis** and the raised meeting-place of earth and sky, kin to the Neolithic tumulus (ch42) and the Mesoamerican pyramid (ch29). And the **Turtle** encodes the whole earth borne on a living back — creation resting on a creature.

## Connections

Native North American religion shares the **earth-diver** and **emergence** creation patterns explored in ch46, and its pervasive **sacred power** (Wakan Tanka, Manitou, orenda) is a close cousin of Polynesian **mana** (ch63) and of the animist substrate beneath the **prehistoric** traditions (ch41). Its **mound-pyramids** align it with the sacred mountains of the **Neolithic** (ch42) and the temple-pyramids of the **Aztec, Maya, and Inca** (ch29); its **vision quest** and medicine-work belong with the shamanism debated in ch41; and its **Ghost Dance** is a classic **apocalyptic/millenarian** movement (ch50) born, like so many, from catastrophe (ch35). It stands beside the **Aboriginal** (ch61) and **Oceanic** (ch63) traditions as one of the great living oral religions of the world.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The mound-building cultures and their monuments — Adena, Hopewell, and the Mississippian Cahokia (c. 950–1350) with Monks Mound and Woodhenge; the Ancestral Puebloan great houses of Chaco Canyon and their astronomical alignments — all built by the ancestors of today's Native peoples. The core religious concepts (Wakan Tanka, Manitou, orenda; animism; the earth-diver and emergence creations; the trickster) and the great ceremonies (Sun Dance, sacred pipe, potlatch, kachina, Green Corn, vision quest) are well attested. The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee (1890), the Longhouse religion (Handsome Lake, 1799), and the Native American Church (peyotism) are documented history.

**Not supported (as established fact) / to be rejected.** There is **no single** Native American religion, and "**Great Spirit**" is a simplifying colonial gloss. The racist **"Mound Builder myth"** — that a lost non-Native race built the earthworks — is **false** and rejected here. Commercial and New Age **appropriations** ("plastic medicine men," sold sweat-lodge and vision-quest "experiences") are distortions, not the traditions. Because so much pre-contact belief is reconstructed from *post*-contact ethnography, some accounts carry the distortions of their outside recorders.

**Genuinely open — and rightly restricted.** The specific religious meanings of much ancient art (the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, Chaco's alignments) are debated; and, as with the Aboriginal traditions, much sacred knowledge is **restricted** by its communities and is not published here. The archive reports the well-documented monuments, movements, and public teachings, refuses the appropriations and the racist myth, and honors what is not ours to tell.
