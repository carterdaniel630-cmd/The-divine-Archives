# Chapter 12 — Confucianism & Daoism: The Way and the Rites

*Tradition chapter · Era 04 — Axial Age · **DRAFT — READY FOR REVIEW***

---

## Two answers to a broken world

Picture the same century from two windows in the same house. Through the first,
a middle-aged man in a small feudal state, out of office and out of favor, is
teaching a handful of students that if you want to mend a world tearing itself
apart, you begin by getting the small things exactly right — how you bow, how you
mourn your parents, how a ruler addresses a minister and a son a father. Order,
he insists, is not imposed from above; it is *cultivated*, one honest person and
one correct ceremony at a time. Through the second window, another voice — half
legend, we shall see — laughs at the whole project. Your rules and your rituals,
it says, are the *symptom* of the sickness, not the cure. The more laws you carve,
the more thieves you make. Stop striving. Stop carving. Return to the uncut block,
to the valley, to the water that conquers stone precisely because it never fights.
Do nothing — *wu-wei* — and nothing is left undone.

These are the two great native traditions of China, born in the same age of
collapse — the **Spring and Autumn** and **Warring States** periods, when the Zhou
order of Chapter 9 had shattered into warring statelets — and they have argued and
embraced ever since. **Confucianism** is the tradition of the man in the first
window, **Kongzi**, "Master Kong," whom the West calls **Confucius**.
**Daoism** is the tradition that gathered around the second voice, the *Daodejing*
and its supposed author **Laozi**, "the Old Master," and the wild philosopher
**Zhuangzi**. The Chinese themselves came to say the two, with Buddhism, were the
**three teachings** (*sanjiao*) — not three churches competing for a single soul,
but three registers a single person might move between: Confucian in the office,
Daoist in the garden, Buddhist before death. This chapter is about the first two,
in their formative age, before Buddhism arrived and before either had hardened into
what came later.

## The world that made them: the Warring States

Neither tradition can be understood apart from the catastrophe that produced them.
The **Zhou dynasty** (Chapter 9) had ruled by the **Mandate of Heaven**, its
nobles bound in a ritual order of ancestor-worship and reciprocal duty. By the
sixth century BCE that order was a memory. The Zhou king was a figurehead; real
power lay with contending states that annexed and swallowed one another through
centuries of escalating war — the aptly named **Warring States** period (c. 475–221
BCE). Into that chaos poured the **Hundred Schools of Thought**, a ferment of rival
teachers each with a prescription for restoring order: the Confucians, the Daoists,
the **Mohists** (who preached impartial universal love and were Confucianism's
fiercest early rivals), the **Legalists** (who trusted only harsh law and state
power, and who would in fact unify China by force), the strategists, the logicians,
and more. The Axial Age in China is this argument — and Confucianism and Daoism are
the two answers that outlived all the others.

## The great lost school: Mohism

One rival deserves rescue from the footnotes, because for two centuries it was
Confucianism's most formidable opponent and because it points a road Chinese thought
did not take. **Mohism**, founded by **Mozi** ("Master Mo," c. 470–391 BCE) and
carried by a disciplined, almost military brotherhood, judged every practice by one
plain test: does it benefit the people? This makes the Mohists the ancient world's
first systematic **consequentialists**. From that test they drew a startling ethic.
Against the Confucian insistence on graded love — more for one's own father than for a
stranger — Mozi preached **jian'ai**, "impartial care": concern for everyone equally,
since partiality is the root of conflict. They condemned offensive war (**feigong**)
while becoming experts in **defensive** fortification, marching physically to the aid
of besieged states; and they attacked the Confucians' lavish funerals and long
mourning as ruinous waste, and fatalism as a creed that excuses the idle and the
cruel.

And where Confucius kept a careful agnostic silence about Heaven and the spirits,
Mohism was frankly **theistic**: it taught the **Will of Heaven** (*Tianzhi*) as a
personal, moral power that loves all people impartially and rewards or punishes their
conduct, and it insisted on the reality of **ghosts and spirits** as agents of that
justice — partly, quite explicitly, because a watching, rewarding Heaven gives
ordinary people a reason to be good. It is the closest the classical Chinese schools
came to the providential God of the western traditions. Mohism flourished, then
vanished almost entirely after the Qin unification, its texts nearly lost — but its
impartial benevolence, its consequentialism, and its theism make it the great road
not taken of Chinese philosophy, and a reminder that the Hundred Schools held
possibilities the victorious traditions did not.

## Confucius: the man and the Analects

**Kongzi** (traditionally **551–479 BCE**) was, by the tradition's own account, a
failure in his own time — a scholar of modest birth in the state of **Lu** (in
modern Shandong) who sought high office to put his ideas into practice, wandered
from court to court, and never truly got the chance. He taught instead. His
influence is entirely posthumous, and colossal: for two thousand years he was the
teacher of a civilization, his thought the official ideology of the Chinese state.

We know his teaching mainly from the **Analects** (*Lunyu*, "Selected Sayings") — a
collection of terse aphorisms, dialogues, and anecdotes, opening "The Master said…"
Here an educational caution matters, and the newest evidence sharpens it. The
*Analects* is **not** a book Confucius wrote; it is a compilation by his disciples
and their disciples, assembled over generations. Modern scholarship treats it as a
**layered text**, its historical kernel of Confucius's own sayings interwoven with
later doctrinal growth, and the whole probably not stabilized until the **Warring
States** period or after. Recently excavated bamboo manuscripts — the Anhui
University *Zhongni yue* ("Confucius said") and the Wangjiazui *Kongzi yue* — confirm
that by around **300 BCE** collections of sayings attributed to Confucius were
circulating in variant forms, which is exactly what a layered, gradually compiled
text should look like. We can recover *Confucianism* with confidence; recovering the
*ipsissima verba* of Confucius is harder.

## The teaching: virtue, ritual, and the cultivated person

Confucius offered no cosmology to speak of and little about gods. Asked about
serving the spirits, he famously replied that one who cannot yet serve the living
should not ask about serving the dead; asked about death, he said one who does not
yet understand life should not ask about death. His concern was **this world** and
how to live rightly in it. His answer turned on a small cluster of interlocking
ideas:

- **Ren** (仁) — "humaneness," benevolence, the empathy one person owes another.
  It is the master virtue, the inner disposition from which right action flows;
  its negative formulation is the first recorded **Golden Rule**: *"Do not impose
  on others what you yourself do not desire."*
- **Li** (禮) — "ritual," "rites," "propriety." Far more than ceremony, *li* is the
  entire fabric of correct, considerate, patterned behavior — from state sacrifices
  down to how you greet a guest or pour the wine. Confucius's deep claim is that
  the outer form and the inner virtue *make each other*: performed sincerely, ritual
  cultivates the humaneness it expresses. Society is held together not by law and
  punishment but by shared, internalized *li*.
- **Xiao** (孝) — "filial piety," reverence for parents and ancestors, the root from
  which all other virtue grows. The well-ordered family is the model and seed of the
  well-ordered state.
- **Junzi** (君子) — the "gentleman" or "exemplary person." Originally the word meant
  an aristocrat by *birth*; Confucius performed a quiet revolution by redefining it
  as nobility by *character*. Anyone, in principle, could become a *junzi* through
  self-cultivation, study, and the practice of virtue. The *junzi* is the opposite
  of the "small person" (*xiaoren*) who thinks only of profit.
- **The rectification of names** (*zhengming*) — social harmony requires that
  everyone act according to the reality their name names: "Let the ruler be a ruler,
  the minister a minister, the father a father, the son a son." Disorder begins when
  names and realities part ways.

The political vision that follows is government by **moral example** rather than
force. A ruler who is himself virtuous draws the people as the wind bends the grass;
one who governs by punishment gets only evasion. This was Confucius's direct answer
to the Legalists, and the two visions would contend for the soul of Chinese
governance for millennia.

## The two who completed it: Mencius and Xunzi

Confucianism became a system through two great successors who agreed on the goal and
split, profoundly, on human nature — a debate as sharp as any in the history of
ethics.

**Mencius** (**Mengzi**, c. 372–289 BCE) taught that human nature is **innately
good**. Every person is born with the "four sprouts" of virtue — the spontaneous
pang of alarm at seeing a child about to fall into a well proves that compassion is
native to us. Cultivation is therefore *gardening*: nurturing what is already there.
Mencius also gave the **Mandate of Heaven** its sharpest political edge, arguing that
a ruler who becomes a tyrant forfeits the Mandate and may **justly be overthrown** —
a doctrine of righteous rebellion embedded in the heart of the tradition.

**Xunzi** (c. 298–238 BCE) taught the reverse: human nature is **bad**, or at least
crooked and self-seeking, and goodness is an *achievement*, laboriously produced by
ritual, education, and effort — a warped board steamed straight. For Xunzi, **Heaven**
(*Tian*) is not a moral agent watching over us but simply **nature**, impersonal and
regular; the moral order is entirely a human construction, and *li* is the
indispensable technology that constructs it. Xunzi's naturalism and his stress on
strict ritual discipline made him, ironically, the teacher of two of the great
**Legalists** — and a figure later "orthodox" Confucianism kept at arm's length,
even as it quietly absorbed much of his thought.

## Laozi and the Daodejing: the Way that cannot be named

Now the other window. The founding text of Daoism is the **Daodejing** ("Classic of
the Way and its Power"), some five thousand characters of gnomic, paradoxical
poetry, traditionally ascribed to **Laozi**, "the Old Master," said to have been an
elder contemporary of Confucius who, weary of the world, rode west on an ox and, at
the frontier pass, wrote the book at a gatekeeper's request before vanishing.

Almost every part of that story is doubted by modern scholarship, and here the
archaeology has been decisive. The oldest known copies — the **Guodian** bamboo
slips (buried c. 300 BCE, containing about 40% of the received text in variant
clusters) and the two **Mawangdui** silk manuscripts (c. 200 BCE) — show that the
*Daodejing* was **not** the seamless work of a single sixth-century sage but a text
that **accreted from circulating clusters of sayings**, reaching something like its
final form only in the late Warring States. Most specialists now date its
characteristic ideas to the **fourth–third centuries BCE**, and many hold that
"Laozi" may have been less a person than a *tradition* — "the Old Master's teachings"
— onto which a founder-legend was later projected. Whether one or more real teachers
stand behind it remains genuinely unresolved.

Its teaching is the deliberate inversion of the Confucian project:

- **The Dao** (道) — "the Way." The nameless, formless source and pattern of all
  things, prior to Heaven and Earth, empty yet inexhaustible. "The Dao that can be
  spoken is not the eternal Dao" — it can be pointed at but never captured, and the
  book's paradoxes are a discipline for loosening the mind's grip on words.
- **De** (德) — "power" or "virtue," but in the sense of the potency a thing has when
  it acts in accord with the Dao — not moral virtue *acquired*, but the natural
  efficacy of things left to their own nature.
- **Wu-wei** (無為) — "non-action," better rendered *effortless action* or
  non-interference: acting in frictionless accord with the grain of things rather
  than forcing them. Water is the great image — soft, yielding, seeking the low
  place, and yet wearing away the hardest stone.
- **Ziran** (自然) — "self-so," spontaneity, the naturalness of things when nothing
  is imposed on them. The sage does not carve and regulate; he returns to the
  **uncarved block** (*pu*), the state of simplicity before the knife of
  distinctions and rules cut nature into pieces.

Where Confucius would *cultivate*, Laozi would *let be*; where Confucius multiplied
rites, Laozi warned that ritual is "the husk of loyalty and faith, and the beginning
of disorder." The best ruler, the *Daodejing* says, is the one the people barely know
is there, and the ideal is a small, contented society that has un-learned its
cleverness.

## Zhuangzi: the laughing philosopher

If the *Daodejing* is Daoism's scripture, the **Zhuangzi** is its wild, brilliant,
literary heart. Its author, **Master Zhuang** (Zhuang Zhou, late fourth century BCE),
is a far more securely historical figure than Laozi, though scholars generally credit
only the **Inner Chapters** (the first seven) to him, with the Outer and Miscellaneous
chapters added by later hands and the whole edited into its received form only around
the fourth century CE by the commentator **Guo Xiang**.

Zhuangzi takes the Dao and turns it into freedom, humor, and vertigo. He dreams he is
a butterfly and wakes unsure whether he is Zhuang who dreamt he was a butterfly or a
butterfly now dreaming it is Zhuang — a parable of the relativity of all our fixed
viewpoints. He mocks the solemn Confucians, converses with skulls, and celebrates the
"useless" tree that grows huge and old precisely *because* no carpenter wants it. His
recurring theme is the **relativity of perspectives**: big and small, useful and
useless, life and death, right and wrong are human distinctions, not features of the
Dao, and the sage who sees this moves through the world "free and easy," unresisting,
even greeting his own death with equanimity as one more of the Dao's transformations.

## The great divide: two Daoisms

Here is one of the most important and most misunderstood distinctions in the study of
religion, and the project's depth standard demands it be drawn clearly. What is
called "Daoism" is really **two related but distinct things**:

- **Philosophical Daoism** (*daojia*) — the tradition of ideas in the *Daodejing* and
  the *Zhuangzi*: the Dao, *wu-wei*, *ziran*, a contemplative naturalism. This is what
  most Westerners mean by "Taoism."
- **Religious Daoism** (*daojiao*) — an organized religion of gods, priests, liturgy,
  scriptures, temples, alchemy, exorcism, and the quest for immortality, which
  emerged centuries later. Its conventional starting point is **142 CE**, when,
  tradition holds, the deified **Laozi** descended to **Zhang Daoling** on Mount
  Heming and established a covenant, founding the **Way of the Celestial Masters**
  (*Tianshi dao*) — the first organized Daoist church, complete with a priestly
  hierarchy, communal confession and healing, and a parish structure. Religious
  Daoism absorbed the old **immortality** quests (*xian*, the transcendent
  immortals), **internal and external alchemy**, breath and body cultivation, and a
  vast pantheon; it produced its own enormous canon, the **Daozang**.

The two are historically continuous — religious Daoism reveres the *Daodejing* as
scripture and read it, in the *Xiang'er* commentary, as a manual for immortality —
but they are not the same, and collapsing them is a common error. The Warring States
philosophers were not priests; the Celestial Masters were not merely reading poetry.

## Ritual, folk religion, and the state cult

Beneath and around both elite traditions ran the older, continuous **folk religion**
of China — the substrate of Chapter 9 that never went away. Most people, most of the
time, venerated their **ancestors**, made offerings at family altars, feared and
fed hungry ghosts, propitiated local gods of soil and grain, of the stove and the
city wall, and consulted diviners and mediums. Confucianism did not replace this; it
*ritualized and dignified* it, making ancestor-reverence and mourning into moral
cornerstones. Daoism, in its religious form, would later organize and systematize the
world of gods and spirits into a bureaucratic pantheon mirroring the imperial state.

Confucianism also became, over centuries, the **state cult**. Confucius himself was
posthumously venerated with sacrifices; temples to him rose across the empire; and
the study of the Confucian **classics** became the basis of the **imperial
examinations** that selected officials — arguably the most consequential institution
in Chinese history, binding the state to the tradition for two thousand years. What
began as one out-of-work teacher's counsel became the operating system of a
civilization.

## The texts: the Classics and the canon

Confucianism organized itself around the **Five Classics** (*Wujing*), revered as the
inheritance of antiquity that Confucius was said to have edited and transmitted (not
authored): the **Book of Documents** (*Shujing*), the **Book of Odes/Poetry**
(*Shijing*), the **Book of Rites** (*Liji*), the **Book of Changes** (*Yijing*), and
the **Spring and Autumn Annals** (*Chunqiu*). Later the **Four Books** — the
*Analects*, the *Mencius*, and two chapters lifted from the *Book of Rites*, the
*Great Learning* and the *Doctrine of the Mean* — became the core curriculum. Daoism's
foundational pair, the *Daodejing* and *Zhuangzi*, were later joined by the sprawling
*Daozang*, the Daoist canon, running to well over a thousand texts.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The Yijing: reality written in broken and unbroken lines.** No system better shows
the Chinese conviction that the cosmos is *legible* than the **Yijing**, the "Book of
Changes" — at once a Confucian classic and a divination manual. Its atoms are two
kinds of line: an unbroken line for **yang** (the active, "male," bright principle)
and a broken line for **yin** (the receptive, "female," dark principle). Stacked
three deep, they yield the eight **trigrams** (*bagua*) — Heaven, Earth, Thunder,
Water, Mountain, Wind, Fire, Lake — and stacked six deep, the **sixty-four
hexagrams**, a complete symbolic map of every situation and its tendency to change.
To divine, one casts lots (traditionally yarrow stalks, later coins) and builds a
hexagram line by line from the bottom, then reads its judgment. The genius of the
system is that it encodes the deepest Chinese metaphysical intuition — that reality
is not static substance but ceaseless **transformation**, the interplay of yin and
yang — into a binary notation so elegant that Leibniz, seeing it, recognized his own
binary arithmetic. *An honesty note:* the traditional ascription of the trigrams to
the mythic sage-king **Fuxi** and the "**Ten Wings**" commentaries to Confucius
himself is legend; the commentaries were most likely written during the **Han
dynasty**, after Confucius, and the text's core is older Zhou divination material.

**Yin-yang and the five phases.** The **taijitu** — the familiar swirl of dark and
light, each half carrying a seed of the other — is a late graphic emblem of an ancient
idea: that the two principles are not opposed armies but complementary phases of one
process, each becoming the other, neither ever total. Alongside it runs the theory of
the **Five Phases** (*wuxing* — wood, fire, earth, metal, water), a grammar of cyclical
generation and conquest used to encode correspondences among the seasons, directions,
colors, organs, and virtues — a whole cosmology of resonance.

**Talismans and the sacred graph.** In religious Daoism the written character itself
became a vehicle of power: **fu** talismans — esoteric, quasi-writing glyphs drawn by
priests — were believed to command spirits, heal, and protect, a direct heir to the
ancient Chinese sense (Chapter 9) that writing is itself a sacred, efficacious act.

**The uncarved block and the empty vessel.** Daoism encodes its philosophy in a
vocabulary of humble physical images that are themselves the teaching: the **uncarved
block** (simplicity before artifice), the **valley** and the **female** (the power of
the low and yielding), the **empty vessel** and the hub's **empty center** (usefulness
residing in emptiness), and **water** (the soft that overcomes the hard). To meditate
on the image *is* to grasp the doctrine — encoding of a subtler kind than any script.

## Connections

Confucianism and Daoism grow directly out of **Chapter 9** — the Zhou world, the
Mandate of Heaven, ancestor-reverence, and oracle-bone divination are their shared
inheritance, and the *Yijing* is the bridge. Across the Axial Age they belong beside
the era's other great awakenings: the **Buddha** (Chapter 11) and the **Upanishadic
sages** (Chapter 5) turning inward in India, the **Greek philosophers** (Chapter 8)
reasoning their way toward first principles, and the **prophets of Israel** (Chapters
7, 10) — all part of what Karl Jaspers named the **Axial Age**, the near-simultaneous
turn across Eurasia toward ethics, reflection, and transcendence. The comparison with
**Greece** is especially rich: like the Greeks, the Chinese produced rival schools
arguing in public over the good life and the good state — but where Greek thought
prized abstract definition and formal logic, Chinese thought prized concrete example,
harmony, and the cultivation of conduct. Forward, both traditions have vast futures in
this archive: the arrival of **Buddhism** in China and the three-way conversation of
the **three teachings**; the flowering of **religious Daoism** and its alchemy; and
the great medieval **Neo-Confucian** synthesis, all of which belong to later chapters.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- A historical teacher, **Kongzi/Confucius** (trad. 551–479 BCE), taught in the state
  of Lu during the collapse of the Zhou order, and a tradition of collecting his
  sayings existed by c. 300 BCE (confirmed by recently excavated bamboo manuscripts).
- The core Confucian concepts — **ren, li, xiao, junzi**, government by moral example,
  and the elaborations of **Mencius** (innate goodness; the right of rebellion) and
  **Xunzi** (nature as crooked; Heaven as impersonal nature) — are well attested in the
  Warring States texts.
- The **Daodejing** existed in partial, clustered form by c. 300 BCE (**Guodian**
  slips) and near-complete form by c. 200 BCE (**Mawangdui**); the **Zhuangzi**'s Inner
  Chapters derive from a real fourth-century-BCE thinker.
- **Mohism**, founded by **Mozi** (c. 470–391 BCE), was a major Warring States school:
  consequentialist ethics of collective benefit, **jian'ai** (impartial care),
  condemnation of aggression with expert defensive warfare, frugality against lavish
  funerals, and a frankly **theistic** doctrine of Heaven's Will (*Tianzhi*) and
  moral-enforcing spirits — before it vanished after the Qin unification.
- The later emergence of **organized religious Daoism** with the **Celestial Masters**
  (traditionally 142 CE) as an institution distinct from the earlier philosophical
  texts.
- The **Yijing** as a genuine Zhou-era divination system built on yin/yang lines,
  trigrams, and hexagrams.

**What's not supported by the evidence**

- That Confucius **wrote** the Analects (it is a later, layered compilation) or
  personally **authored** the Five Classics (he was, at most, a transmitter/editor in
  tradition; the ascriptions are traditional).
- The **Laozi legend** as literal biography — the elder contemporary of Confucius, the
  ox, the frontier pass, the single-author composition. "Laozi" may be a tradition
  rather than a man.
- That Confucius wrote the **"Ten Wings"** of the Yijing, or that **Fuxi** devised the
  trigrams (both traditional attributions, not history).
- Conflating **philosophical Daoism** (*daojia*) with **religious Daoism** (*daojiao*)
  — a widespread but real error.

**What's genuinely open**

- Whether a single historical **Laozi** existed at all, and the precise process by
  which the *Daodejing* reached its final form.
- How much of the **Analects** preserves Confucius's own words versus his school's
  later development.
- The exact authorship and dating of the outer layers of the **Zhuangzi**.
- The long-debated relationship and mutual influence between the schools during the
  Warring States, and how much of "Daoism" is a retrospective category imposed by
  later (especially Han) classifiers.
