# Chapter 52 — Jainism: The Religion of Harmlessness

*Recently added — pending full review.*

A monk walks a road in India wearing nothing at all, sweeping the ground ahead of him with a soft broom so as not to crush an ant, breathing through his awareness of the countless invisible lives in the air and the water. He owns nothing, not even a robe. He is a Jain, and he belongs to a tradition that took a single idea — *do no harm to any living thing* — further than any other religion on earth, and built an entire cosmology, ethics, and science of the soul around it.

## The ford-makers

Jainism honors twenty-four **Tīrthaṅkaras** — the word means "ford-makers" or "crossing-makers," those who have found the ford across the river of rebirth and shown others the way. The twenty-fourth and last of the present age was **Mahāvīra** ("Great Hero"), by tradition born around 599 and dying around 527 BCE — a contemporary, near-neighbor, and rival of the Buddha, born like him a prince who renounced everything to seek liberation. Jains do not regard Mahavira as a founder but as a *reviver*: the twenty-third Tirthankara, **Pārśvanātha**, who taught some two and a half centuries earlier, is regarded by many historians as a genuine historical figure, which would make Jainism's roots among the oldest of any living tradition. Mahavira and the Buddha both belonged to the great **śramaṇa** ("striver") movement — the wandering renunciants of the Ganges plain who, in the axial age, broke with the Vedic sacrificial religion of the brahmins (ch05) and sought release not through ritual but through discipline and insight.

## No creator, and a universe full of souls

Jainism is, strikingly, a religion with **no creator god**. The universe is eternal and uncreated, running by its own laws through endless cycles of ascending and descending ages, and shaped — in Jain cosmology — like a vast cosmic *man*. What fills it is not one God but countless **jīvas**, souls, and **ajīva**, non-soul (matter, space, time, motion). And souls are *everywhere*: not only in humans and animals but in plants, in tiny beings in earth, water, fire, and air, and in the *nigodas*, clusters of the most rudimentary life packed into every corner of the cosmos. Every one of these souls is, in its true nature, infinitely knowing and blissful — and every one is trapped, weighed down into matter and rebirth by **karma**.

Jain karma is not, as elsewhere, an abstract moral bookkeeping: it is a subtle **physical substance**, a fine dust that clings to the soul through our passions and actions and drags it down. Liberation (*mokṣa*) is the burning away of all accumulated karma and the stopping of any new karma from binding — after which the freed, weightless soul (*siddha*) rises to the very top of the universe and dwells there forever in omniscient bliss. This physical picture of karma is why Jain practice is so austere: the goal is to *starve* the influx of karma through non-action, non-attachment, and above all non-harm.

## Ahimsa, and the many-sidedness of truth

The heart of Jain ethics is five vows — **ahiṃsā** (non-violence), **satya** (truth), **asteya** (not stealing), **brahmacarya** (chastity), and **aparigraha** (non-attachment/non-possession) — kept absolutely by monks and nuns (the *mahāvratas*, "great vows") and in lesser form by laypeople (*aṇuvratas*). Of these, **ahimsa** is supreme, and Jains have pursued it to lengths unmatched anywhere: strict vegetarianism (and avoidance even of root vegetables, whose harvesting kills the whole plant and the tiny lives around it); the monk's broom and mouth-cloth; the filtering of water; the exclusion of occupations that involve killing. It was this tradition, transmitted through his Gujarati upbringing, that decisively shaped **Mahatma Gandhi**'s ahimsa and his politics of nonviolence.

Jain thought matched this ethical humility with an epistemological one. **Anekāntavāda**, "many-sidedness," holds that reality is complex and that any single viewpoint grasps only part of the truth — famously illustrated by the parable of the **blind men and the elephant**, each certain, from the part he touches, that the whole is a wall, a rope, or a fan. Its companion doctrine, **syādvāda**, qualifies every assertion with *syāt*, "in some respect / maybe," yielding a seven-fold logic of conditional truth. In an age of dogma, Jainism built non-absolutism into its very theory of knowledge — a kind of intellectual ahimsa.

## The sky-clad and the white-clad

Around the beginning of the common era Jainism divided into two great sects, still distinct today. The **Digambara** ("sky-clad") hold that a fully advanced monk renounces even clothing, going naked as the ultimate non-possession; they also hold that the original scriptural canon was lost, and that a woman must first be reborn as a man to attain liberation. The **Śvetāmbara** ("white-clad") monks and nuns wear simple white robes, preserve a written **canon** they trace to Mahavira's teaching, and hold that women can be liberated as they are (one Svetambara tradition even counts the nineteenth Tirthankara, Māllī, as a woman). Both sects share the philosophical core and the great summary text, the **Tattvārtha Sūtra**.

## Scripture, worship, and the fast unto death

The Jain scriptures are the **Āgamas**, teachings ascribed to Mahavira, compiled by his chief disciples (*gaṇadharas*) and long transmitted orally in the **Ardhamāgadhī Prākrit** language before being written down — the anxiety over that oral transmission is part of what split the sects. Jain worship is distinctive precisely because there is no God to petition: in temple **pūjā** the images of the Tirthankaras are venerated not as gods who answer prayers but as perfected *examples*, liberated beings who are beyond the reach of appeal but who show the way. Daily practice centers on **sāmāyika** (a period of meditative equanimity, in which a layperson briefly "assumes ascetic status"), **pratikramaṇa** (ritual repentance), fasting, and the recitation of the **Namokāra Mantra**, which bows not to any deity but to the five worthy classes of soul (the arhats, the siddhas, and the spiritual teachers).

Most striking of all is **sallekhanā** (also *santhāra*): the vow, taken at the very end of life in the face of unavoidable death, to fast gradually and voluntarily to death, thinning away the body and the passions in a state of serene detachment. Jains sharply distinguish it from suicide, which is driven by passion; sallekhana is meant to be the calm, willed completion of a life of non-attachment. It is rare today, and legally fraught — an Indian court declared it illegal in 2015 before the ruling was stayed — but it remains, for Jains, the ideal death, and by tradition the emperor **Chandragupta Maurya** himself ended his life this way as a Jain ascetic.

## The record on the ground

For a community that has always been small, Jainism has left an outsized mark. Its antiquity is attested from the Mauryan period onward; it drew royal patronage across Indian history; and it built some of India's most extraordinary sacred art: the colossal monolithic statue of **Bāhubali (Gommaṭeśvara)** at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, fifty-seven feet tall, carved from a single rock around 981 CE and still ritually bathed in a great festival every twelve years; the exquisitely carved marble **Dilwara temples** at Mount Abu; and the rock-cut Jain caves at Ellora and elsewhere. Jain merchants and scholars, bound by their ethics into trade and learning rather than farming or war, became major patrons of libraries, manuscripts, and mathematics.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Jainism is unusually explicit about its symbols, having adopted an official emblem in 1975 that gathers its whole worldview into one image.

- **The Jain emblem.** Its outline is the shape of the **loka**, the cosmos as a standing figure; at its heart is a raised **open hand** inscribed with the word *ahiṃsā* — the universe itself commanded to stop and do no harm; below the hand a **wheel** (dharmachakra) turns. Above sit a **swastika**, three **dots**, and a crescent with a dot.
- **The swastika**, ancient and auspicious in Indian religion, here encodes the **four states of rebirth** (heavenly beings, humans, animals/plants, and hell-beings) — the wheel of transmigration one must escape.
- **The three dots** above it are the **Three Jewels** of the path: Right Faith, Right Knowledge, and Right Conduct. The **crescent and dot** at the very top encode the abode of the liberated **siddhas** at the summit of the universe — the goal.
- **Tirthankara iconography.** The twenty-four are shown in profound stillness, either seated in meditation or standing in the *kāyotsarga* ("body-abandonment") posture, so motionless that vines grow up their legs; each is identified by a small emblem (a bull for the first Tirthankara, a lion, a lotus) and by the auspicious *śrīvatsa* mark on the chest. Their nudity or serenity encodes total non-attachment: a being who wants, and fears, nothing.

## Connections

Jainism is one of the twin fruits of the axial-age **śramaṇa** revolution, and its closest sibling is **Buddhism** (ch11): both reject the creator-god and the Vedic sacrifice of **early Vedic** religion (ch05), both teach karma, rebirth, and liberation, and both arose in the same time and place — yet Jainism kept the reality of the soul that Buddhism denied, and pushed asceticism and non-harm further than the Buddha's Middle Way allowed. Its radical **ahimsa** is the sharpest instance of the turn away from blood-**sacrifice** (ch51), and it fed directly into the modern world through **Gandhi**. Its many-sidedness stands as one of the ancient world's boldest answers to the problem of competing truths, echoed in the pluralism of later **Bhakti** and modern thought (ch30).

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** That Jainism is an ancient, continuous Indian tradition of the śramaṇa movement, contemporary with early Buddhism; the historicity of Mahavira as a real 6th–5th century BCE teacher; the doctrines (no creator, jiva/ajiva, physical karma, the five vows, anekantavada); the Digambara/Svetambara split; the practices of ahimsa, samayika, and sallekhana; and the archaeological and artistic record (the Gommateshwara colossus of 981 CE, the Dilwara temples, Mauryan-era Jain presence). Its formative influence on Gandhi's ahimsa is well documented.

**Not supported (as established fact).** The traditional dates and full biographies of Mahavira and especially the earlier Tirthankaras are religious tradition, not established history — Parshvanatha (the 23rd) is *plausibly* historical, but the first twenty-two are legendary, set in vast mythical time-spans. The traditional claim that Chandragupta Maurya died by sallekhana is a later Jain tradition, not securely documented. The precise date of the sect split and of the loss (or not) of the original canon is debated.

**Genuinely open.** The exact relationship and mutual influence between the earliest Jain and Buddhist communities; how much of the Agama canon preserves Mahavira's actual words after centuries of oral transmission; and the deep prehistory of the tradition before Parshvanatha. The archive reports the doctrines and practices as they are held and lived, and marks the legendary Tirthankara-lineage as sacred history rather than documented fact.
