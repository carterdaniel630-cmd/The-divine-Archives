# Chapter 10 — Second Temple Judaism: The Many Judaisms

*Tradition chapter · Era 04 — Axial Age · **DRAFT — READY FOR REVIEW***

---

## Jars in a cliff

In 1947 a Bedouin shepherd, tossing stones into a cave in the cliffs above the
Dead Sea at a place called **Qumran**, heard pottery shatter. Inside were tall
clay jars, and inside the jars were scrolls — leather and papyrus, wrapped in
linen, that had lain in the dry dark for nearly two thousand years. Over the next
decade, eleven caves gave up the remains of some **900 manuscripts**: the oldest
copies of the Hebrew Bible by a thousand years, and, alongside them, the private
library of a Jewish community that no one had known existed — with its own rule
book, its own calendar, its own hymns, and its own conviction that it alone were
the true "sons of light" awaiting God's final war against the "sons of darkness."

The **Dead Sea Scrolls** did more than push our biblical manuscripts back a
millennium. They demolished a comfortable assumption. Before them, the Judaism of
this age was often imagined as a single, settled, law-bound religion — the tidy
backdrop to the New Testament. The scrolls revealed something far richer and
stranger: a Judaism of astonishing **diversity**, a ferment of rival sects,
competing scriptures, and clashing visions of God's plan. Scholars now speak, in
the plural, of the **Judaisms** of the Second Temple period. This chapter is about
that ferment — the roughly six centuries (about 516 BCE to 70 CE) between the
building of the Second Temple and its destruction, in which the religion of
pre-exilic Israel (Chapter 7) was transformed into something recognizably the
ancestor of Judaism, Christianity, and, later, Islam.

## The exile that made a religion

The transformation began with catastrophe. In **586 BCE** the Babylonians burned
Jerusalem, destroyed Solomon's Temple, and carried the Judean elite into exile —
the event we watched approaching at the end of Chapter 7. For a people whose whole
theology was bound to a land, a temple, and a king, this should have been the end:
in the ancient logic, a defeated nation's god had been defeated too.

Instead, the exile became the forge of a new faith, and the response was radical.
Rather than concede that Babylon's gods had won, the exilic prophets drew the
opposite, breathtaking conclusion: Yahweh had **used** Babylon to punish his own
people, and — pushing monolatry across the final line into **monotheism** — the
prophet scholars call **Second Isaiah** declared that the other gods are simply
*nothing at all*, idols of wood and stone, and that Yahweh alone is God of the
whole earth. The **arrow reversed** in Chapter 7 reaches its point here: this is
where Israel's long road from many gods to one arrives at outright monotheism.

The exile forced two other innovations that would outlast every temple. First,
worship began to detach from sacrifice: with no temple, the exiles turned to
**prayer, study, and the reading of sacred texts** — the seed of the **synagogue**,
a portable, non-sacrificial way of being religious that could travel anywhere.
Second, the sacred **texts themselves moved to the center**: the Torah was edited
and compiled into something close to its final form, and a people began to define
itself not by a place but by a **book**. When Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered
Babylon and, in **538 BCE**, permitted the exiles to return and rebuild (the
**Second Temple** was finished around 516 BCE), they came back changed — the
inventors, in effect, of a religion that could survive the loss of everything
external.

## An age of empires: the historical arc

Second Temple Judaism was lived under a succession of foreign empires, and its
religion cannot be understood apart from that pressure:

- **Persian period** (539–332 BCE): two centuries under the Zoroastrian-ruled
  Achaemenid empire — the crucial window for the influence question below. Ezra
  and Nehemiah reestablish Torah as the community's constitution.
- **Hellenistic period** (332–63 BCE): **Alexander** conquers the East, and Greek
  language and culture flood in. Under the Seleucid king **Antiochus IV**, an
  attempt to suppress Judaism and desecrate the Temple sparked the **Maccabean
  Revolt** (from 167 BCE) — the rededication of the Temple is still celebrated as
  **Hanukkah** — and produced the independent **Hasmonean** Jewish state.
- **Roman period** (from 63 BCE): Pompey takes Jerusalem; **Herod the Great**
  rebuilds the Temple on a colossal scale; tensions with Rome build until the
  **Great Revolt** and the **destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE** — the
  event that ends this era and forces the birth of Rabbinic Judaism (a later
  chapter).

The great struggle running through it all is the encounter with **Hellenism**:
how much Greek thought and custom could a Torah-centered people absorb before it
stopped being itself? Every sect below is, in part, an answer to that question.

## New heavens and new fears: the theology of the age

Something remarkable happens to Jewish belief in this period: a whole apparatus of
ideas that is **thin or absent in the older Hebrew Scriptures appears, developed,
in the literature of the Second Temple.** Because this is exactly where the
Zoroastrian question of Chapter 6 comes due, it must be laid out carefully.

- **Satan.** In the oldest texts, *ha-satan* ("the accuser") is a member of God's
  own court, a kind of heavenly prosecutor (as in Job). Across this period he
  hardens into a proper name and a cosmic **adversary** — Belial, Mastema, the
  prince of darkness — the leader of the powers of evil.
- **Angels and demons.** The vague "sons of God" of older texts become a ranked
  hierarchy of named **archangels** (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Uriel) and, in
  works like *1 Enoch*, a mythology of **fallen angels** (the Watchers) whose sin
  loosed evil on the world.
- **Resurrection and judgment.** The grim, shadowy afterlife of ancient Israel
  gives way, for the first time clearly in **Daniel 12:2**, to the **resurrection
  of the dead** — "some to everlasting life, and some to shame" — and a final
  division of the righteous and the wicked, with developing notions of heaven and
  hell (Gehenna).
- **Apocalyptic.** A whole new genre appears — **apocalypse** ("unveiling") — in
  which a seer is shown the hidden shape of history and its imminent, catastrophic
  end, when God will overthrow the evil empires and inaugurate a new age. *Daniel*
  and *1 Enoch* are its monuments, and at Qumran the cosmos is split between
  **light and darkness** in a way that sounds strikingly dualist.

**The Zoroastrian question, honestly.** For roughly two centuries the Jews of
Judea lived inside a Persian empire whose official religion (Chapter 6) already
taught a cosmic struggle of good against evil, a hierarchy of divine beings,
individual judgment, resurrection, and a final renovation of the world — and it is
precisely *in and after* that window that these ideas blossom in Judaism. The
timing and the parallels are real, and many scholars see genuine Zoroastrian
influence, especially on apocalyptic dualism and angelology. But the careful
verdict must be stated: **direct borrowing is unprovable, and the honest word is
"resonance" rather than demonstrable dependence.** The complications are the ones
we met before — the Zoroastrian texts were written down late, the parallels are
partial, and Jewish thinkers were also responding to their own scriptures and to
Hellenistic ideas. So: real, important, widely argued, and not settled. This
project reports the debate; it does not close it.

## Many Judaisms: the sects

The diversity the scrolls revealed was organized, in part, into rival parties. Our
main witness, the historian **Josephus**, describes several "schools," and to them
we must add others:

- **The Pharisees** — a lay movement devoted to the **Torah** and to an **Oral
  Law** they believed accompanied it, extending its holiness into every corner of
  daily life. They believed in resurrection, angels, and fate-and-free-will
  together. Marginal to the Temple establishment, they were closest to the people —
  and, crucially, it is their heirs who, after 70 CE, will become the **rabbis**
  and carry Judaism forward.
- **The Sadducees** — the priestly and aristocratic elite centered on the
  **Temple**. They accepted only the written Torah, rejected the Oral Law, and
  denied the resurrection and the elaborate angelology. When the Temple fell, they
  vanished with it.
- **The Essenes** — a strict, communal, often celibate movement that regarded the
  Jerusalem priesthood as corrupt and withdrew to await God's end. **Most scholars
  associate them with the Qumran community** behind the Dead Sea Scrolls, though
  this identification is debated.
- **The Zealots and Sicarii** — revolutionary movements committed to throwing off
  Roman rule by force, prominent in the war that ended in 70 CE.
- **The Samaritans** — not a "sect" but a parallel people who worshipped the same
  God with their own version of the Torah and their own temple on **Mount
  Gerizim**, and who survive to this day.
- **Diaspora and Hellenistic Jews** — the vast Jewish populations outside Judea,
  above all in **Alexandria**, whose greatest thinker, **Philo**, fused the Torah
  with Greek (especially Platonic) philosophy by reading scripture allegorically.
- And, near the end of the period, a small Jewish messianic movement around a
  Galilean teacher named Jesus — at this stage simply **one more Judaism**, whose
  own chapter belongs to the next era.

## The library: scripture and its rivals

The depth of this period demands that we take its whole literature seriously — for
there was, as yet, **no single fixed "Bible."** What existed was a rich, contested
library:

- **The emerging Hebrew canon** (the **Tanakh**): the **Torah** was settled first,
  the **Prophets** next, and the **Writings** were still fluid at the end of the
  period. (The old idea of a "Council of Jamnia" that fixed the canon around 90 CE
  is now largely discredited; canonization was a gradual, contested process.)
- **The Septuagint** (LXX): the Greek translation of the scriptures, made from the
  third century BCE for the Greek-speaking Jews of Alexandria — itself revered as
  inspired, and the "Bible" of the early Church.
- **The Apocrypha / Deuterocanon**: books included in the Greek (and later
  Catholic and Orthodox) canon but not the Hebrew — **Tobit, Judith, Ben Sira
  (Sirach), the Wisdom of Solomon, 1–2 Maccabees, Baruch**, and additions to
  Daniel and Esther.
- **The Pseudepigrapha**: a vast body of works written under the names of ancient
  heroes — **1 Enoch, Jubilees, the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, the
  Psalms of Solomon, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch** — carrying much of the age's apocalyptic
  and esoteric imagination, and never canonized (though 1 Enoch remains scripture
  in the Ethiopian church).
- **The Dead Sea Scrolls**: besides biblical manuscripts, the sectarian writings —
  the **Community Rule**, the **War Scroll**, the thanksgiving **Hodayot**, the
  **pesharim** (line-by-line "decodings" of prophecy as fulfilled in the sect's own
  history), and the enigmatic **Copper Scroll**.

## Law, ritual, and the two centers

At the heart of the tradition stood the **Torah** and its interpretation
(**halakhah**), and under the pressures of empire the great identity-markers
intensified: **circumcision, the Sabbath, the dietary laws (kashrut), and ritual
purity** became the boundaries that kept a people distinct in a Hellenizing world.
The Maccabean crisis was fought precisely over these — whether a Jew could remain a
Jew while adopting Greek ways.

Practice had **two centers**, and their coexistence is the key to the age. The
first was the **Temple** in Jerusalem — rebuilt gloriously by Herod — where a
hereditary priesthood offered the daily sacrifices, where all Israel streamed for
the three **pilgrimage festivals**, and where, once a year on the **Day of
Atonement (Yom Kippur)**, the high priest alone entered the **Holy of Holies**
(now empty — the Ark was long lost) and sent the scapegoat into the wilderness
bearing the people's sins. The second center was the emerging **synagogue** — a
house of assembly, prayer, and Torah-reading, requiring no altar and no priest,
found wherever Jews lived. When Rome destroyed the Temple in 70 CE, it was the
synagogue and the Torah-scholarship of the Pharisees that allowed Judaism to
survive the loss — a resilience built, as we saw, in the exile.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

This is one of the richest periods in the whole archive for sacred encoding, and
several strands begin here that later chapters will follow.

**The unspeakable Name and the rise of number.** The reverence for the divine Name
(Chapter 7) deepened until **YHWH** was no longer pronounced at all, replaced in
reading by *Adonai*. Meanwhile, contact with Greek **isopsephy** helped the Jewish
practice of **gematria** — reading the number-values of Hebrew letters for hidden
meaning — take clearer shape in this period, though its great flowering lies later,
in rabbinic and kabbalistic hands. *An honesty note the project insists on:* the
deliberate number-symbolism and cipher of the ancient apocalyptic writers (below)
is a genuine literary device; the modern "**Bible code**" claims — hidden messages
found by equidistant-letter-sequence searches — are a twentieth-century phenomenon,
statistically discredited, and must not be confused with the real thing.

**Apocalyptic as code.** The apocalypses are, quite literally, **encoded political
theology.** Daniel's four beasts are a cipher for four empires; his "seventy weeks"
encode a chronology of the end; his "time, times, and half a time" hides a span
from hostile authorities. To read an apocalypse rightly was to *decode* current
history as the unfolding of a divine script — the same impulse the Qumran
**pesharim** turned on the prophets, reading Habakkuk or Isaiah as secret
predictions of the sect's own leaders and enemies.

**The Temple as a model of the cosmos.** Both **Philo** and **Josephus** read the
Temple as an encoded universe: its three zones as heaven, earth, and sea; the
great veil woven of four colors standing for the four elements; the high priest's
vestments as a wearable diagram of the cosmos, so that when he entered God's
presence he carried all creation with him. Sacred architecture and vestment as
theology made visible.

**Scripture on the body and the door.** The instinct we first met in the Ketef
Hinnom amulets (Chapter 7) — to wear the holy word for protection — matured into
the **tefillin** (phylacteries, small boxes of scripture bound on arm and head)
and the **mezuzah** (a scripture scroll on the doorpost). Tefillin have been found
at Qumran; here the sacred text becomes a physical, apotropaic presence, worn and
posted, encoding the covenant onto the very surfaces of daily life.

## Connections

This chapter is a great junction. It is the direct sequel to **pre-exilic Israel**
(Chapter 7), completing the journey to monotheism; it is where the fingerprints of
**Zoroaster** (Chapter 6) are most hotly sought; and it plays out in the Babylon
and under the Persia we met in Chapters 3 and 6. Sideways, across the Axial Age, it
is locked in dialogue with **Hellenism** and Greek philosophy (the Septuagint,
Philo), tying it to the classical-Greece thread of Chapter 8. And forward, it is
the indispensable seedbed of the next era's giants: **Christianity**, which begins
as one of these Second Temple Judaisms; **Rabbinic Judaism**, the Pharisees' heirs
who will remake the religion around Torah after the Temple's fall; and, more
distantly, **Islam**, **Gnosticism**, and the esoteric traditions of **Kabbalah**.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- The **Babylonian exile** (586 BCE) was the decisive transformation: it is in and
  after the exile that Israelite monolatry becomes explicit **monotheism** (Second
  Isaiah), that Torah moves to the center, and that non-sacrificial, text-centered
  worship (the seed of the synagogue) emerges.
- Second Temple Judaism was strikingly **diverse** — Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes,
  Zealots, Samaritans, Hellenistic/diaspora Jews, and apocalyptic groups — as the
  **Dead Sea Scrolls** dramatically confirmed.
- A large, layered **literature** existed with no single fixed canon: the emerging
  Tanakh, the Septuagint, the Apocrypha/Deuterocanon, and the Pseudepigrapha (1
  Enoch, Jubilees, etc.).
- New theological developments — a personalized **Satan**, ranked **angels and
  demons**, **resurrection** and final judgment (Daniel), and the **apocalyptic**
  genre — appear developed in this period.
- The historical framework (Persian → Hellenistic → Roman; the Maccabean revolt;
  Herod's Temple; the destruction of 70 CE) is well established.

**What's not supported by the evidence**

- The picture of a single, monolithic "Judaism" in this era. It was plural.
- A fixed, early biblical canon settled by a "Council of Jamnia" (a discredited
  idea); canonization was gradual and still open at the period's end.
- The claim that Zoroastrian influence on Jewish angelology, dualism, and
  eschatology is a **proven** fact. The parallels and the historical window are
  real; direct borrowing is unprovable ("resonance," not demonstrated dependence).

**What's genuinely open**

- The precise **extent and direction of influence** — Zoroastrian *and* Hellenistic —
  on Second Temple theology.
- The **identity of the Qumran community** (the majority view links it to the
  Essenes, but this is debated) and the figure of its "Teacher of Righteousness."
- The **dating and provenance** of many pseudepigrapha.
- How the diverse **messianic expectations** of the age related to one another —
  material this project takes up directly in the Christianity chapter.
