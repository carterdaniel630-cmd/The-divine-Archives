# Chapter 43 — Pistis Sophia: The Fallen Wisdom and the Books of the Saviour

*Tradition chapter · Era 05 — Late Antiquity · **Reviewed — cleared.***

## A book that surfaced a lifetime before Nag Hammadi

In 1772 a London physician named **Anthony Askew**, a collector with a taste for old
manuscripts, bought from a bookseller a heavy parchment codex written in a language few in
England could read: **Coptic**, the last stage of the Egyptian tongue, set down in Greek
letters. When Askew died the volume passed to his heirs, and in **1785 the British Museum
purchased it**, where it sits today as **MS Additional 5114** — the manuscript scholars call
the **Askew Codex**. It is a fat book of roughly **three hundred and fifty leaves** in the
**Sahidic** dialect, and it contains the single most complete Gnostic scripture that survived
the fall of the ancient Gnostic churches: the work we now call the **Pistis Sophia**.

That date matters. When a farmer's mattock struck the jar of codices at **Nag Hammadi** in
1945 (Chapter 17), the Pistis Sophia had already been sitting in a European library for
**a hundred and sixty years**. Long before the great modern recovery of the Gnostics'
own words, this one book — together with its rougher cousin, the **Books of Jeu** in the
**Bruce Codex** brought out of Egypt in 1769 — was almost the whole of what the modern world
possessed of Gnostic scripture *in the Gnostics' own voice* rather than in the hostile
paraphrase of the Church Fathers. For most of the nineteenth century, if you wanted to read a
Gnostic gospel, this was the gospel you read.

## The name, and the puzzle of what it is

The title is a modern convenience. In the manuscript itself the running headings call the work
**"The Books of the Saviour,"** and the label **Pistis Sophia** — Greek for **"Faith-Wisdom"**
or **"the Faithful Wisdom"** — was lifted by early editors from the name of the text's tragic
heroine and stuck to the whole. It is not one seamless composition. Scholars have long noticed
that the book is a **compilation**: episodes repeat, the setting shifts, names are used
inconsistently, and the work divides into **four "books"** (the divisions are editorial, and
Mead's popular translation even splits the material into more) that were plainly not written at
one sitting. The consensus is that the surviving Coptic is a **translation from a lost Greek
original**, and that the material was assembled in **Egypt** somewhere across the **third and
fourth centuries CE** — with the fourth book generally judged the **oldest and most archaic**
layer, closer in feel to the ritual handbooks of the Bruce Codex, and the long first section
the most developed.

So the Pistis Sophia is less a single revelation than an **anthology of a late Egyptian Gnostic
community's teaching** — the record of what one circle of initiates believed the risen Christ
had told them in secret. Which "school" that circle belonged to is genuinely uncertain; it is
often placed loosely in the orbit of **Sethian** and **Barbelo-Gnostic** mythology and linked
to the milieu of the Books of Jeu, but it fits no single system cleanly, and honest scholarship
leaves the label open.

## The frame: eleven silent years and a garment of light

The book opens on a scene the canonical gospels never give us. **Eleven years after the
resurrection**, Jesus is still on earth, sitting with his disciples on the **Mount of Olives**.
For all those years — the text says — he has taught them only in part, only the outer things,
"as far as the regions of the First Mystery." Then, at dawn, a **blaze of light** comes down
upon him, so bright the disciples cannot look, and Jesus **ascends into the heavens** clothed in
a radiant **garment** on which are written the secret names and the whole map of the upper
worlds. He passes through the gates of the firmament, the spheres, the aeons; the rulers of
those regions are thrown into terror and fall down before the light. Then he **descends again**,
and now — at last — he will tell his disciples **everything**: the full architecture of the
cosmos, and how a soul climbs home through it.

This "garment of light" frame is the engine of the whole book. It sets up the governing idea of
this particular Gnosticism: that salvation is a matter of **knowing the topography of the
heavens** — the names, seals, and passwords of each region — so that the soul, rising after
death, can satisfy the gatekeepers and pass. The Pistis Sophia is, at bottom, a **guidebook for
the ascent of the soul**, dressed as a conversation.

## The heart of the story: the fall and the thirteen repentances of Sophia

The long central drama — and the source of the title — is the myth of **Pistis Sophia**
herself. She is one of the **aeons**, a divine emanation, and she dwells in the **thirteenth
aeon**, gazing upward toward the light of the height. An arrogant power below, the
**Authadēs** ("the **Self-Willed** / the Arrogant One"), envies that light. He produces a
**false light**, a lure like a great lion-faced counterfeit, and Sophia — mistaking it for the
true light of the height she longs for — leaves her station and **descends toward it**. It is a
trap. The emanations of Authadēs and the lion-faced power surround her, **strip away her own
light**, and drive her down into **Chaos**, the lower darkness, where she is tormented and her
divine substance is drained.

What follows is the book's most beautiful and strangest passage. From the depths, Sophia sings
**thirteen songs of repentance** — thirteen **metanoiai**, hymns of grief and turning — crying
up toward the light she can no longer reach: *"O Light of Lights, in whom I have had faith from
the beginning, hear now my repentance…"* She confesses, she pleads, she remembers. And here the
text does something remarkable: after each of Sophia's laments, **the disciples interpret it** —
they tell Jesus that this repentance is "the word thy light-power prophesied through **David**"
or through **Solomon**, and they **recite the matching Psalm** or a matching **Ode of Solomon**.
The Pistis Sophia thereby weaves Sophia's cries together with the **Psalter** and with **five of
the Odes of Solomon** — and in doing so it became, for centuries, the **main surviving witness
to those Odes**, quoting them in Coptic long before the Syriac originals were rediscovered in
1909. It is a scripture built by **stitching older hymns into a new myth**.

Jesus does not rescue Sophia all at once. He is sent by the First Mystery, and he **descends by
stages**, each time carrying more of her stolen light back to her, beating back the archons of
Chaos, until finally her power is restored and she is led back up — not yet to her old
thirteenth aeon, but to a waiting place just below it, until the whole cosmic drama is complete.
The disciples, above all **Mary Magdalene**, question him at every step about what it all means.

## Mary Magdalene, and the quarrel over who may speak

The Pistis Sophia is one of the ancient world's most striking documents of a **woman as the
foremost disciple**. Of the roughly **forty-six** questions the disciples put to Jesus across
the book, **Mary Magdalene asks the great majority** — more than all the male disciples
together. She interprets the repentances, presses the hardest questions, and Jesus praises her
repeatedly, calling her blessed, "**pure spiritual one**," and the one "**whose heart is
directed to the Kingdom of Heaven** more than all thy brethren."

That prominence provokes a famous flash of conflict. **Peter** complains that Mary dominates
the conversation and **will not let the men speak**: "My Lord, we cannot endure this woman who
taketh the opportunity from us and hath let none of us speak, but she discourseth many times."
Later Mary confesses to Jesus that she is **afraid of Peter**, "because he threateneth me and
hateth our sex." Jesus overrules him: **whoever is filled with the spirit of light** may come
forward and speak, "and no one shall be able to prevent" them. Set beside the similar friction
in the **Gospel of Mary** and the **Gospel of Thomas**, this scene has become central to the
modern study of **women, gender, and authority in early Christianity** — a window onto
communities in which spiritual insight, not sex, decided who could teach.

## The machinery of salvation: mysteries, forgiveness, and the wheel of rebirth

Behind the story lies a whole **soteriology** — a theory of how souls are saved — and it is
unusually generous and unusually intricate.

Salvation comes through **mysteries**: specific rites, names, seals, and prayers that Jesus
grants, arranged in **ascending grades**. To "receive a mystery" is to be given the password and
the map for a region of the heavens; the higher the mystery, the higher the soul may climb.
Souls **without** the mysteries are caught, at death, by the **archons** — the rulers of the
spheres — who run a kind of celestial customs-house, and are sent back down. This is where the
Pistis Sophia teaches an explicit doctrine of **reincarnation**: souls that have not received
the saving mysteries are poured back into new bodies, cycle after cycle, bound to the **wheel of
Fate (Heimarmenē)** and the turning spheres.

One haunting image recurs: the **cup of forgetfulness**. Before a soul is sent back into a body,
a receiver-archon (named **Yaluham**) hands it a **cup of the water of oblivion**, "and it
drinketh it and **forgetteth all things** and all the regions to which it had gone" — so that it
returns to earth remembering nothing, and its former terrors teach it nothing. Against this
machinery of forgetting stands the mystery, which "**blotteth out**" the record kept by the
**counterfeit spirit** (a false soul the archons bind to each person to lead it into sin) and
frees the receiver from ever returning.

And yet the book's ethical tone is strikingly **merciful**. It insists there is scarcely a sin
the mysteries cannot dissolve; that the disciples may **forgive sins**, again and again; that
even souls carried into the deepest punishments — into the jaws of the **great dragon of the
outer darkness**, that ring of ice and fire "where there is neither pity nor light, but wailing
and gnashing of teeth" — are **not beyond reach**, and can be released if the living perform the
mystery on their behalf. Terror and tenderness sit side by side: a cosmos of merciless
gatekeepers, governed by a savior who wants everyone out.

## The oldest layer: the fire on the mountain

The **fourth book** — reckoned the most archaic part — drops the Sophia story and shows us
something rarer still: a **Gnostic rite in progress**. Jesus tells the disciples, "**Bring me
fire and vine-branches**," and lays out an **offering**: two vessels of **wine**, one to the
right and one to the left, bread, water, and herbs. The disciples stand behind him **clothed in
linen garments**, holding "the cipher of the name of the Father of the Treasury of the Light."
And Jesus turns to the **four corners of the world** and cries out an **invocation** made of
strings of **mystical vowels** — *"Hear me, O Father, father of all fatherhood, boundless Light:
iaō, iouō, iaō, aōi…"* — asking that the sins of the disciples be forgiven and their names
inscribed in the Kingdom of the Light. It is one of the very few places in ancient literature
where we seem to look directly at a Gnostic sacrament being performed rather than merely
described — a scene of enormous value for understanding what these communities actually **did**,
not only what they thought.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

If Chapter 17 called Gnosticism "the tradition of sacred encoding itself," the Pistis Sophia is
that principle carried to its extreme: a text in which **the cosmos is a cipher and salvation is
knowing the code.**

**The numbered heavens.** The whole universe of the book is an **enumerated hierarchy** — the
**thirteen aeons** (Sophia's home is the thirteenth), the **twelve aeons** below ruled by their
archons, the **five** Great Rulers of the midst, the **Treasury of the Light** with its orders
and its "**Nine Guards**," the spheres of Fate. To know a region is to know its **number, its
name, and its seal**. Cosmology here is literally a **theology-by-enumeration**, exactly as with
the thirty aeons of Valentinus and the 365 heavens of Basilides — but pressed further, into a
soul's practical itinerary.

**The vowels as divine names.** The invocations are built from **strings of Greek vowels** —
**IAŌ** and its permutations (*iaō, iouō, aōi*). In Greco-Egyptian magic and Gnosticism the
seven vowels **Α Ε Η Ι Ο Υ Ω** were held to encode the names of the powers and the music of the
seven spheres; **IAŌ** itself was a form of the divine name (a Greek rendering associated with
the Hebrew tetragrammaton). The Pistis Sophia uses these not as decoration but as **operative
words of power** — the actual keys spoken to open the heavens.

**The garment of light and the written name.** Jesus's shining **vesture**, inscribed with the
names and the plan of the upper worlds, is itself an encoded object — the map of salvation worn
as a robe — and the disciples in the fire-rite hold "**the cipher of the name**" of the Father
of the Treasury. The idea that a **written name or symbol carries the power it names** — met
already in Egyptian hieroglyphs and Hebrew divine names — reaches a kind of climax here.

**The lion-faced power.** The counterfeit light that lures Sophia down wears the face of a
**lion** — the same **lion-headed** iconography given to the demiurge **Yaldabaoth** in the
Sethian myth of Chapter 17 (and echoing the leontocephaline of the Mithraic mysteries). The
lion-face is the encoded sign of **arrogant, material power** masquerading as the divine.

**The hymn as hidden scripture.** Finally, the very technique of the book — folding the
**Psalms** and the **Odes of Solomon** into Sophia's repentances — is a form of encoding:
reading older sacred songs as **secretly about** the fall and rescue of Wisdom, so that
Scripture becomes a coded prophecy of the Gnostic myth.

## Connections

The Pistis Sophia is the **fullest surviving expression of a distinct late-Egyptian Gnosticism**,
and it sits in a dense web of relations across this archive. It is the near-twin of the
ritual **Books of Jeu** in the **Bruce Codex**, which it names, and which are generally thought
**older** — together they are the "Books of the Saviour" literature, the practical, ascent-and-
initiation wing of the Gnostic world. It shares its **Sophia myth**, its **aeons and archons**,
its **lion-faced demiurge**, and its inverted cosmos with the **Sethian and Valentinian**
systems of **Chapter 17**, but develops them toward ritual and eschatology rather than pure
myth. Its ladder of **spheres and gatekeepers**, its **wheel of Fate**, and its **passwords for
the dead** rhyme with the Orphic gold tablets and the astral ascent of **Chapter 15** and the
salvation-cults of **Chapter 18**; its explicit **reincarnation** and the **cup of
forgetfulness** recall the transmigration taught from India (Chapters 5, 11, 20) to Plato's myth
of Er. Its post-resurrection **dialogue form** and its prominent **Mary Magdalene** connect it to
the **Gospel of Mary** and the wider argument, still live in **Chapter 16**, over women's
authority in the earliest churches. And its afterlife runs straight into the **modern occult
revival**: its first English translator, **G. R. S. Mead**, was a **Theosophist** and secretary
to **Madame Blavatsky** (Chapter 37), and it was through Theosophy that "Pistis Sophia" entered
the twentieth-century esoteric imagination — a reminder that a text can have two lives, one in
late-antique Egypt and one on the séance-era bookshelf, and that the two must never be confused.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- The **manuscript** is real and well documented: the **Askew Codex** (British Library **MS
  Add. 5114**), a Coptic **Sahidic** parchment codex of some 350 leaves, bought by Dr. Anthony
  Askew around 1772 and acquired by the British Museum in **1785**; it contains the **Pistis
  Sophia** and a short untitled second text.
- The text is a **compilation** in (editorially) **four books**, a **Coptic translation of a
  lost Greek original**, assembled in **Egypt** across roughly the **3rd–4th centuries CE**, with
  the fourth book usually judged the **oldest** layer; its own running title is "**the Books of
  the Saviour**," and it **names the Books of Jeu**, which are generally thought earlier.
- Its **content** as described: the frame of the risen Jesus after **eleven years**, the myth of
  **Pistis Sophia's** fall through the deceit of **Authadēs** and her **thirteen repentances**,
  the interpretive weaving-in of the **Psalms** and **five Odes of Solomon** (of which this text
  was long the chief witness), the doctrine of saving **mysteries**, **reincarnation** and the
  **cup of forgetfulness**, the **punishments** and the **dragon of the outer darkness**, and the
  **fire-offering** rite of Book 4.
- The **prominence of Mary Magdalene** as the leading questioner and the **Peter–Mary tension**
  over whether she may speak — textually explicit and much discussed in modern scholarship.
- The **editorial history**: Woide's early transcript; the first printed edition, the Coptic text
  with a **Latin** translation by **Schwartze and Petermann** (Berlin, **1851**); **Carl
  Schmidt's** German work (1905); and **G. R. S. Mead's** influential English translation
  (1896, revised 1921) via the Theosophical movement.

**What's not supported by the evidence**

- That the Pistis Sophia records the **actual post-resurrection teaching of the historical
  Jesus**. It is a **3rd–4th-century** revelation-dialogue, a genre that puts new teaching in the
  mouth of the risen Christ; it is a witness to **later Gnostic belief**, not to the events it
  narrates.
- The common confusion that it is a **Nag Hammadi** text or part of the **Bruce Codex**. It is
  neither: it was known **150+ years before** Nag Hammadi, and it is the **Askew** Codex, while
  the Bruce Codex holds the separate **Books of Jeu**.
- The modern esoteric **"unveilings"** of the book (Theosophical, and later systems such as
  Samael Aun Weor's) as recoveries of its **original** meaning. These are **20th-century
  readings** laid over the text, valuable as reception history but not as evidence of what its
  ancient authors meant.

**What's genuinely open**

- The precise **date, place, and sectarian identity** of the community behind it — whether it is
  best called **Sethian**, **Barbeloite**, Valentinian-adjacent, or a school of its own. There is
  no consensus, and the compilation may blend more than one milieu.
- How the **"books" relate** — how many distinct sources were combined, in what order, and just
  how much **older** Book 4 (and the fire-rite) is than the Sophia narrative.
- The exact **original Greek** wording behind the Coptic, and how faithfully the surviving
  translation preserves it.
- How its rituals were **actually practiced** — the fire-offering scene is precious but isolated,
  and much of the community's real cult remains inferred rather than known.
