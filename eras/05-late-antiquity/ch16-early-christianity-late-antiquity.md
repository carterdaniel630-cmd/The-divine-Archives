# Chapter 16 — Early Christianity: The Faith That Conquered an Empire

*Tradition chapter · Era 05 — Late Antiquity · **DRAFT — READY FOR REVIEW***

## A fish scratched on a wall

In the dim tunnels of the Roman catacombs, among the tombs of the early faithful, a
simple sign recurs — a fish, drawn in a few curved strokes. To a Roman passer-by it
meant nothing. To those who knew, it was a compressed confession of faith. The Greek word
for fish, **ΙΧΘΥΣ** (*ichthys*), was read as an acrostic: **Ι**ησοῦς **Χ**ριστός **Θ**εοῦ
**Υ**ἱός **Σ**ωτήρ — *Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior*. In an age when to be a Christian
could mean death, a whole creed was hidden in the outline of a fish, a password one
believer could show another without a word.

That image holds the two themes of this chapter. First, that Christianity began as a
small, persecuted, and astonishingly **diverse** movement within the Jewish world of the
Roman East — not the vast institution it would become, but a scattering of assemblies
meeting in houses, hiding their faith in symbols. And second, that within three centuries
this movement would capture the very empire that crucified its founder and executed its
apostles, becoming the official religion of Rome. This is the story of how that happened —
told, as this archive tells every story, with the tools of history, and with a clear line
kept between **what the tradition holds sacred** and **what the historical evidence can
show**. Both are set down; neither is dressed up as the other.

## A note on faith and history

This chapter reconstructs the early Christian movement the way historians reconstruct any
ancient movement: from texts, dates, and material remains, asking what can be established
and what cannot. That method has its limits, and they must be stated honestly at the
outset. History can weigh whether a man named Jesus lived, was crucified, and was
proclaimed risen by his followers; it **cannot**, by its nature, confirm or deny that God
raised him, that he was the Son of God, or that the tomb was empty by divine act. Those
are claims of **faith**, held by billions, and the evidence-honesty standard of this work
neither asserts them as proven history nor dismisses them — it marks them clearly as what
they are. What a tradition holds sacred and what the material record establishes are two
different statements, and this chapter keeps them distinct while respecting both.

## The historical Jesus: what can be known

On the bare historical facts there is a scholarly consensus of unusual breadth, shared
across believers and skeptics alike. **A Jewish man named Jesus of Nazareth lived in
Roman Galilee in the early first century**, was **baptized by John the Baptist**, gathered
followers as an itinerant teacher and healer proclaiming the coming **Kingdom of God**,
and was **crucified in Jerusalem under the prefect Pontius Pilate**, around 30–33 CE. This
much is attested by multiple independent early sources and even, briefly, by non-Christian
writers: the Jewish historian **Josephus** (c. 93 CE) and the Roman **Tacitus** (c. 116
CE), who records that "Christus" was executed under Pilate. That Jesus existed and was
crucified is about as firmly established as anything in ancient history.

Beyond that skeletal outline, historical certainty fades quickly, because our sources are
themselves documents of faith. The earliest are the letters of **Paul** (written c. 50s
CE, within a generation of the crucifixion), which say strikingly little about Jesus's
life. The **Gospels** — Mark first (c. 70 CE), then Matthew and Luke (c. 80–90), and John
(c. 90–100) — were written decades after the events, in Greek, by authors traditionally but
not certainly identified, drawing on oral tradition and (in the case of Matthew and Luke) on
Mark and a hypothesized sayings-source scholars call **Q**. They are theological
proclamations, not neutral biographies, and they sometimes disagree on details. What Jesus
"really said and did" beneath this layered testimony is the subject of two centuries of the
scholarly "**quest for the historical Jesus**," which has reached no single answer.

## The resurrection and the birth of the church

At the center of the faith stands a claim history cannot adjudicate. Jesus's followers,
scattered and devastated by his execution, came within a very short time to proclaim that
**God had raised him from the dead** — and this conviction, whatever its ultimate nature,
is the engine of everything that follows. Paul preserves what is likely the **earliest
Christian creed**, a formula he says he "received" and passed on (1 Corinthians 15), listing
appearances of the risen Christ; it dates to within just a few years of the crucifixion.
Historians can establish *that* the first Christians sincerely believed and proclaimed the
resurrection, and that this belief transformed a broken band of Galileans into a missionary
movement of extraordinary energy. Whether the resurrection *happened*, and what it was, lies
beyond the historian's reach and within the domain of faith. This chapter records the belief
and its world-changing consequences, and leaves the verdict where it belongs.

## Paul and the parting from Judaism

Christianity began entirely **within Judaism** (Chapter 10) — Jesus and all his first
followers were Jews, and the earliest community in Jerusalem, led by **James** the brother
of Jesus and by **Peter**, kept the Torah and worshipped at the Temple. The figure who did
more than any other to turn a Jewish messianic sect into a universal religion was **Paul of
Tarsus**, a Pharisee who had persecuted the movement, then experienced a shattering vision
of the risen Christ and became its greatest missionary. Paul's radical conviction — that
through Christ, **Gentiles could be included without becoming Jews**, without circumcision
or the full Law — opened the movement to the whole Roman world and provoked the first great
controversy of the church, resolved (in Acts's account) at the **Council of Jerusalem**.
Over the following decades, hardened by the trauma of the Jewish War and the destruction of
the Temple in **70 CE**, and by mutual rejection, Christianity and Judaism gradually became
two separate religions — a painful "parting of the ways" whose long and bitter legacy the
later chapters of this archive will have to reckon with.

## The many Christianities

One of the most important corrections modern scholarship has made to the traditional
picture is this: there was no single, unified early Christianity. The first two centuries
saw a startling **diversity** of Christian movements, each with its own texts and
theology, competing to define the faith. There were **Jewish Christians** (such as the
**Ebionites**) who kept the Law and saw Jesus as a human Messiah; there was **Marcion**
(c. 140 CE), who rejected the Jewish scriptures and their God entirely, teaching that the
alien Father of Jesus was a different, higher God — and who, by publishing his own pared-down
scripture (an edited Luke and ten letters of Paul), forced the wider church to begin defining
its own **canon**; there were the various **Gnostic** Christians, with their secret knowledge
and elaborate mythologies (the subject of the next chapter); and there was the
"**proto-orthodox**" stream that would eventually prevail and define what "Christianity" came
to mean. The winners wrote the history, and it is only with modern discoveries — above all the
**Nag Hammadi** library of Gnostic texts (reserved for Chapter 17) — that the sheer variety of
the earliest movement has come back into view.

## The scriptures and the making of a canon

Christianity became a religion of a book, but the book was assembled slowly. The
**New Testament** — 27 documents in Greek — took shape over roughly three centuries. Its core
is the letters of **Paul** (of which about seven are near-universally accepted as authentically
his, several others disputed, and the "Pastoral" letters widely regarded as later works written
in his name), the four **Gospels**, the **Acts** of the Apostles, other epistles, and the
apocalyptic **Revelation**. Alongside these circulated a vast further literature — other
gospels, acts, and apocalypses (the **apocrypha** and the Gnostic texts) — that the emerging
church would ultimately exclude. The pressure to draw a firm boundary came partly from Marcion's
rival canon; the fourfold Gospel and the Pauline collection were established early, but the full
27-book list was not fixed until the later fourth century (Athanasius's letter of **367 CE** is
the first to name exactly our canon). Which books were "in" and which "out" was itself one of the
great struggles of early Christianity.

## Ritual, ethics, and the life of the community

Early Christian worship was intimate and domestic — believers gathered in private **houses**
(the earliest identified Christian building, at **Dura-Europos** in Syria, is a converted house
of c. 240 CE), where they prayed, sang, read scripture, and shared two central rites: **baptism**,
the water-initiation that washed away sin and joined the believer to Christ's death and
resurrection, and the **Eucharist**, the shared bread and wine commemorating the Last Supper as
the body and blood of Christ. Their **ethics**, drawn from Jesus's teaching — the **Sermon on the
Mount**, the command to love God and neighbor, even to love enemies, and a special care for the
poor, the sick, and the outcast — were radical in the Roman world, and their practical charity
(caring for plague victims, burying the destitute, elevating the status of women and slaves within
the community) is widely credited by historians as a real engine of the movement's growth.

## Persecution, martyrdom, and the turn of Constantine

For its first three centuries the church was an illegal or semi-legal sect, and its refusal to
worship the Roman gods or offer incense to the **imperial cult** (Chapter 13) marked Christians as
subversive "atheists" and traitors. Persecution was mostly local and sporadic, punctuated by
empire-wide waves under emperors such as **Nero** (who, Tacitus reports, scapegoated Christians for
the fire of Rome in 64 CE), **Decius**, and finally **Diocletian** in the "Great Persecution" (303
CE). The **martyrs** who died rather than recant became the heroes of the faith, their courage a
powerful witness. Then, astonishingly, the tide turned: the emperor **Constantine**, crediting a
vision of the cross with his victory at the Milvian Bridge (312 CE), legalized Christianity with the
**Edict of Milan** (313), convened the **Council of Nicaea** (325) to settle the church's disputes
over the nature of Christ, and set the faith on the road to dominance. By **380 CE**, under
**Theodosius**, Christianity was the official religion of the Roman Empire. The persecuted had become
the establishment — a transformation whose consequences run through the rest of this archive.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

**The fish and the hidden creed.** As we began, the **ΙΧΘΥΣ** fish was the great encoded emblem of
the persecuted church — a symbol that was simultaneously a picture, an acrostic creed (*Jesus Christ,
Son of God, Savior*), and a secret sign. It drew on the Gospel imagery of Jesus making his fishermen
disciples "fishers of men" and the miraculous catches, and let believers mark tombs and greet one
another in safety.

**The monograms of the Name.** Christian sacred encoding clustered around the **name of Christ**. The
**Chi-Rho** (☧) superimposes the first two Greek letters of *Christos* (Χ and Ρ) — the sign
Constantine placed on his soldiers' shields. The **staurogram** or Tau-Rho (⳨), combining *tau* and
*rho*, is older still, and remarkably, in the earliest Gospel manuscripts it was used within the word
"cross/crucify" as a tiny **pictogram of the crucified Jesus** — the earliest visual reference to the
crucifixion, two centuries before the scene was openly depicted. And the letters **Alpha and Omega**
(Α and Ω), the first and last of the Greek alphabet, encoded Christ's claim in Revelation to be "the
beginning and the end."

**The reticence of the cross.** Strikingly, the **cross** itself — later Christianity's universal
symbol — was rare in the first centuries. To a Roman world that knew crucifixion as the most shameful
of deaths, an instrument of torture was not an obvious emblem, and early Christians more often
depicted Christ as the youthful **Good Shepherd** carrying a lamb, or as the praying **orant** figure,
or through the **anchor** (hope) and the **dove** (the Spirit and peace). The cross rose to dominance
only after Constantine, when crucifixion was abolished and the symbol could be embraced without shame.

**666: a number that names an enemy.** The most famous piece of encoded meaning in the New Testament
is the "**number of the beast**," 666, in **Revelation** (13:18), which the text itself frames as a
riddle — "let the one who has understanding calculate." Applying Hebrew **gematria** (letter-values, as
in Chapter 7 and 10) to the name **Nero Caesar** (Hebrew *nrwn qsr*) yields exactly **666** — and the
identification is strengthened by an ancient manuscript variant reading **616**, which is what one gets
from the Latin form of the name. Most scholars now read the beast as the persecuting emperor **Nero**,
his name encoded to shield the author from Roman eyes: sacred numerology deployed as coded political
resistance.

## Connections

Christianity is unthinkable without **Second Temple Judaism** (Chapter 10) — its scriptures, its
messianic and apocalyptic hopes, its God — and it grew up in the religious marketplace of the Roman
Empire (Chapter 13), whose imperial cult it defied and whose roads and Greek language it used to
spread. Its theology drew deeply on **Greek philosophy** (Chapter 15): the Gospel of John opens by
identifying Christ with the **Logos**, and the Church Fathers would build Christian doctrine with the
tools of Plato and Aristotle. It is the sibling and rival of the **Gnosticism** and the **Roman mystery
cults** of the next chapters, and of the **Rabbinic Judaism** forming in the same centuries. Forward,
its story dominates much of this archive: the great **Christological councils** and the making of
orthodoxy, the split of **East and West**, the rise of **Islam** partly in response to it (Chapter on
the early medieval), the **medieval** church, the **Reformation**, and the countless movements and
mysticisms that flow from it down to the present.

## The evidence, honestly

**What's well supported by the evidence**

- The **existence and crucifixion of Jesus** — a Galilean Jewish teacher baptized by John and
  executed under Pontius Pilate around 30–33 CE — is affirmed by a very broad scholarly consensus, on
  multiple independent early sources plus Josephus and Tacitus.
- That his followers **proclaimed him risen** within a very few years (the pre-Pauline creed of 1 Cor
  15), and that this belief launched a rapidly spreading movement led by **Paul**, whose Gentile
  mission opened it to the Roman world.
- The **diversity** of early Christianities (Jewish-Christian, Marcionite, Gnostic, proto-orthodox),
  the slow **formation of the New Testament canon**, the domestic worship and rites of **baptism** and
  **Eucharist**, the reality of **persecution and martyrdom**, and the **legalization under
  Constantine** and establishment under Theodosius.

**What's not supported by the evidence (as history)**

- The **miraculous and theological claims** — the virgin birth, the miracles, and above all the
  **resurrection** and divinity of Jesus — are matters of **faith**, not propositions history can
  establish; this chapter records them as the tradition's beliefs, not as demonstrated facts (and
  equally does not claim to disprove them).
- The traditional **authorship** of the Gospels by eyewitnesses, and the assumption that they are
  straightforward biography rather than later, theologically-shaped testimony.
- The old picture of a single, uniform original Christianity later corrupted by "heresies" — the
  evidence shows diversity from the very beginning.

**What's genuinely open**

- The **historical Jesus** beneath the Gospel portraits — how much of his teaching and biography can
  be securely recovered (the unresolved "quest").
- The precise **dating and authorship** of several New Testament books, and the existence and shape of
  the hypothesized **Q** source.
- The exact process and timing of the **parting of the ways** between Christianity and Judaism, now
  seen as more gradual and regionally varied than once thought.
- How and why Christianity **grew** so successfully — the relative weight of its charity, its
  martyr-witness, its message, and Constantine's patronage.
