# Chapter 24 — Tantra: The Body as the Cosmos


The esoteric revolution that swept across early-medieval India and Buddhism alike — a ritual technology of mantra, diagram, and the subtle body that made the human being a map of the universe — and the careful work of separating what the tradition teaches, what history can date, and what a century of Western reinvention has laid on top of it.

## The circle of the sixty-four

On a low rise at Khajuraho, in central India, stands a temple unlike the famous carved spires nearby: an **open-air ring of stone shrines**, roofless, arranged in a great rectangle open to the sky. It is the **Chausath Yogini** temple — "the sixty-four yoginis" — and it is the oldest surviving building at the Chandela capital, dated to about **885 CE**. Once it held sixty-four cells for the images of the *yoginis*, fierce and powerful goddesses attendant on the Great Goddess, with a sixty-fifth shrine for the deity herself. Thirty-five cells survive. Here, the tradition holds, rites were performed under the yoginis' gaze that were never meant for the ordinary temple crowd.

The open circle of yoginis is one of the few things Tantra left standing in plain sight, and it is a fitting emblem — because Tantra is a religion of the hidden. Its central texts are written in code, its practices are transmitted only from teacher to initiate, and much of what the modern world thinks it knows about it is a recent invention. This chapter is about a genuine and enormously influential current in early-medieval religion — and about the unusual care needed to describe it, because few subjects have been so thoroughly overwritten.

## What "Tantra" is — and is not

"**Tantra**" is a Sanskrit word (from a root meaning "to weave," hence "loom," "system," or "continuity") that names, most narrowly, a class of ritual scriptures. But **"Tantrism" as a single -ism is a scholarly construct** — a category assembled by Western Indologists in the nineteenth century to group a diverse array of Hindu and Buddhist esoteric practices that their own practitioners never gathered under one name. Keeping that in mind guards against the commonest error: treating "Tantra" as one doctrine rather than a family resemblance among many traditions.

What those traditions share is a distinctive approach. Tantra arose in India no later than the **fifth century CE**, flowered from roughly **600 to 1100**, and appears in both **Hinduism and Buddhism**. It seeks not only liberation (*mokṣa* or *nirvāṇa*) but also **power** (*siddhi*), through the ritual channeling of divine energy — **śakti** — and it works by aligning the human microcosm with the cosmic whole through **mantras** (sacred sound), **yantras** and **mandalas** (sacred diagrams), visualization, and above all **initiation** (*dīkṣā*) under a **guru**. Its scriptures are written in a deliberately veiled "**twilight language**" (*sandhābhāṣā*) that, the tradition insists, cannot be read without a teacher.

## The textual record

The Hindu tantric scriptures are known as **Āgamas** (where the god Shiva instructs the Goddess) or, conversely, **Nigamas**; they typically treat four domains — metaphysical knowledge (*jñāna*), yoga, ritual (*kriyā*), and conduct (*caryā*). The **Shaiva** Agamas, the **Shakta** Tantras of the Goddess, and the **Vaishnava** Pancharatra Samhitas each form vast bodies of text, most surviving in manuscripts of the seventh to tenth centuries.

The Buddhist tantras were sorted by later tradition into a rising series — **Kriyā, Caryā, Yoga, and Anuttarayoga** (highest) tantras. The most advanced, the **Mahāyoga** and **Yoginī** tantras, emerged between the eighth and tenth centuries: the **Guhyasamāja**, **Cakrasaṃvara**, and **Hevajra** tantras, the last composed in eastern India in the late eighth to tenth century. These texts introduced the coded language of advanced practice — deity visualization, charnel-ground meditation, wrathful deities, and the ritual use of substances and sexual yoga that scandalized outsiders and were hedged in secrecy within.

Dating any of this precisely is hard, and honestly so: the texts are esoteric, often undated, transmitted orally as much as in writing, and deliberately obscure.

## The body as a map of the cosmos

Tantra's most influential idea is the **subtle body**. Beneath the gross physical body, the tradition teaches, lies an energetic body threaded by channels (*nāḍī*) — said to number 72,000, with three chief ones: the central **suṣumṇā** running up the spine and the **iḍā** and **piṅgalā** spiralling around it. Where the channels meet sit the **chakras**, "wheels," centers of concentrated energy, each imagined as a lotus with a **seed-syllable** (*bīja* mantra) at its heart. Coiled at the base sleeps **kuṇḍalinī**, the serpent-power, a form of Śakti; the goal of much tantric yoga is to wake her and draw her up the central channel to union with **Śiva** at the crown, a union that is the microcosmic image of the reuniting of God and Goddess, consciousness and energy.

It must be said plainly: the subtle body is a **contemplative and experiential map, not a claim about anatomy**. No dissection finds a chakra or a nāḍī, and the tradition, at its most careful, does not say one would. This is a religious model of inner experience — powerful, coherent, and deeply influential — and it is a category error, common in modern popular writing, to treat it as physiology.

## The deities

Tantric Hinduism is above all a religion of **Śiva** and the **Goddess**. Śiva appears in his fierce tantric form as **Bhairava**; the Goddess as **Kālī**, dark and terrible, or as **Tripurasundarī** ("the beautiful one of the three cities"), serene sovereign of the Śrī Vidyā tradition, or as the ring of **yoginīs**. In the nondual Shaiva theology, Śiva is pure consciousness and Śakti his inseparable power; the world is their play, and the aim is to realize one's own identity with that divine ground. In tantric **Buddhism**, the practitioner visualizes and identifies with a chosen deity (*yidam*) — often a wrathful, cremation-ground figure like **Hevajra** or **Cakrasaṃvara** in union with his consort — as a technique for realizing emptiness and buddha-nature.

## The schools

- **Kashmir Shaivism** (Trika) is the philosophical summit. Arising in Kashmir after about **850 CE**, it found its great synthesizer in **Abhinavagupta** (c. **950–1020**), whose encyclopedic **Tantrāloka** — some 5,800 verses in 37 chapters — wove every available Shaiva tantric stream into a single nondual philosophy of consciousness. He is one of the towering intellects of Indian thought, and he belongs squarely to this era.
- The **Kāpālikas** ("skull-bearers") and **Kaulas** were the radical ascetics who wrote the **Bhairava Tantras**. The Kāpālika imitated his ferocious god: smeared with cremation ash, carrying a skull-bowl, propitiating his deities with the impure — blood, meat, wine, and sexual fluids — deliberately breaking caste and purity taboos. Their descendants include the **Aghoris**.
- The **Śākta** traditions of the Goddess, especially **Śrī Vidyā** centered on the Śrī Yantra, carried tantric worship into more mainstream and "right-handed" forms.
- The **Nāth** yogis systematized the subtle-body yoga that would later feed haṭha yoga.
- In Buddhism, the **Vajrayāna** ("thunderbolt vehicle") descended from the wandering **mahāsiddhas**, the "great adepts," and became the tantric form of Mahayana.

## Ritual and the two hands

Tantric practice is built on initiation and technique. After **dīkṣā** from a guru, the initiate works with **mantra** (repeated sacred sound), **yantra** (meditation on a diagram), **nyāsa** (ritually installing mantras and deities onto the body by touch), **mudrā** (gesture), and elaborate visualization. Within this, tradition drew a famous distinction between the "**right-hand**" path (*dakṣiṇācāra*), which kept the transgressive elements symbolic or substituted, and the "**left-hand**" path (*vāmācāra*), which enacted them.

The left-hand rites center on the **pañcamakāra**, the "**five Ms**": *madya* (wine), *māṃsa* (meat), *matsya* (fish), *mudrā* (parched grain), and *maithuna* (ritual sexual union) — the five things orthodox Hindu purity most forbade, consumed in a tightly bounded ritual frame to dissolve the ego's divisions of pure and impure and to realize the nonduality behind them. Whether particular lineages performed the fifth M literally or interpreted the whole set symbolically is a **genuine and unresolved scholarly question**; the sources themselves range from the plainly literal to the frankly allegorical, and the secrecy that surrounded the rite means the historical reality is often simply not recoverable. What must be resisted is both the old **outsider's caricature** of orgy and the modern **sanitizing** that pretends the literal rites never existed; the honest record holds both the transgression and the ritual discipline that framed it.

## Buddhist Vajrayāna and the road to Tibet

Tantric Buddhism grew from the **mahāsiddhas**, the wandering adepts of eastern and northwestern India, and its texts appeared from perhaps the sixth or seventh century, with the term *Vajrayāna* itself first attested in the eighth. Its method is **deity yoga**: the practitioner generates a vivid identification with an enlightened being to actualize buddhahood swiftly. In **747**, tradition holds, the master **Padmasambhava** carried these teachings into Tibet, anchoring the lineage of the Nyingma school; after a persecution, a **second diffusion** in the late tenth and eleventh centuries reintroduced the later cycles — the Hevajra and Cakrasaṃvara tantras among them — and set Tibetan Buddhism on the tantric path it has followed since. The early-medieval centuries are, precisely, the age in which this transmission happened.

## Material record: temples of the yoginis

Tantra's clearest physical trace is the **yoginī temple** — around thirteen survive across India, eight of them in Madhya Pradesh, most built between the ninth and twelfth centuries. Their **open, circular, roofless** plan, unique in Indian temple architecture, set the sky itself as the ceiling over the ring of goddesses, and marks them as spaces for rites outside the ordinary temple order; they are associated with the Kāpālika and Kaula streams. At Khajuraho more broadly, the **Chandela temples** (c. 950–1050) carry the famous erotic sculpture that so shocked later observers — a fraction of the total carving, and one whose exact relation to tantric ritual is debated, but which belongs to the same world in which the sacred and the sexual were not held apart.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Tantra is, more than almost any tradition in this book, a religion of **encoded form** — of sound and diagram treated as the very body of the divine.

- **Mantra**: the tantric mantra is not merely a prayer but a **sonic form of the deity**. The **bīja** or "seed" syllables — *oṃ*, *hrīṃ*, *śrīṃ*, *aiṃ*, and the rest — carry no dictionary meaning; they are held to be the deity in the form of sound, and each chakra is assigned its own. To speak the seed is to invoke the power.
- **Yantra**: a sacred diagram used as a focus of meditation, the deity in geometric form. The supreme example is the **Śrī Yantra** — **nine interlocking triangles** (traditionally four "male," pointing up, and five "female," pointing down) radiating from a central point, the **bindu**, and enclosed in lotus petals and a square frame, the **bhūpura**, with four gates. The practitioner meditates **inward** from the outer frame (the earth and body) through the petals (the senses) and the triangles (the play of Śiva and Śakti) to the **bindu**, the dimensionless point of pure consciousness. It is a map of the cosmos and of the path drawn as one figure.
- **Mandala**: the Buddhist counterpart, a circular palace of the deity into which the initiate is symbolically led, used for visualization and initiation.
- **Nyāsa and mudrā**: the ritual **writing of mantras onto the body** by touch, and the coded language of hand-gestures, encode the divine directly into flesh and posture.
- **Twilight language** (*sandhābhāṣā*): the deliberate double-coding of the tantras themselves, in which a word may mean one thing to the uninitiated reader and quite another to the initiate — encoding built into the scripture as a lock against the unprepared.

A firm line must be drawn here, as the tradition's own rule about honesty demands. The **classical** system above is well attested in medieval texts. But the familiar modern **seven-chakra rainbow**, with its fixed colors and one-to-one map to glands and psychology, is substantially a **twentieth-century synthesis** — shaped by early translators and by Western esoteric and New Age reworking — not a straight transcription of the medieval sources, which vary in the number and character of the centers. And the popular Western equation of "**Tantra**" with **sacred sex** is a **modern distortion**: classical Tantra is overwhelmingly a matter of mantra, visualization, and ritual, in which sexual yoga is one bounded, secret, and contested element of some left-hand lineages, not the heart of the thing.

## Connections

Tantra grew out of the **Vedic and Puranic Hinduism** and the **Mahayana Buddhism** of the earlier eras, and reshaped both from within. It fed directly into **Tibetan Buddhism** (the neighboring transmissions of this very period), into the **Nāth** yogis and thence into **haṭha yoga**, into the devotional **Bhakti** movements of the next era, and into the Śrī Vidyā worship still living today. Its Kāpālika radicals stand beside the cremation-ground ascetics of many traditions; its coded diagrams rhyme with the sacred geometry of Islam and the Kabbalah of the same centuries. And in the twentieth century its vocabulary — chakras, kundalini, mantra, mandala — became, in reworked form, part of the global language of yoga and the New Age, which is exactly why separating the medieval reality from the modern overlay matters so much here.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The existence of a vast tantric scriptural corpus in both Hinduism and Buddhism from roughly the fifth century on; the historical reality of **Abhinavagupta** (c. 950–1020) and the **Tantrāloka**; the **Kāpālika/Kaula** ascetic traditions and their transgressive reputation; the **yoginī temples** and their distinctive open plan (Khajuraho c. 885); the rise of **Vajrayāna** and its transmission to **Tibet** across these centuries; and the core ritual technology of **mantra, yantra, mandala, initiation, and the subtle-body model**.

**Not supported (as history or science).** That the **chakras and nāḍīs** are anatomical structures — they are a contemplative model of inner experience, not physiology. That the popular **seven-chakra color system** is the unaltered medieval teaching — it is largely a modern synthesis. That **"Tantra" means sacred sex** — a modern Western distortion of a mostly ritual and meditative tradition.

**Genuinely open.** The precise **dating and authorship** of most tantric texts, given their esoteric, oral, and deliberately obscure transmission. **How literally** particular lineages performed the pañcamakāra, especially the fifth M — the sources genuinely differ and the secrecy is real. The exact **relationship** between Khajuraho's erotic sculpture and tantric ritual. And the always-difficult question, in a tradition built on secrecy and twilight language, of how much of the inner practice was ever written down at all.
