# Chapter 25 — Shinto: The Way of the Kami

*Recently added — pending full review.*

Japan's religion of the kami — a faith with no founder and, for most of its history, no separate name, so entangled with Buddhism that scholars still argue whether "Shinto" as an independent religion existed at all before modern times — reconstructed from an eighth-century imperial chronicle, a shrine that is torn down and rebuilt every twenty years, and the hard task of separating ancient kami-worship from its later reinventions.

## The shrine that is always new

At **Ise**, in central Japan, stands the holiest shrine of the kami — a simple structure of unpainted **cypress**, raised on posts, roofed in thatch, in a style so plain it looks older than any temple. Beside it lies an empty plot of raked gravel the same size and shape. Every twenty years the shrine is **torn down and built again, exact in every joint, on that neighboring ground**, the deity moved to the new hall and the old one dismantled — a ritual called the **shikinen sengū**, carried out for roughly **1,300 years**, most recently begun in 2025. The point is not decay but renewal: the building is mortal so that the form, the craft, and the presence within it can be endlessly, perfectly reborn.

That paradox — a tradition that keeps itself ancient by continually remaking itself — runs through the whole of Shinto, and through the problem of writing about it. For "Shinto" is a faith with **no founder, no single scripture, and, for most of its history, no separate name**. What we can study is real: shrines, festivals, an eighth-century mythology, a thousand years of ritual. What is genuinely disputed is whether any of it was a distinct "religion" called Shinto before quite recent times, or whether that is a category we have read backward onto something far less separable.

## What "Shinto" is — and the problem of the name

The word **Shinto** ("the way of the kami," from Chinese *shen-dao*) names the worship of the **kami** — the gods, spirits, and sacred powers of Japan: the sun, storms, mountains, rice, ancestors, and the **eight million kami** (*yaoyorozu no kami*), a number meaning simply "countless." Kami are not omnipotent creators but presences to be honored, feared, and kept in good relation through offering and purity.

But the influential thesis of the historian **Kuroda Toshio** (1926–1993) reshaped the field: he argued that **Shinto as an independent religion took shape only in modern times**, and that for most of Japanese history kami-worship existed *within* a Buddhist framework — so fused with Buddhism as to be, in effect, one of its branches. Not everyone accepts the full claim — recent scholars (Breen, Teeuwen, and critics who ask whether "Shinto history begins at Kuroda") have qualified it, arguing for real continuities in kami-worship across the centuries. But the core caution is now standard: **for the early-medieval period this chapter covers, "Shinto" and "Buddhism" were not two religions a Japanese person chose between; they were a single, blended religious world.** That fusion is the heart of this chapter.

## The textual record

Shinto's mythology survives because an eighth-century imperial court wrote it down — for its own reasons. The **Kojiki** ("Record of Ancient Matters," **712**), compiled by **Ō no Yasumaro** from oral traditions, and the **Nihon Shoki** ("Chronicles of Japan," **720**), a committee work in the Chinese annalistic style, both open with the age of the gods and run down to the historical emperors. They are not neutral scripture: they were composed to **legitimize the imperial line** by tracing it to the gods, and the Nihon Shoki was partly written to correct the Kojiki and restore clans it had slighted. To these are added the **Fudoki** (regional gazetteers), the poems of the **Man'yōshū**, the ritual **norito** prayers preserved in the **Engishiki** (**927**), and the **Kogo Shūi**. The mythology is old — passed down orally as the *Teiki* and *Kuji* before it was ever fixed — but the form we have is an **eighth-century political document**, and the two cannot be fully separated.

## Cosmology and the kami

In the beginning, the Kojiki says, the primordial pair **Izanagi and Izanami** stood on the floating bridge of heaven, stirred the sea with a jeweled spear, and made the islands of Japan and a host of kami. Bearing the fire-kami, **Izanami was burned to death** and went down to the land of the dead; **Izanagi** pursued her, fled her corruption, and, washing himself clean in a river — the first **misogi**, or purification — brought forth from his very act of cleansing the three great kami: **Amaterasu**, the sun-goddess, from his left eye; **Tsukuyomi**, the moon, from his right; and **Susanoo**, the storm, from his nose.

When Susanoo's violence drove **Amaterasu** to hide in a cave and plunge the world into darkness, the other kami lured her out with a mirror and revelry — restoring the sun. Her grandson **Ninigi** was sent down to rule the land, carrying the sacred regalia; and his descendant **Jimmu** became, in the tradition, the **first emperor**. The claim that the imperial house descends from the sun-goddess is the theological center of the whole mythology — and it is a claim of faith, not a finding of history.

## Purity, pollution, and worship

If one idea organizes Shinto practice, it is **purity**. The tradition is far less concerned with sin than with **kegare** — pollution, the defilement that comes from death, blood, disease, and disorder — and with restoring **purity** through ritual cleansing. Worship centers on the **jinja** (shrine), entered through a **torii** gate that marks the passage from ordinary to sacred ground; visitors rinse hands and mouth at the **temizuya** water basin; priests (**kannushi**), assisted by shrine maidens (**miko**), perform **harae** (purification), recite **norito** prayers, present offerings of rice, sake, and cloth, and hold **matsuri** — the seasonal festivals that carry the kami through the community. The most rigorous purification, **misogi**, immerses the body in cold river or waterfall, re-enacting Izanagi's cleansing. The concern is not moral guilt but ritual cleanness: to come before the kami rightly, and to keep the community and the land in order.

## The fusion with Buddhism

The defining fact of Shinto in this era is its **union with Buddhism** — *shinbutsu-shūgō*, "the amalgamation of kami and buddhas." When Buddhism arrived in the sixth century it did not displace the kami; the two grew together. From the **early eighth century**, **jingū-ji** — Buddhist temples built at Shinto shrines — appeared, so that sutras were chanted to the kami and kami were understood as protectors of the Dharma.

In the **Heian period (794–1185)** this hardened into a theology: **honji suijaku**, "original ground and manifest trace." The kami, it held, were **local manifestations** (*suijaku*, "traces") of universal **buddhas and bodhisattvas** (*honji*, "original ground") — Amaterasu, for instance, identified with the cosmic Buddha Dainichi (Mahāvairocana). The great Buddhist schools built their own syntheses: the Shingon school's **Ryōbu Shintō** and the Tendai school's **Sannō Shintō** wove kami and buddhas into single systems of doctrine and ritual. For roughly a thousand years, until the forced separation of 1868, this blended religion — not a "pure" Shinto — was simply the religion of Japan.

## Shrines, treasures, and the ground

The material record is grand and specific. **Ise Jingū**, sacred to Amaterasu, in its austere **shinmei-zukuri** style of raw cypress, is rebuilt every twenty years. **Izumo Taisha**, one of the oldest and largest shrines, sacred to **Ōkuninushi**, is renewed on a longer cycle; its region, Izumo, holds an alternative mythic tradition that the imperial Kojiki had to absorb and subordinate — a hint that the "national" myth papered over older regional powers.

At the symbolic center stand the **Three Sacred Treasures**, the imperial regalia: the mirror **Yata no Kagami** (said to be kept at Ise), the sword **Kusanagi no Tsurugi** (at Atsuta Shrine), and the jewel **Yasakani no Magatama**. They are said to have descended with Ninigi and to authenticate the emperor. Crucially for an honest account: they are **never shown** — not to the public, not to scholars, arguably not even to the emperor. Their existence, age, and nature cannot be independently verified; what history can attest is the *tradition* of them and their role in enthronement, not the objects themselves.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Shinto encodes the sacred less in doctrine than in **thresholds, objects, and words**.

- The **torii** gate encodes the boundary itself — the line between the profane and the sacred; to pass through it is already a small purification.
- The **shimenawa**, a thick twisted rope of rice straw, hung with **shide** (white zigzag paper streamers), marks a space, tree, rock, or rope-bound object as the dwelling or presence of a kami — a visible fence around the holy.
- The **mirror** is the supreme sacred object: at Ise, a mirror is the **shintai**, the "god-body," the vessel in which Amaterasu's presence resides — not an image of the kami but the very thing the kami inhabits. The **three regalia** are read as encoded virtues: the mirror for **wisdom** (and honesty), the sword for **valor**, the jewel for **benevolence**.
- **Kotodama**, "word-spirit," is the belief that spoken sounds carry real spiritual power — that the very syllables of a **norito** prayer, correctly uttered, act upon the world. It makes ritual language itself a sacred technology, the Japanese cousin of the mantra.
- The **architecture** encodes theology: Ise's plainness, its perishable cypress, and its perpetual renewal are a statement — that purity and presence, not permanence of stone, are what the sacred requires.

A necessary caution, as the tradition's own honesty demands: much of what is now presented as timeless "Shinto symbolism" was **codified far later**, and the fiercely nationalist **State Shinto** of 1868–1945 — with the emperor as a living god and the shrines as arms of the state — was a **modern political construction**, not an ancient faith. It must not be read back onto the kami-worship of this era.

## Connections

Shinto is the Japanese member of this era's great pattern: an **indigenous, nature-centered polytheism meeting a universal world religion** — precisely the encounter the neighboring **Norse** chapter describes for northern Europe, though here the two fused rather than one replacing the other. Its partner and near-inseparable other half is the **Buddhism** of the same centuries, especially the esoteric **Shingon** and **Tendai** schools that are the Japanese branches of the **Tantra** of the previous chapter. It absorbed Chinese **Confucian** and **Daoist** cosmology through the same channel that brought its writing system. And its later, modern reinventions — the 1868 separation of kami and buddhas, and State Shinto — belong to a much later era and are noted here only to be kept out of this one.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The existence and dating of the **Kojiki (712)** and **Nihon Shoki (720)** and their role as imperial-legitimation texts; the antiquity and continuity of **kami-worship** and **shrine ritual**; the reality of **shinbutsu-shūgō** and the **honji suijaku** theology as the dominant religious form of the era; the shrines themselves — **Ise** and its twenty-year renewal, **Izumo** — and the tradition of the **three regalia**; the centrality of **purity/pollution** and purification ritual.

**Not supported (as history).** The **divine descent of the imperial line** from Amaterasu — a claim of faith, not a historical finding. The idea of a **pure, original Shinto** cleanly separable from Buddhism in the medieval period — the evidence points the other way. The projection of modern **State Shinto** (1868–1945) back onto ancient kami-worship — a modern nationalist construction, not the early religion. The **existence and authenticity of the three regalia** as physical objects — they are never shown and cannot be verified; only the tradition can be attested.

**Genuinely open.** How much of the **Kojiki mythology** is genuinely old oral tradition and how much is eighth-century political shaping. Whether **"Shinto" was a distinct religion** before modern times at all — the Kuroda thesis is influential but debated. The real independent power and religion of **Izumo** before the imperial myth absorbed it. The precise dating of many shrine origins and practices, in a tradition that wrote little down and renews rather than preserves its oldest material forms.
