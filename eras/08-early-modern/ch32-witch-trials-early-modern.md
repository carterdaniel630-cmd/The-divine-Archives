# Chapter 32 — The Witch Trials: The Persecution of an Imaginary Crime

The two-and-a-half centuries in which early modern Europe hunted, tortured, and killed tens of thousands of its own people — overwhelmingly poor women — for a crime that, as its persecutors imagined it, did not exist; told as the documented history of a persecution, not as evidence that its victims were what they were accused of being.

## The confession

Somewhere in the German lands around 1600, an old woman — a widow, perhaps, or the village healer people came to for a charm against fever — is denounced by a neighbor after a cow sickens or a child dies. She is arrested, stripped, and searched for the **Devil's mark**. She denies everything. She is tortured — the thumbscrews, the strappado that pulls the arms from their sockets — until she confesses what her interrogators already know she must confess: that she made a pact with Satan, flew by night to the **witches' sabbath**, feasted and copulated with demons, and blighted her neighbors' crops and cattle. Then she is made to name the others she saw there. Some of them will be arrested next. And then she is burned.

She was not a witch. There was no secret society of Devil-worshippers flying to sabbaths; the crime she confessed to was, in that form, a **fantasy** — assembled by learned men and extracted by pain. This is the hardest thing to hold steady about the witch trials, and the most important: the persecution was utterly real, and the conspiracy it punished was not.

## What the witch trials were

Between roughly **1450 and 1750**, the courts of Christian Europe and its colonies tried perhaps **100,000 people** for witchcraft and executed somewhere between **40,000 and 60,000** — the great majority in the fragmented **Holy Roman Empire**. The "witch" they hunted was a specific and terrible invention: not merely someone who worked harmful magic, but a person who had **renounced God, sworn allegiance to the Devil**, and joined a vast secret conspiracy against Christendom. It is this second, diabolical element — the pact, the sabbath, the anti-church of Satan — that was, as modern scholarship overwhelmingly agrees, **largely fabricated** by the persecutors themselves.

## The making of the witch

The idea was a **welding-together** of two very different things. The first was ordinary belief in **maleficium** — harmful magic — which almost everyone in pre-modern Europe accepted, and which shaded into the genuine world of village **cunning-folk**: healers, charmers, and finders of lost things who really existed and mostly did good. The second was an elite theory of **demonology**, worked out by theologians and lawyers, that reinterpreted this folk magic as **Devil-worship**. The two fused into the "cumulative concept" of the witch, and the fusion was given its most infamous handbook in the **Malleus Maleficarum** ("Hammer of Witches," 1486), the venomous, deeply misogynistic treatise of the Dominican **Heinrich Kramer**. It was a best-seller for two centuries — yet, tellingly, it was **never authorized by the Catholic Church**, and was in fact condemned by leading theologians of its own day for bad doctrine and illegal methods.

## The machinery

What turned belief into slaughter was **procedure**. Where courts adopted **inquisitorial** methods and permitted **torture**, two things followed: confessions could be produced to order, and tortured suspects could be forced to **name accomplices** — so that a single accusation could chain-react into a panic that consumed dozens or hundreds. The trials were not the work of one church: **Catholic and Protestant** courts both hunted witches, and the worst outbreaks — Trier, Bamberg, Würzburg — came where central legal authority was weakest and local zeal unchecked. Across the Atlantic, the **Salem** trials of 1692, which hanged twenty, were a small, late colonial flare of the same fire.

## Who died, and why

About **three-quarters to four-fifths** of those executed across Europe were **women** — most of them old, poor, widowed, or marginal, and often the very healers and midwives their communities had once relied on. That pattern makes misogyny a central thread of the hunts. But it was not the whole story: in **Iceland** some ninety percent of the accused were men, and men were the majority in **Finland** and **Estonia** too. Historians such as Brian Levack therefore reject any single cause and stress a **convergence**: the legal machinery of torture, the demonological theory of the learned, the social stress of a Europe wracked by plague, war, famine, and the religious terror of the Reformation age — and the ancient human reflex to find, in a frightened time, someone to blame.

## The end of the hunts

The trials did not stop because Europe stopped believing in the Devil. They stopped because **the machinery lost its legitimacy**: judges grew skeptical of confessions won by torture, courts demanded evidence that spectral flights and pacts could not provide, and voices of protest — most bravely the Jesuit **Friedrich Spee**, whose *Cautio Criminalis* (1631) exposed how torture manufactured guilt — slowly turned educated opinion. The rising **Enlightenment** finished the work. By the eighteenth century the last legal executions had ceased, and the witch had passed from the courtroom into folklore.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

The witch trials ran on a dark and revealing **symbolic** system — much of it projected onto the accused rather than found in them.

- The **Devil's mark** and the **witch's mark** were read on the body as encoded proof: any mole, scar, or insensitive patch could be "the Devil's seal," and searchers pricked the accused for a spot that would not bleed. The evidence was written on the flesh because it could be found nowhere else.
- The **confession** itself was an encoded, formulaic text: the sabbath, the pact, the flight, the demonic sex recur in trial after trial in the same shape — not because witches did these things, but because interrogators, working from the same demonological manuals, asked the same leading questions and tortured until they got the same script.
- The imagined sabbath was a systematic **inversion** of Christian symbol — a black anti-Mass, prayers said backward, the cross trampled, the world turned upside down — the projection of a society's fears about its own most sacred things.
- And the familiar **iconography** of the witch — the pointed hat, the broomstick, the cauldron and black cat — is very largely **later folklore and art**, not the reality of the trials, a reminder that even our picture of the persecution is a construction.

## Connections

The witch trials belong to the anxious, violent world of the **Reformation** that fills the neighboring chapters — an age when both churches were certain the Devil was loose. They inherited the machinery of the medieval **Inquisition** and the demonology of the schoolmen. Crucially, they are the historical root of a distinction this book draws carefully in the **modern** era: between **fabricated, accusatory "Satanism"** — the imaginary Devil-cult of the trials, and its descendants in later panics — and the real, self-identified Satanism of the twentieth century, which is an entirely different thing. And they are the (largely mythical) charter of modern **Wicca**, whose twentieth-century founders claimed descent from a surviving witch-religion — a claim examined, and doubted, in its own later chapter.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The witch trials themselves — their chronology (c. 1450–1750), their concentration in the Holy Roman Empire, and a death toll now soberly estimated at **40,000–60,000**; the *Malleus Maleficarum* and the demonological literature; the role of **torture** and inquisitorial procedure; the predominance of women among the victims; and the reality of village **cunning-folk** and folk magic.

**Not supported (as history).** That the accused were actually members of a **satanic witch-cult** — the once-popular "witch-cult" thesis of Margaret Murray is thoroughly discredited. That the **confessions**, extracted under torture, describe real events. And that **maleficium** — harmful magic — actually worked. The crime, as charged, was imaginary; the persecution was not.

**Genuinely open.** The precise **death tolls**, which are estimates from incomplete records and were once wildly exaggerated (the "nine million" sometimes claimed is pure myth). The relative weight of the many **causes**. And, in any given trial, how much a real tradition of folk healing or magic underlay the accusation, before torture buried it under fantasy.
