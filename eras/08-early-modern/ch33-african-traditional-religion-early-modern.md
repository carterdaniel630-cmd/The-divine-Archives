# Chapter 33 — African Traditional Religion: The Faiths Carried Across the Water

*Recently added — pending full review.*

The vast family of indigenous religions of sub-Saharan Africa — a Supreme God above a teeming world of intermediary spirits and living ancestors, read through divination and danced in masks — traced across its great traditions (Yoruba, Akan, Kongo, Dogon, San, and more), and the story of how, in the colonial and slave-trade centuries, these faiths were carried across the Atlantic in the memories of the enslaved and survived, hidden inside the saints of their captors.

## The seed that would not die

When a Yoruba man or woman was chained into the hold of a slave ship on the Bight of Benin in the seventeenth or eighteenth century, they were stripped of nearly everything — name, home, language, kin. But one thing could not be taken: what they carried in the mind. They remembered the **orishas**, the deities of thunder and river and iron; they remembered the wisdom of **Ifá** divination and the reverence owed the ancestors. On the far shore, forbidden their gods and driven toward the cross, they did something quietly astonishing: they matched their orishas to the Catholic **saints** and worshipped the old powers behind the new faces. Out of that concealment were born Santería, Candomblé, and Vodou — the subject of Chapter 40 — and the African gods crossed an ocean that was meant to erase them. This chapter is about the religions those people carried, at their African source.

## Not one religion, but hundreds

"African traditional religion" is a convenience, not a single thing. It names the indigenous religions of a continent — hundreds of distinct traditions, mostly **oral**, spread across thousands of languages — and the category itself is contested. The Kenyan theologian **John Mbiti**, whose *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969) was the first work to take these traditions seriously against the missionary verdict that they were "demonic," wrote that Africans are "notoriously religious," with no sharp line between sacred and secular life. Others (the Ugandan poet **Okot p'Bitek**) warned that even sympathetic scholars had distorted African gods by dressing them in Greek and Christian philosophical clothes — imposing a tidy monotheism on a far more various reality. And nearly everything we have was recorded by **outsiders** — colonizers, missionaries, anthropologists — who often despised or romanticized what they described. What follows traces the deep family resemblances while remembering how varied, and how imperfectly recorded, the reality was.

## A shared shape

Beneath the immense diversity, many sub-Saharan traditions share a recognizable structure. Above all stands a **Supreme Creator God** — **Olodumare** among the Yoruba, **Nyame** among the Akan, **Nzambi a Mpungu** in the Kongo, **Amma** among the Dogon, **Kwoth** among the Nuer — usually remote, having withdrawn from the daily running of the world after making it. Between that high God and humanity move a crowd of **lesser divinities and spirits** who are close and active: the Yoruba **orishas**, the Fon **vodun**, the powers of rivers, mountains, forests, and storms. Closest of all are the **ancestors** — the honored dead, who remain members of the family, watching, helping, and requiring remembrance. The living stay in right relation with all of them through **offering**, **festival**, and, above all, **divination** — the reading of the hidden will of the spirits through trained specialists. There is, characteristically, **no scripture**: the tradition lives in memory, proverb, song, mask, and the diviner's art.

## The great traditions

The **Yoruba** of what is now Nigeria and Benin built the most elaborate of these systems. Above stands **Olodumare** (Olorun), the remote creator and source of **àṣẹ**, the life-force that makes things happen; beneath him a vast pantheon of **orishas** — the arch-divinity **Obatala**, who molds human bodies; **Eshu** (Elegba), the trickster-messenger at the crossroads who must be greeted first (and whom missionaries wrongly branded the Devil); **Ogun** of iron and war; **Shango**, a deified historical king of Oyo, lord of thunder; the river-goddesses **Oshun** and **Yemoja**. Each person is born with an **ori**, an inner head or personal destiny, chosen before birth. The whole is read through **Ifá** divination, presided over by the wisdom-orisha **Orunmila** and the priest called the **babalawo**, who casts palm nuts or a divining chain to reach one of **256 figures** (the *odu*), each unlocking a memorized body of verse. The sacred city of **Ile-Ife** is held to be where the world itself began, lowered from heaven by **Oduduwa**. The ancestors return, masked and thunderous, in the **Egungun** masquerade.

The **Akan** of the Gold Coast (Ghana) honored **Nyame** the sky-god and **Asase Yaa** the earth, a host of lesser deities (*abosom*), and the revered ancestors (*Nananom Nsamanfo*). Their most famous sacred object is the **Golden Stool** (*Sika Dwa Kofi*) of the Asante, which — legend says — the priest **Okomfo Anokye** called down from the sky onto the lap of the first king Osei Tutu; it houses the very soul of the Asante nation, and no one, not even the king, may sit on it. The Akan encoded their proverbs in the **Adinkra** symbols and spoke across distances with the "talking drums."

The **Kongo** and wider **Bantu** peoples of Central Africa held a cosmology of two worlds — the living and the dead — divided by the water-line **Kalûnga** and mapped in the **Kongo cosmogram** (the *dikenga* or *yowa* cross), a diagram of the sun's four moments and the soul's cycle of birth, death, and return. Their spiritual power was concentrated in the **minkisi** — consecrated objects containing sacred medicine granted by Nzambi — of which the fierce, nail-studded **nkisi nkondi** are the most famous. These ideas would shape the diaspora (in Palo, Chapter 40) as deeply as the Yoruba's.

The **Dogon** of Mali worship **Amma**, the creator, and the amphibious **Nommo**, primordial beings of the deep — a cosmology famous for a modern controversy (below). The **San** ("Bushmen") of southern Africa carry perhaps the oldest continuous religious tradition on earth: a **trance-healing dance** in which shamans enter altered states to heal and to travel the spirit world, drawing on the potency of the sacred **eland** — the world David Lewis-Williams reads directly into the region's ancient **rock art** (Chapter 41). And among the Nilotic **Nuer** of the Sudan, the anthropologist E. E. Evans-Pritchard found in *Nuer Religion* (1956) a subtle theology of **Kwoth**, "Spirit," everywhere and in everything — the first study to grant a preliterate people's faith the seriousness given to any world religion.

## Ritual and the body

Where these religions are most themselves is in **ritual**, and ritual here is danced, drummed, and embodied. The defining act of worship across much of the family is **spirit possession**: through rhythmic drumming and dance a deity "mounts" a devotee, who becomes for a time the living presence of the god, speaking and moving as the orisha or vodun. **Initiation** binds a person to a particular deity for life; **sacrifice** and **offering** feed the spirits and the ancestors; **masks** and masquerade make the invisible powers visible in festival. And **divination** is everywhere — the Yoruba Ifá and its sixteen-cowrie shorthand, the throwing of bones by the southern African *sangoma*, the reading of a fowl's entrails — the trained specialist's art of hearing what the spirits will. This is a religion not primarily of belief stated in creeds but of **relationship** enacted with the body and the community.

## Witchcraft and the reading of misfortune

For many of these traditions, misfortune is never merely bad luck — it has a cause, and often that cause is **witchcraft**. The classic account is E. E. Evans-Pritchard's study of the **Azande** of Central Africa (*Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande*, 1937), still one of anthropology's most influential books. For the Azande, witchcraft (*mangu*) was thought to be a physical substance in the body that could harm others even without the witch's conscious intent; it explained not *how* a granary collapsed — termites did that — but *why* it fell on this person at this moment. To find the responsible witch the Azande consulted **oracles**, above all the **poison oracle** (*benge*): a substance was fed to a fowl and the bird's living or dying answered the question put to it. This was not superstition floating free but a coherent, self-checking system of explanation, and it named a real social role — the diviner who detects witchcraft and the specialist who counters it. It must be held with the same care as the neighboring witch-trials chapter (32): to describe a people's belief in witchcraft is to document what they held, not to endorse that anyone actually bewitched anyone — and, unlike Europe's hunts, African anti-witchcraft was usually aimed at *healing* the affliction rather than mass burning.

## The believer's lens

To the person inside the tradition, the world is dense with **presence and relationship**, and religion is not a separate compartment of life but its whole fabric. The high God is real but distant; what matters day to day is right dealing with the near powers — the orisha who owns your head, the ancestors at your shoulder, the spirit of the river you drink from. Misfortune is not random: illness, barrenness, a failed harvest are messages, and divination is the technology for reading them and setting things right through the proper offering. The ancestors are not "worshipped" as gods but honored as elders who have crossed over and still belong to the family — to forget them is the real impiety. Possession is the highest intimacy, the god arriving in person to counsel and heal the community. And personhood itself is communal — "I am because we are" — the self woven into a web of the living, the dead, and the unborn. Seen from inside, these are not "primitive superstitions" but comprehensive, coherent ways of keeping a community in balance with the seen and unseen worlds.

## The skeptical lens

The critical historian's first task is to strip away the **colonial lens** through which nearly all of this reached the wider world. The old labels — "**fetishism**," "**animism**," "**ancestor worship**," "**juju**" — were European verdicts that carried contempt and distortion; the ancestors are honored, not deified, and the "fetish" (*minkisi*) is a sophisticated theology of contained power, not idol-worship. The very idea of a single, systematic "African religion" is partly an artifact of scholars (Mbiti, Idowu) who, for good motives, may have over-systematized a diverse and local reality — the critique p'Bitek pressed. And one famous claim deserves a clear verdict: the assertion, popularized by Robert Temple's *The Sirius Mystery* (1976), that the **Dogon** possessed impossible ancient knowledge of the invisible companion star **Sirius B** (and so must have met visitors from the stars) does not survive scrutiny. The anthropologist **Walter van Beek**, restudying the Dogon, found no such knowledge among them, and concluded that the original account owed much to **Marcel Griaule's** own leading questions in the 1940s — a case study in how the observer can manufacture the very "tradition" he reports. The honest position is Evans-Pritchard's: take these faiths with full seriousness as religions, while remaining rigorous about how little of the pre-colonial reality the written record actually preserves.

## Across the water

The **transatlantic slave trade** — which tore an estimated **12.5 million** people from West and West-Central Africa between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries — became, against every intention, one of history's great engines of religious transmission. Forbidden their rites and pressed to convert, the enslaved practiced a **strategic syncretism**, hiding their orishas and vodun behind Catholic saints and folding African drumming and possession into the Church's calendar. From this concealment emerged the enduring **Afro-Atlantic religions** — Santería, Candomblé, Vodou, Palo, and the traditions of New Orleans — treated in full in Chapter 40. Meanwhile, on the continent, these traditions also met and partly fused with the expanding **Islam** of the Sahel and the **Christianity** of the coasts and the colonial missions, producing the African Independent Churches and a living, still-evolving religious landscape.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Africa's traditional religions are rich in **encoded** systems — and one of them is among the most remarkable in the world.

- **Ifá divination** is a genuine information system. The diviner reads the result of the cast as one of **256 figures** (the *odu*), each a distinct pattern of marks that is, in effect, an eight-position **binary** signature — and each of which unlocks a vast body of memorized verse. It is a sacred encoding of the cosmos, so significant that UNESCO named the Ifá corpus a masterpiece of the intangible heritage of humanity.
- The Akan **Adinkra** symbols are a visual language of **proverbs**: each stamped emblem — *Gye Nyame*, "except for God (I fear none)"; *Sankofa*, the backward-looking bird, "go back and fetch it" — encodes a maxim of wisdom and belief.
- The **Nsibidi** ideograms of the Cross River peoples formed an indigenous **script** of signs, some sacred and secret, known only to initiates of the Ekpe society.
- The Kongo **cosmogram** (*dikenga*) encodes the whole cosmos in a cross-in-a-circle: the horizon-line between the living and the dead, the vertical axis joining them, and the sun's daily round as the soul's journey — a diagram that would resurface, scratched on the ground, across the American South.
- In the diaspora these encodings became the **vèvè** of Vodou and the color-and-attribute codes of the orishas (Chapter 40).

## Connections

African traditional religion belongs, in its deep structures — a High God, spirits of place, and honored ancestors — to the oldest layer of human belief that the **Prehistory** chapters (41–42) reach toward, still living; the San trance-dance is a direct thread from the world of the painted caves. In the colonial centuries it collided with, resisted, and partly fused with the expanding **Christianity** and **Islam** of the age. And its transplantation across the Atlantic makes it the direct parent of the **diaspora religions** of the modern era (Chapter 40) — Santería, Candomblé, Vodou, and, further on, Rastafari — one of the deepest and least acknowledged roots of the religious life of the whole Americas.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The broad shared structures of sub-Saharan traditional religion (a high creator God, intermediary spirits, powerful ancestors, divination, and possession); the specific great systems (Yoruba orisha and Ifá, Akan and the Golden Stool, Fon vodun, Kongo cosmology and minkisi, Dogon, San trance-religion, Nuer Kwoth); the scale and route of the **transatlantic slave trade** (~12.5 million people); and the historical formation of the **Afro-Atlantic religions** through syncretism.

**Not supported — colonial distortions and a modern myth.** The old labels "**fetishism**," "**animism**," and "**ancestor worship**" as verdicts of contempt; the picture of a single, static "African religion"; and, specifically, the claim that the **Dogon** held impossible ancient astronomical knowledge of Sirius B — a modern legend that van Beek's fieldwork undercut and that likely originated in Griaule's own leading questions.

**Genuinely open.** Because these traditions were **oral** and are known largely through outside and often hostile observers, the pre-colonial detail of many is genuinely uncertain; reconstructing what was believed before the missionaries and anthropologists arrived — and how far the act of recording it reshaped it (the Griaule problem) — is a standing difficulty. The exact African origins of particular diaspora practices remain actively debated.
