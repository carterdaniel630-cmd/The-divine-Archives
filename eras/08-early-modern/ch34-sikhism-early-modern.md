# Chapter 34 — Sikhism: The Disciples of the One

*Recently added — pending full review.*

The faith born in the Punjab five centuries ago from the meeting of Hindu devotion and Muslim mysticism — a religion of one formless God, honest work, and radical human equality, shaped by ten Gurus, forged by martyrdom into a saint-soldier nation, and handed at last to a scripture that became a living master — told with care for its documented history and for the founding story it tells about itself.

## "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim"

One morning around 1499, near the town of Sultanpur in the Punjab, a man named **Nanak** went down to the river Bein to bathe and did not come back. His clothes were found on the bank; his family gave him up for drowned. Three days later he reappeared — and his first recorded words were: "**There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim.**" He did not mean that the two faiths were false, but that before the one God the labels dissolved: what mattered was not the community one was born into but the truth one lived. From that river Guru **Nanak** (1469–1539) emerged as the founder of a new religion — **Sikhism**, the way of the *sikhs*, the "disciples" of the one God.

## What Sikhism is

Sikhism is a **monotheistic** religion founded in the **Punjab** in the late fifteenth century, today the faith of some twenty-five to thirty million people and the fifth-largest in the world. Its center is devotion to a single, formless, creator God — named in the opening words of its scripture as **Ik Onkar**, "**One God**," and invoked as **Waheguru**, "Wondrous Lord" — approached not through ritual, image, priest, or pilgrimage but through three plain disciplines: **naam simran** (loving remembrance of the divine Name), **kirat karni** (earning an honest living), and **vand chakna** (sharing with others). It accepts **karma and rebirth** but teaches that release comes not by works alone but by God's **grace** (*nadar*), and that liberation — merging with the divine — can be reached **while living** (*jivanmukti*). It rejects, root and branch, the **caste system**, idol worship, ritualism, superstition, asceticism, and the domination of priests, and insists on the absolute **equality** of all human beings, women and men, of every birth.

## Guru Nanak and the first community

Born in 1469 near Lahore, Nanak spent decades on long journeys — the *udasis* — carrying his message across South Asia and beyond, accompanied by the Muslim musician Mardana who set his hymns to music. He settled at last at **Kartarpur** on the Ravi, where he founded the first Sikh community and built its enduring institutions: the **sangat**, the congregation in which all sat as equals, and the **langar**, the free communal kitchen where people of every caste and creed ate the same food seated in the same row on the same floor — a deliberate, daily assault on the barriers of caste and purity. His teaching was carried in **hymns**, sung, that fused the fervor of the **Bhakti** saints and the **Sufi** mystics into something new. Crucially, before he died Nanak passed his authority not to his sons but to a devoted disciple — establishing that the Guruship went to the worthiest, "the light passing from lamp to lamp."

## The ten Gurus

Nanak was the first of **ten Gurus**, each adding to the work of the last across two centuries. **Angad** shaped the **Gurmukhi** script for the community's tongue and collected Nanak's hymns. **Amar Das** entrenched the langar (making even the Mughal emperor Akbar eat there before an audience), advanced the standing of women, and opposed sati and the veil. **Ram Das** founded the city of **Amritsar** around its sacred pool. **Arjan** compiled the first scripture, the **Adi Granth** (1604), and built the **Harmandir Sahib**, the Golden Temple, with doors on four sides to welcome all castes and peoples — and in **1606**, under the emperor Jahangir, became the first Sikh **martyr**, tortured to death.

Persecution then transformed the community from a devotional movement into a militant one. **Hargobind** girded on two swords — the doctrine of **miri-piri**, temporal and spiritual authority held together — and raised the **Akal Takht**, the throne of temporal power, facing the Golden Temple. The ninth Guru, **Tegh Bahadur**, was beheaded in Delhi in **1675** on the order of the emperor Aurangzeb for defending the religious freedom of Kashmiri **Hindus** not even of his own faith — a martyrdom for another's liberty that Sikhs hold as a defining act. The tenth, **Gobind Singh**, whose own four sons were all killed in the wars with the Mughals, completed the line — and, before his death in **1708**, declared that there would be **no more human Gurus**: authority would pass to the scripture (the **Guru Granth**) and the community (the **Guru Panth**) together.

## The Guru Granth Sahib

That scripture, the **Guru Granth Sahib**, is unlike any other. Compiled by Guru Arjan (1604) and sealed by Guru Gobind Singh, written in **Gurmukhi** across **1,430** pages (*angs*), it is organized not by author or theme but by **raga** — thirty-one musical modes — because it is meant to be **sung**. It opens with the **Mul Mantar**, the root statement of the faith ("One God, whose name is Truth, the Creator, without fear, without hate…"), followed by Nanak's morning prayer, the **Japji Sahib**. And, remarkably, alongside the hymns of the Sikh Gurus it enshrines the **bhagat bani** — the verses of some fifteen Hindu and Muslim saints, including the Bhakti poet **Kabir**, the low-caste **Ravidas**, and the Sufi **Baba Farid**. It is perhaps the only major scripture that places the words of other faiths within its own canon. And it is treated not as a book but as a **living Guru**: enthroned under a canopy, waved with a whisk, ceremonially put to rest at night, and consulted as the present voice of the divine teacher. (A separate collection attributed to Guru Gobind Singh, the **Dasam Granth**, holds a distinct and sometimes debated place.)

## The Khalsa and the Five Ks

In **1699**, at Anandpur on the festival of Vaisakhi, Guru **Gobind Singh** called the community together and founded the **Khalsa**, the order of the "pure." He asked who would give their head for the faith; five who stepped forward became the **Panj Pyare**, the "Five Beloved," and were initiated with **amrit** — sweetened water stirred with a double-edged sword while the scriptures were recited — in a rite (*amrit sanchar*) that Sikhs still perform. Then the Guru knelt and was initiated by the five in turn, dissolving the last distance between master and disciple. Khalsa members take the names **Singh** ("lion") and **Kaur** ("princess"), abandoning caste surnames, and wear the **Five Ks** — *kesh* (uncut hair), *kangha* (a comb), *kara* (a steel bracelet), *kachera* (a cotton undergarment), and *kirpan* (a sword) — the visible uniform of a people who would never again be invisible or defenceless, bound by a code (*Rahit*) that forbids cutting the hair, intoxicants, adultery, and ritually slaughtered meat. In the Khalsa the Sikh ideal took its lasting form: the **sant-sipahi**, the saint-soldier, devoted to God and sworn to defend the oppressed of every faith.

## The inner path

Beneath the history lies a spiritual psychology. The human problem, Nanak taught, is **haumai** — the "I-am-ness," the self-centered ego that walls a person off from God and drives the endless cycle of rebirth. From haumai spring the **five thieves**: *kaam* (lust), *krodh* (anger), *lobh* (greed), *moh* (attachment), and *ahankar* (pride). The cure is to still the ego and align oneself with **hukam**, the divine order or will, through **naam simran** — the constant, loving repetition of God's Name — and through honest work and selfless service (*seva*). The Sikh who does so lives in **chardi kala**, a spirit of rising, resilient optimism, and may attain liberation here and now, the soul merging into God like a drop into the ocean. It is a path of the householder, not the hermit: God is found not by fleeing the world but by living rightly within it.

## From devotion to sovereignty

The Khalsa's militancy carried the Sikhs into history as a power. After Gobind Singh, **Banda Singh Bahadur** led a short-lived Sikh state before his execution; through the eighteenth century the Sikh **misls** (confederacies) fought Mughal and Afghan invaders; and in **1799** **Ranjit Singh** united them into the **Sikh Empire**, the "Lion of the Punjab," whose secular, tolerant rule from Lahore lasted until his death in 1839 and the British annexation of the Punjab in 1849. The reforming **Singh Sabha** movement of the late nineteenth century defined modern Sikh identity. The twentieth century brought trauma: the **1947 partition** of the Punjab uprooted millions of Sikhs, and in **1984** the Indian army's **Operation Blue Star** assault on the Golden Temple — against armed separatists demanding a state of Khalistan — killed hundreds and wounded the community deeply, followed by the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi by her Sikh bodyguards and the anti-Sikh massacres that followed. The saint-soldier's history has not been a quiet one.

## The believer's lens

To the Sikh, the faith is at once the most demanding and the most straightforward of paths: there is one God, present in all creation, and the whole of religion is to remember that God constantly, to earn one's bread honestly, and to share it. The Guru Granth Sahib is not a text about the divine teacher but the teacher himself, living and present, which is why one uncovers the head and removes the shoes before it and why it is put to bed at night. The langar — anyone, of any faith or none, fed for free, seated on the floor as an equal — is theology one can eat: the visible proof that caste, wealth, and rank mean nothing before God. To take amrit and wear the Five Ks is to make one's very body a public declaration of faith and a pledge to stand for justice. And the martyrdoms of Arjan and Tegh Bahadur, and the sacrifice of Gobind Singh's sons, are held not as tragedies but as the highest examples of *chardi kala* — faith that will not bow, joyful even in the face of death. Seen from inside, Sikhism is the pure worship of the One, stripped of every barrier that priests and castes had built between a person and their God.

## The skeptical lens

The historian's questions are gentler here than for many traditions, because so much of Sikh history is recent and well recorded — the Gurus' dates, the compilation of the scripture, the Khalsa, the empire, all rest on solid ground. Two areas remain genuinely open. First, the **life of Nanak** himself comes to us largely through the *janamsakhis*, devotional birth-narratives written down a century or more later and full of miracles — his three days in the river, his debates with wonder-workers — which historians read as pious legend around a historical core we can only partly recover. Second, and more sensitively, scholars debate how far Sikhism is a **synthesis** of the Sant/Bhakti and Sufi currents that surrounded Nanak — the formless God of Kabir, the devotion of the bhagats whose verses the scripture preserves — and how far it is a wholly **original revelation**, as the tradition holds. The academic "Sant synthesis" reading and the community's self-understanding do not fully agree, and the question touches identity, not just history. This book records the debate; it does not presume to settle a living faith's account of itself.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Sikhism carries its theology in a handful of potent signs.

- **Ik Onkar** (ੴ) — the opening glyph of the scripture, joining the numeral **one** to *Onkar*, the primal sound of God — is itself the whole creed in a single symbol: reality is **One**.
- The **Khanda**, the Sikh emblem, encodes the faith in steel: the central **double-edged sword** for the one truth and divine power, the **chakkar** (a ring) for the eternity and unity of God, and the two flanking **kirpans** of *miri* and *piri* — worldly and spiritual authority held in balance.
- The **Five Ks** are theology **worn on the body**: uncut hair as acceptance of God's will, the sword as the duty to justice — a faith one literally cannot take off. Over the gurdwara flies the **Nishan Sahib**, the triangular saffron flag bearing the Khanda.
- And at the heart of it all is the **Shabad**, the divine **Word**, and the **Naam**, the Name: to sing the scripture (*kirtan*) in its thirty-one ragas and repeat the Name is to encode God in sound — the Sikh member of the same family as the Sufi *dhikr*, the Bhakti *nama-japa*, and the mantra.

## Connections

Sikhism grew directly from the meeting this book has been tracing: the **nirguna Bhakti** of Kabir (Chapter 30) and the **Sufism** of medieval India (Chapter 27), whose shared love of a formless God beyond Hindu and Muslim became, in Nanak, a wholly new faith — and whose poets Sikhism honored by placing their verses in its own scripture. It rose in the same century as the **Reformation** (Chapter 31) half a world away, and rhymes with it uncannily: a vernacular scripture, a rejected priesthood, a return to the one God over ritual and hierarchy. From a devotional movement it was hammered by Mughal persecution into a distinct and martial nation of faith, and it stands today as one of the world's great living religions — the youngest of the major traditions born before the modern age.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The historical Gurus and their dates, from Nanak (1469–1539) to Gobind Singh (d. 1708); the founding of Kartarpur, Amritsar, and the langar; the compilation of the **Guru Granth Sahib** (1604) and its inclusion of non-Sikh saints; the **martyrdoms** of Arjan (1606) and Tegh Bahadur (1675); the founding of the **Khalsa** (1699); and the later history — Ranjit Singh's empire (1799–1839), the Singh Sabha, and the events of 1947 and 1984. These rest on solid historical record.

**Not supported (as history).** Nanak's **revelation** and the miracle-tales of the **janamsakhi** narratives — the three days in the river, the wonders of his travels — are matters of **faith and devotional legend**, not documented history. The theological claims of the tradition are recorded here as belief, not adjudicated.

**Genuinely open — and handled with care.** The precise **biography** of Guru Nanak is uncertain, since the janamsakhis are late and devotional. And there is a real, sensitive scholarly discussion about how far Sikhism is a **synthesis** of the Sant/Bhakti and Sufi currents around it and how far a wholly **original revelation** — a question on which the tradition's self-understanding and academic history do not always agree. This chapter records the debate; it does not settle it.
