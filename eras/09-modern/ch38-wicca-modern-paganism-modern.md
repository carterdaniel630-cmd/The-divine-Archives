# Chapter 38 — Wicca and Modern Paganism: The Old Gods Return

The twentieth-century rebirth of Pagan religion in the West — a nature faith of Goddess and God, coven and sabbat, magic and moon — told honestly as what it is: not the ancient survival its founders claimed, but a real, living, modern religion assembled in living memory, and none the less genuine for it.

## The man who said the witches were real

In 1951, England repealed the last of its **Witchcraft Acts**, and within a few years a retired British civil servant named **Gerald Gardner** stepped forward with an astonishing claim. He said that witchcraft had never died — that a secret, ancient, pre-Christian **religion of the Goddess** had survived in hidden covens through all the centuries of persecution, and that he had been **initiated** into one, the New Forest coven, in 1939. Through his books and his eager courting of the press, Gardner launched **Wicca** upon the world.

Almost none of the historical claim was true. There was no unbroken ancient witch-religion; the covens Gardner described cannot be traced before him; and the theory he leaned on — Margaret Murray's idea of a surviving pan-European witch-cult — is, as the witch-trials chapter showed, thoroughly **discredited**. And yet Wicca is entirely real: a **new religion**, sincerely believed and now practiced by hundreds of thousands, assembled in the twentieth century from older parts. Both halves of that sentence are the honest story.

## What Wicca is

**Wicca** is a modern, initiatory, nature-centered **Pagan** religion. Most Wiccans worship a duality of the divine — a **Goddess**, often the **Triple Goddess** of maiden, mother, and crone, linked to the moon; and a **Horned God** of the wild, the hunt, and the turning cycle of life, death, and rebirth, linked to the sun. Divinity is felt as **immanent** in the natural world rather than ruling from outside it. Wiccans practice **magic** — understood as the focusing of will and intention — work in small groups called **covens** (traditionally led by a High Priestess) or as solitaries, and hold to a simple ethic, the **Wiccan Rede**: "**An it harm none, do what ye will**," often paired with the belief that what one sends out returns threefold.

## The Wheel and the circle

Wiccan worship turns on two cycles. The **Wheel of the Year** marks eight **sabbats** — the solstices and equinoxes and the four cross-quarter days between them, bearing names drawn from Celtic and Germanic tradition: **Samhain**, **Yule**, **Imbolc**, **Ostara**, **Beltane**, **Litha**, **Lughnasadh**, and **Mabon**. Between them fall the **esbats**, the full-moon gatherings. Rites are held within a ritual **circle** cast to create sacred space, using tools that stand for the four elements — the **athame** (ritual knife), the chalice, the wand, and the pentacle — and, in the drama called **drawing down the moon**, the Goddess is invoked to speak through the High Priestess herself.

## The honest origins

Where did Wicca actually come from? Modern scholarship (above all Ronald Hutton's) has shown it to be a **twentieth-century creation**, woven by Gardner and his circle from several real strands: the **ceremonial magic** of the Golden Dawn and Aleister Crowley from the previous chapter (much of Wicca's ritual language is theirs); nineteenth-century **Romanticism** and its love of a lost, natural, pagan past; genuine surviving **folk magic** and seasonal custom; **Freemasonry's** initiatory structure; and Murray's mistaken but inspiring theory of the witch-cult. The result was something new. To say so is not to debunk Wicca but to describe it truthfully — exactly as this book describes the medieval dating of the Zohar or the thirteenth-century authorship of a text that claims to be ancient. **A religion need not be old to be real.**

## Valiente and the shaping of the Craft

Gardner launched Wicca, but he did not shape it alone — and its most important early liturgist was a woman. **Doreen Valiente**, initiated by Gardner in 1953 and for a time his High Priestess, is often called "the mother of modern witchcraft." Finding Gardner's rituals thick with borrowed lines from **Aleister Crowley**, she largely **rewrote the Book of Shadows**, stripping out the Crowleyisms and giving the tradition much of the graceful liturgy it still uses — above all the **Charge of the Goddess** (c. 1957), the central devotional text spoken in Wiccan rites, with its promise that "all acts of love and pleasure are my rituals." From Gardner's Gardnerian line others branched: **Alex Sanders**, the self-styled "King of the Witches," founded **Alexandrian Wicca** in the 1960s, weaving in more ceremonial and Kabbalistic magic; and **Raymond Buckland**, a Gardnerian initiate, carried the religion to the **United States**, where it flowered into a hundred forms. What Gardner began as a small English coven became, within a generation, an international religion.

## The wider Pagan revival

Wicca is the largest current in a broad modern **Pagan** revival that has grown explosively since the 1970s. **Druidry**, revived from the eighteenth century (partly on the forged "ancient" texts of Iolo Morganwg), organizes modern orders around the old Celtic priesthood. **Heathenry** or **Ásatrú** reconstructs the **Norse paganism** of Chapter 23 from the Eddas and the sagas. The feminist **Goddess movement** and Dianic witchcraft (Starhawk, Z. Budapest) made the Craft a vehicle of women's spirituality. And **reconstructionists** work to rebuild the Hellenic, Egyptian, and other ancient religions from the sources, while countless **eclectic** and solitary practitioners assemble their own paths. Together they are among the fastest-growing religious currents in the modern West, and deeply entwined with **environmental** spirituality.

## From coven to courtroom

A religion assembled in the twentieth century has had to win, in the twentieth and twenty-first, the ordinary legal standing older faiths take for granted — and Wicca has. The landmark came over a grave. When **Sgt. Patrick Stewart**, a Wiccan soldier, was killed in Afghanistan in 2005, the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs refused to place the **pentacle** on his memorial marker, though it maintained a list of dozens of approved religious emblems. His widow **Roberta Stewart**, the Wiccan church **Circle Sanctuary**, and others sued; and in **2007**, in *Circle Sanctuary v. Nicholson*, the government settled and added the pentacle to the emblems permitted on military headstones. It now appears at **Arlington** beside the cross, the Star of David, and the crescent. Wicca and Paganism are today recognized in the U.S. military's own handbook of faiths and served, however unevenly, by chaplaincy — the plainest possible marker that a religion born in living memory has become, in law and in fact, simply a religion.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Modern Paganism is rich in deliberately chosen and reconstructed **symbols**.

- The **pentagram** — the five-pointed star, usually within a circle as the **pentacle** — is Wicca's central emblem: its five points encode the four elements (earth, air, fire, water) crowned by the fifth, spirit, all bound in the circle of unity.
- The **Book of Shadows** is the coven's encoded sacred text — a hand-copied book of rituals, spells, and lore, traditionally passed only from initiator to initiate, each line a piece of a transmitted, semi-secret liturgy.
- The **Triple Moon** (waxing crescent, full circle, waning crescent) encodes the Goddess in her three aspects; the ritual **tools** encode the elements; and the cast **circle** encodes the making of sacred space out of ordinary ground.
- Honesty requires noting that the eight-fold **Wheel of the Year** is itself a **modern synthesis** — no single ancient people kept exactly these eight festivals under these names — a reconstructed calendar that has nonetheless become genuinely sacred to those who keep it.

## Connections

Wicca and modern Paganism grow directly out of the **occult revival** of the previous chapter — the Golden Dawn's ceremonial magic is their immediate parent — and out of the **Romantic** longing for the pre-Christian past. They take their (mistaken) historical charter from the **witch trials** of Chapter 32, and their reconstructed gods from the **Norse** and **Celtic and Germanic** paganisms of earlier eras. As a self-consciously new, eclectic, individualist faith they are a classic **new religious movement**, kin to the others of this age — and, in their reverence for the living earth, one of the most distinctly modern of all.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** Wicca as a documented **twentieth-century** religion — Gerald Gardner, the 1950s launch, the beliefs, rituals, and rapid growth; the broader modern Pagan revival (Druidry, Heathenry, the Goddess movement, reconstructionism) and its real and expanding presence. The roles of **Doreen Valiente** (the rewritten Book of Shadows and the Charge of the Goddess), **Alex Sanders** (Alexandrian Wicca), and **Raymond Buckland** (Wicca's spread to America); and Wicca's **legal recognition** — the 2007 *Circle Sanctuary v. Nicholson* settlement placing the pentacle on U.S. military headstones — are well documented.

**Not supported (as history).** That Wicca is an **unbroken survival** of an ancient pre-Christian witch-religion — it is not; Gardner's ancient lineage cannot be traced, and the **Murray witch-cult thesis** it rests on is discredited. The "**burning times**" claim that nine million witches of this surviving religion were martyred is, as the witch-trials chapter showed, a **myth**.

**Genuinely open — and a point of principle.** Gardner's exact **sources**, and whether any pre-Gardnerian coven existed, remain debated; and real fragments of old **folk custom** do survive within a new religious frame. But the central point is one of principle, not doubt: that Wicca is a **modern construction** takes nothing from its reality as a **living, sincerely practiced religion**. This book distinguishes a tradition's true history from its self-understanding for every faith it treats, ancient or new, and applies the same courtesy and the same honesty here.
