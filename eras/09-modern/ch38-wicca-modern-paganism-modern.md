# Chapter 38 — Wicca and Modern Paganism: The Old Gods Return

*Recently added — pending full review.*

The twentieth-century rebirth of Pagan religion in the West — a nature faith of Goddess and God, coven and sabbat, magic and moon — told honestly as what it is: not the ancient survival its founders claimed, but a real, living, modern religion assembled in living memory, and none the less genuine for it.

## The man who said the witches were real

In 1951, England repealed the last of its **Witchcraft Acts**, and within a few years a retired British colonial civil servant and amateur folklorist named **Gerald Gardner** stepped forward with an astonishing claim. He said that witchcraft had never died — that a secret, ancient, pre-Christian **religion of the Goddess** had survived in hidden covens through all the centuries of persecution, and that he had himself been **initiated** into one, the New Forest coven, in 1939. He had trailed the idea first in a novel, *High Magic's Aid* (1949), then stated it outright in *Witchcraft Today* (1954) — carrying a preface by the Egyptologist **Margaret Murray**, whose theory of a surviving witch-cult it leaned on — and *The Meaning of Witchcraft* (1959). Through his books, his television appearances, and a witchcraft museum on the Isle of Man, Gardner launched **Wicca** upon the world.

Almost none of the historical claim was true. There was no unbroken ancient witch-religion; the covens Gardner described cannot be traced before him; and Murray's pan-European witch-cult, as the witch-trials chapter (32) showed, is thoroughly **discredited**. And yet Wicca is entirely real: a **new religion**, sincerely believed and now practiced by hundreds of thousands, assembled in the twentieth century from older parts. Both halves of that sentence are the honest story.

## What Wicca is

**Wicca** is a modern, initiatory, nature-centered **Pagan** religion. Most Wiccans worship a duality of the divine — a **Goddess**, often the **Triple Goddess** of maiden, mother, and crone, linked to the moon; and a **Horned God** of the wild, the hunt, and the turning cycle of life, death, and rebirth, linked to the sun. Divinity is felt as **immanent** in the natural world rather than ruling it from outside. Wiccans practice **magic** — understood as the focusing of will and intention — work in small groups called **covens** (traditionally led by a **High Priestess**) or as solitaries, and hold to a simple ethic, the **Wiccan Rede**: "**An it harm none, do what ye will**," often paired with the **Law of Threefold Return**, the belief that what one sends out comes back multiplied.

## Gardner, Valiente, and the making of a liturgy

Gardner did not work alone, and the religion's literary soul was largely the work of a woman he initiated in 1953: **Doreen Valiente**. Becoming his High Priestess, she found his ritual book — the **Book of Shadows** — heavily borrowed from **Aleister Crowley** (Chapter 37) and other modern sources, and from 1954 she systematically **rewrote it**, stripping out much of the Crowley material and replacing it with luminous verse of her own. Her rewritten **Charge of the Goddess** — itself reworked from Crowley and from Charles Leland's *Aradia* — became Wicca's central devotional text, and her poetry gave the Craft the nature-mystical voice by which it is now known. The familiar rhyming couplet of the Rede ("An it harm none…") was popularized only in the 1960s and '70s. The liturgy that feels timeless was, in fact, written down — much of it — in the 1950s by two people whose names we know.

## The lineages

From Gardner's covens the Craft branched into recognizable traditions. **Gardnerian** Wicca, the original line, initiates through **three degrees** and passes its Book of Shadows only from initiator to initiate. In the 1960s **Alex Sanders** and his wife **Maxine** founded the very similar **Alexandrian** tradition (Sanders, styling himself "King of the Witches," had in fact trained in a Gardnerian coven). Together these form what is called **British Traditional Wicca**. **Raymond Buckland** carried the Gardnerian line to the United States around 1964, from which American Wicca exploded outward into countless eclectic and solitary forms — most modern Wiccans today belong to no formal coven at all, having learned the Craft from published books.

## The wider Pagan revival

Wicca is the largest current in a broad modern **Pagan** revival that has grown explosively since the 1970s.

The feminist **Goddess movement** turned the Craft into a vehicle of women's spirituality: **Zsuzsanna Budapest** founded the women-only **Dianic** tradition with her Susan B. Anthony Coven No. 1 in 1971, and **Starhawk**'s *The Spiral Dance* (1979) — and the **Reclaiming** tradition she helped build in San Francisco — married witchcraft to feminism and political activism for a new generation. **Druidry**, revived from the eighteenth century (partly on the forged "ancient" Welsh texts of **Iolo Morganwg**, and organized in modern orders such as **OBOD**), gathers around the idealized Celtic priesthood. **Heathenry** or **Ásatrú** reconstructs the **Norse paganism** of Chapter 23 from the Eddas and sagas — formally founded in Iceland in **1972** under the poet-farmer Sveinbjörn Beinteinsson, and since split between universalist and exclusionary "folkish" wings. And **reconstructionists** work to rebuild the Hellenic, Kemetic (Egyptian), and other ancient religions from the sources. Together these are among the fastest-growing religious currents in the modern West, deeply entwined with **environmental** spirituality — and increasingly recognized in law: the United States added the Wiccan **pentacle** to its approved emblems for veterans' headstones in **2007**, after a lawsuit.

## The believer's lens

To the Wiccan, the question of ancient lineage misses the point that matters. The gods are experienced as **real and present** — in the turning seasons, the phases of the moon, the body, the living land — and the ritual works whether or not a coven met in 1400. The Craft offers what its practitioners feel the mainstream faiths withheld: a **divine feminine** restored to the center of worship; a **sacralized natural world** rather than one to be subdued; a hands-on, participatory magic in which every member is priest or priestess, not a passive congregant; and an ethic of personal responsibility ("harm none") free of sin and hell. For the initiate, standing in the cast circle as the Goddess is "drawn down" into the High Priestess, the encounter is not theater but genuine sacred presence. Wicca understands itself less as a claim about the past than as a **reverence for the living earth and the cycle of life** — and by that measure its practitioners find it wholly real, whatever the historians conclude about Gerald Gardner.

## The skeptical lens

The historical charter, however, does not survive scrutiny — and the definitive demolition came from a sympathetic historian, **Ronald Hutton**, whose *The Triumph of the Moon* (1999) traced modern pagan witchcraft to its actual, documentable roots. Wicca was **assembled in the twentieth century** from identifiable strands: the ceremonial magic of the **Golden Dawn** and **Crowley** (much of the early ritual language is theirs); the Romantic and literary cult of a lost pagan past — Leland's *Aradia* (1899), Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, Margaret Murray's *Witch-Cult in Western Europe* (1921), Robert Graves's *The White Goddess* (1948); the initiatory structure of **Freemasonry**; genuine surviving fragments of folk custom; and Gardner's own considerable invention. The New Forest coven into which he claimed initiation cannot be independently confirmed to have existed before him.

Two specific myths must be named as myths. The idea that Wicca is the **unbroken survival** of an ancient religion is false. And the "**burning times**" claim — that nine million practitioners of this surviving Goddess-religion were martyred in the witch trials — is, as Chapter 32 shows, both wildly inflated in number and wrong in kind: the victims of the trials were overwhelmingly Christians accused of an imaginary crime, not members of a secret pagan faith. To state all this is not to debunk Wicca but to describe it truthfully — exactly as this book dates the Zohar to the thirteenth century or the tarot's "Egyptian" pedigree to the eighteenth. **A religion need not be old to be real.**

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Modern Paganism is rich in deliberately chosen and reconstructed **symbols**.

- The **pentagram** — the five-pointed star, usually within a circle as the **pentacle** — is Wicca's central emblem: its five points encode the four classical elements (earth, air, fire, water) crowned by the fifth, **spirit**, all bound in the circle of unity. (Inverted, the same star is read very differently in the Satanism of Chapter 39 — the identical sign, opposite meanings.)
- The **Book of Shadows** is the coven's encoded sacred text — a hand-copied book of rituals, spells, and lore, traditionally passed only from initiator to initiate, each line a piece of a transmitted, semi-secret liturgy. That the "immemorial" book was in fact largely composed by Gardner and Valiente in the 1950s is the honesty this book requires.
- The **Triple Moon** (waxing crescent, full circle, waning crescent) encodes the Goddess in her three aspects; the ritual **tools** — the **athame** (black-handled knife), chalice, wand, and pentacle — encode the four elements; and the cast **circle** encodes the making of sacred space out of ordinary ground.
- The eight-fold **Wheel of the Year** is itself a **modern synthesis**: no single ancient people kept exactly these eight festivals under these names (**Samhain, Yule, Imbolc, Ostara, Beltane, Litha, Lughnasadh, Mabon** — the last a name coined only in the 1970s). It is a reconstructed calendar that has become genuinely sacred to those who keep it.

## Connections

Wicca and modern Paganism grow directly out of the **occult revival** of Chapter 37 — the Golden Dawn's ceremonial magic is their immediate parent, and Gardner knew Crowley and held an O.T.O. charter — and out of the **Romantic** longing for the pre-Christian past. They take their (mistaken) historical charter from the **witch trials** of Chapter 32, and their reconstructed gods from the **Norse** (23) and **Celtic and Germanic** (14) paganisms of earlier eras. As a self-consciously new, eclectic, individualist faith they are a classic **new religious movement** (Chapter 35), kin to the others of this age — and, in their reverence for the living earth, one of the most distinctly modern of all.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** Wicca as a documented **twentieth-century** religion — Gerald Gardner, the 1949–59 books, Doreen Valiente's authorship of much of the liturgy, the beliefs and rituals, the Gardnerian and Alexandrian lineages, and the rapid global growth; and the broader modern Pagan revival (Dianic and feminist witchcraft, Starhawk's Reclaiming, Druidry, Heathenry/Ásatrú, reconstructionism), its expanding presence, and its legal recognition (the 2007 VA pentacle).

**Not supported (as history).** That Wicca is an **unbroken survival** of an ancient pre-Christian witch-religion — Gardner's ancient lineage cannot be traced, and the **Murray witch-cult thesis** it rests on is discredited (Hutton, *The Triumph of the Moon*). The "**burning times**" claim of nine million martyred witches of a surviving pagan religion is a **myth**, on both count and character.

**Genuinely open — and a point of principle.** Gardner's exact **sources**, and whether any pre-Gardnerian coven existed, remain debated; and real fragments of old **folk custom** do survive within the new frame. But the central point is one of principle, not doubt: that Wicca is a **modern construction** takes nothing from its reality as a **living, sincerely practiced religion**. This book distinguishes a tradition's true history from its self-understanding for every faith it treats, ancient or new, and extends the same honesty and the same courtesy here.
