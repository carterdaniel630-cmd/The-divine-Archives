# Chapter 36 — Spiritualism: The Age of the Séance

The religion of the talking dead — born from two girls and a knocking sound in 1848, swept through a grieving Victorian world of darkened parlors and rapping tables, and honest about the difference between the consolation it gave, which was real, and the communication it claimed, which was not.

## The rappings at Hydesville

In March 1848, in a small farmhouse at **Hydesville**, New York, two sisters — **Kate Fox**, aged eleven or twelve, and **Margaret**, a little older — announced that they were hearing mysterious **rapping** sounds, and that the raps answered their questions: one knock for no, two for yes, and, with a run through the alphabet, whole sentences. The spirit, they said, was a murdered peddler buried in the cellar. The news spread like fire. Within a few years the Fox sisters were famous, public mediums, and a new religion had been born — **Spiritualism**, built on a single electrifying claim: that the dead are not gone, and that the living can talk to them.

Forty years later, in 1888, Margaret Fox stood on a New York stage and confessed that it had all been a trick: the raps were made by cracking the joints of her toes and knuckles, a childish prank that had grown into a worldwide movement. She later recanted the confession. But the shadow it cast is the honest frame for this whole chapter.

## What Spiritualism is

**Spiritualism** is the belief — and, for many, the religion — that the human personality **survives death** and that the living can **communicate** with the surviving spirits, most often through a specially gifted intermediary called a **medium**. It is both a practice, centered on the **séance**, and an organized faith, with **Spiritualist churches** that continue today. Its promise was intimate and immense: not a distant heaven argued over by theologians, but your own mother, your own dead child, speaking to you across the dark of a parlor room.

## The forerunners

The raps at Hydesville fell on ground long prepared. Two eighteenth-century figures had already built the world in which talking to spirits could seem plausible. The Swedish scientist and visionary **Emanuel Swedenborg** (1688–1772) — sometimes called "the father of Spiritualism" — claimed that in trance his spirit walked the tiers of the afterlife and conversed with angels and the dead, and mapped a detailed spirit-world (a "Summerland" of continued moral progress) that Spiritualism would inherit whole. The German physician **Franz Anton Mesmer** (1734–1815) supplied the technique: his "**animal magnetism**" (mesmerism) put subjects into a trance in which, practitioners found, some spoke with uncanny knowledge and reported visions of spirits — the entranced medium before the word existed. In America the two streams met in **Andrew Jackson Davis** (1826–1910), the "Poughkeepsie Seer," who fused Swedenborg's cosmology and Mesmer's trance into a homegrown spiritual philosophy — and who, on the very day in 1848 that the Fox sisters heard their first raps, recorded feeling a spirit tell him that "the good work has begun." Spiritualism did not appear from nowhere; it was the popular flowering of a seed a century old.

## The séance and its wonders

Spiritualism's rites unfolded in the **séance circle** — a group seated around a table in low light, hands joined, a medium in their midst. Through the medium the spirits were said to act: **rapping** out answers, **tipping and levitating** the table, guiding a hand in **automatic writing**, speaking in a trance, and, in the boldest séances, producing **materializations** — ghostly forms, floating instruments, ectoplasm. The age's new technologies were pressed into service: **spirit photography** claimed to catch the dead on the new film, and the **planchette** and **talking board** — the Ouija board — spelled out messages letter by letter.

## Why the Victorians believed

Spiritualism swept the United States and Britain in the second half of the nineteenth century for reasons both deep and specific. It rode a tide of **grief**: the mass death of the American **Civil War** and, later, the **First World War** and the influenza pandemic left millions desperate to reach the dead, and Spiritualism offered them contact. It gave **women** — who dominated its ranks as mediums — a rare position of public spiritual authority in an age that allowed them little, and it ran alongside the era's reform movements, from abolition to women's suffrage. And it claimed to be not superstition but **science**: a demonstrable, experimental contact with the beyond, fit for a confident scientific century. It won famous converts, among them the chemist **Sir William Crookes** and Sir Arthur Conan **Doyle**, the creator of the supremely rational Sherlock Holmes.

## Science, fraud, and the reckoning

Because Spiritualism claimed to be testable, it was tested — and the results were damning. Investigators and stage magicians, above all **Harry Houdini**, exposed medium after medium in outright fraud: hidden accomplices, trick tables, cheesecloth "ectoplasm," reused spirit photographs. The **Society for Psychical Research**, founded in 1882 to study the claims rigorously, documented deception again and again and never established a single verified case of communication with the dead. And at the movement's very origin stood Margaret Fox's confession of the cracking toes. None of this stopped Spiritualism — grief is stronger than debunking — but it settled the empirical question.

## Kardec and the spirits of Brazil

While Anglo-American Spiritualism was being exposed in its séance rooms, a parallel movement took a very different and more durable path. In France the educator Hippolyte Rivail, writing as **Allan Kardec**, gathered the messages of many mediums into a systematic doctrine and published **The Spirits' Book** (1857), the founding text of **Spiritism** (*Espiritismo*). Kardec deliberately distinguished his creed from Spiritualism by one decisive doctrine: **reincarnation**. Where most Anglo-American Spiritualists imagined a single afterlife, Spiritists hold that the soul returns through many lives, each an opportunity to learn and to advance morally toward perfection — a fusion of séance-contact with a law of progress and near-karmic justice. Carried to **Brazil**, Spiritism became something Kardec never saw: a mass religion. There the medium **Chico Xavier** (1910–2002) produced, by "**psychography**" (spirit-guided automatic writing), more than four hundred books and, through television, made Spiritism a household faith; Spiritist **charitable hospitals** and centers spread across the country. Today Brazil has the largest Spiritist population on earth — millions of adherents, the third-largest religious group in the nation — making this quiet French offshoot of the séance one of the most successful of all the new religions of the modern age.

## Symbology and sacred encoding

Spiritualism was, distinctively, a religion of **codes and channels** — and it took its model from the technology of its age.

- The **rap** was a literal binary code: yes and no, and the laborious spelling-out of messages letter by letter through the alphabet — the dead reduced to Morse.
- The **talking board** (Ouija) and its **planchette** encode the séance's central act: a message assembled letter by letter as the sitters' hands drift across the board, its motion (as science shows) produced unconsciously by the sitters themselves — the **ideomotor effect** — though experienced as the touch of the dead.
- The whole movement arose as the twin of the **telegraph**: Spiritualism was imagined as a "**spiritual telegraph**," communication-at-a-distance with the other world, in the very decade the electric telegraph made communication-at-a-distance ordinary. The medium was the operator; the séance, the receiving station.

## Connections

Spiritualism is a modern, scientific-age form of one of the most ancient human practices — the **consultation of the dead**, the necromancy and ancestor-contact found from Mesopotamia to the African traditions of the neighboring chapters. It fed directly into the two movements beside it: **Theosophy**, which built a whole cosmology on communication with hidden Masters, and the broader **occult revival**, and onward into the twentieth-century **New Age** and the paranormal. It shaped early **psychology**, too: the study of mediums, trance, and automatic writing fed directly into the exploration of the **unconscious mind**. Its churches survive, and the séance never entirely ended.

## The evidence, honestly

**Well-supported by evidence.** The history of Spiritualism — its birth with the Fox sisters in 1848, its explosive spread, its practices, its churches, and its enormous social role in a grieving age; the prominence of women; the famous believers; and the extensive, documented record of **fraud**, including Margaret Fox's own confession. The **forerunners** (Swedenborg, Mesmer, Andrew Jackson Davis) and the rise of **Kardecist Spiritism** — Allan Kardec's *The Spirits' Book* (1857), its reincarnationist doctrine, and its growth into a mass religion in **Brazil** (Chico Xavier, the Spiritist centers) — are firmly documented.

**Not supported (as history or science).** That mediums actually **communicated with the dead**. Every claim that was rigorously tested was either exposed as fraud or explained by ordinary means (the ideomotor effect, cold reading, stagecraft); more than a century of psychical research produced **no verified case**. The phenomena, as tested, were not real.

**Genuinely open — and worth respecting.** The **consolation** Spiritualism gave the bereaved was entirely real as human experience, whatever its source; the **sincerity** of many mediums and believers is not in doubt; and Spiritualism's genuine, lasting influence on **psychology, women's public life, and the study of the mind** is a real historical legacy. That the mechanism was never verified does not make the grief, or the comfort, less real.
