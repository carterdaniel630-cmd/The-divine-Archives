    <p class="lead">Sixty lines of cuneiform on a piece of clay the size of a mobile phone — 11.5 by 6 centimetres — written in Babylonia some 3,700 years ago and carried home from the Middle East in an RAF man's luggage after the Second World War. It is the only ancient tablet that does not merely tell the flood story but stops to explain, in a builder's quantities, <strong>how to construct the boat</strong>: so much palm-fibre rope, so many wooden ribs, so many bathfuls of hot bitumen. And the boat it specifies is not the oblong ark of a thousand Bible illustrations. It is round — a giant <strong>coracle</strong>, the reed river-boat of Mesopotamia scaled up to the size of two-thirds of a football pitch. In 2009 the tablet's owner brought it to a British Museum identification day, and the curator who picked it up, <strong>Irving Finkel</strong>, has called it "one of the most important human documents ever discovered."</p>

    <h2 id="study">Study the object</h2>
    <p>The Ark Tablet is privately owned, so — unlike most objects in this Vault — the British Museum publishes no collection record or scan of it, and there is no institutional viewer to link. What can be studied openly is Finkel's published edition: the tablet is photographed and translated in full in <em>The Ark Before Noah</em> (2014), and the summary accounts below reproduce his translation and measurements. That is stated plainly here because it is the honest position: for this one object, the archive works from the scholar's published edition, not from the institution's open record.</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Irving Finkel, <em>The Ark Before Noah: Decoding the Story of the Flood</em> (Hodder &amp; Stoughton, 2014) — the tablet's text and translation at pp. 107–110.</li>
      <li>"The Ark Tablet," History of Information (provenance, dimensions, and Finkel's British Museum blog summary of 23 January 2014) — <a href="https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=4060">historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=4060</a></li>
    </ul>

    <h2 id="souvenir">A souvenir from the war</h2>
    <p><strong>Leonard Simmonds</strong> served with the Royal Air Force in the Middle East from 1945 to 1948 and came home with a small collection of antiquities — tablets, pottery, seals — picked up along the way. He showed them to experts over the years; most of the pieces were judged commonplace, and the little brown tablet among them attracted no particular attention. It was his son, <strong>Douglas Simmonds</strong>, who inherited the collection and, in 2009, brought the tablet to one of the British Museum's public identification days and put it in front of Irving Finkel, the museum's curator of cuneiform — one of the few people alive who can sight-read Babylonian the way the rest of us read a newspaper. Finkel has described knowing at first glance that this was no ordinary tablet. It took him years of work around his other duties to publish it, and when he did, in January 2014, the tablet went on public display at the museum on loan from its owner — placed, pointedly, beside the Babylonian Map of the World, whose reverse text speaks of a distant island where the remains of the ark were said to rest. The newest flood document in the world, next to the oldest map that mentions one.</p>

    <h2 id="what-it-says">What the tablet says</h2>
    <p>The text is a speech. The god <strong>Enki</strong> (Ea in Akkadian), having resolved to spare one man, addresses himself — exactly as in the epic — to the wall of the man's reed house, and the man, <strong>Atra-hasis</strong>, "exceedingly wise," overhears his instructions. In Finkel's translation the god begins: <em>"Wall, wall! Reed wall, reed wall! Atra-hasis, pay heed to my advice, that you may live for ever! Destroy your house, build a boat; despise possessions, and save life!"</em> Then the poetry stops and the specification begins.</p>
    <p><strong>The shape.</strong> <em>"Draw out the boat that you will build with a circular design; let its length and breadth be the same."</em> The vessel is a circle — the form of the <em>gufa</em>, the round coracle of woven reeds and bitumen that carried people, produce and livestock across the Tigris and Euphrates in the ancient world and was still doing so in Iraq into living memory. The tablet is not inventing a boat. It is enlarging the boat its audience already knew, past all reason.</p>
    <p><strong>The size.</strong> The ground plan the tablet prescribes covers <strong>one ikû of field — 3,600 square metres</strong>, about two-thirds of a football pitch, with walls rising some <strong>six metres</strong>. Finkel notes that this is the same one-ikû footprint the <em>Gilgamesh</em> epic gives Utnapishtim's vessel — the same floor, drawn in Babylon as a circle and in the epic as a square.</p>
    <p><strong>The build.</strong> The hull is to be a basket on a heroic scale: plaited <strong>palm-fibre rope</strong> in specified quantities, bent <strong>wooden ribs</strong> within, and <strong>bitumen</strong> — two kinds, worked inside and out, hot bitumen poured over the structure to seal it. The quantities are the point. This is the flood story told for an audience of boatmen, with the shopping list left in.</p>
    <p><strong>The animals.</strong> In the tablet's closing lines — damaged, but legible — the wild animals of the steppe are to be brought aboard <strong>"two by two."</strong> It is the earliest known appearance of the detail the whole world now attaches to Noah, written down well over a thousand years before the Genesis text reached its final form. Then Atra-hasis goes aboard, and the door is sealed.</p>

    <h2 id="building-it">Building it</h2>
    <p>In 2014 the tablet's instructions were put to the test. For a television documentary (shown in the United States as the NOVA episode <em>Secrets of Noah's Ark</em>), traditional boatbuilders in Kerala, India, constructed the round ark at about <strong>one-third scale</strong>, following Finkel's reading of the specification. The result deserves to be stated exactly, because it is often rounded up in the retelling: the vessel <strong>floated</strong> — a rope-and-rib coracle of that size is a genuine, workable hull form — but its bitumen seams leaked from the start, and a petrol pump had to run continuously to keep it afloat. The shape was vindicated; the ancient sealant specification, at that scale and in those hands, was only partly reproduced. A one-third-scale boat that needs a pump is not proof the full-size original would have failed; nor is it proof it would have succeeded. It is what it is: the design floats, and the waterproofing is hard to get right. This archive's chapter on the Flood describes the attempt more generously ("a full-scale attempt… broadly vindicated"); this entry follows the documented record, and the difference is flagged in the evidence block below.</p>

    <h2 id="what-it-is-not">What the tablet is — and is not</h2>
    <p>Finkel's own verdict on his tablet is the strongest in this entry, and it is a sceptic's. He is, in his much-quoted phrase, <strong>"107% convinced the ark never existed,"</strong> and he reads the tablet's marvellous practicality as a storyteller's art: an ancient narrator, writing for people who built and caulked boats for a living, supplying the convincing technical detail that makes a wonder believable — precisely the detail his audience was qualified to check. On that reading the Ark Tablet is not a shipwright's document that happens to survive. It is evidence of something almost as interesting: of how a sacred story was maintained, generation by generation, by craftsmen of narrative who kept it seaworthy. The tablet also quietly rearranges the family tree of the flood: with its date in the Old Babylonian period and its hero named Atra-hasis, it belongs to the same stream as the <em>Atrahasis</em> epic — the stream from which, centuries later, the <em>Gilgamesh</em> flood (see the Flood Tablet, V__, this Vault) and then Genesis both drew.</p>

    <h2 id="symbology">Symbology and sacred encoding</h2>
    <p><strong>The circle.</strong> Every other ark in the tradition points somewhere: the epic's cube sits like a building, Genesis's barge has a prow's proportions. The coracle points nowhere, because it does not need to go anywhere. It has no destination, no voyage, no landfall of its own choosing — it only has to rise with the water and hold. Finkel calls it "a cosmic lifeboat," and the shape is the theology: survival not as a journey but as a trust that floating is enough.</p>
    <p><strong>The shopping list as scripture.</strong> The tablet's sacred content is inseparable from its quantities. Rope, ribs, bitumen, bathfuls — in this telling, salvation is not only decreed, it is <em>procured and assembled</em>, by human hands, to a specification. The Mesopotamian flood tradition is unique among the world's deluge stories for this texture: it keeps answering the heckler's question — <em>yes, but how would it actually work?</em> — three thousand years before the hecklers learned to ask it.</p>

      <div class="evidence-head">✦ The evidence, honestly</div>
      <div class="ev supported">
        <h4>What's well supported by the evidence</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>The tablet exists and its text is published: sixty lines of Old Babylonian cuneiform, 11.5 × 6.0 cm, translated and edited by Irving Finkel in <em>The Ark Before Noah</em> (2014), after the owner brought it to the British Museum in 2009. Its provenance — an RAF serviceman's Middle East souvenir of 1945–48, inherited by his son — is consistently reported.</li>
          <li>The text really is a construction specification: it orders a vessel of circular design, equal in length and breadth, of one ikû (3,600 m²) ground plan with six-metre walls, built of palm-fibre rope and wooden ribs and sealed with bitumen — and its closing lines carry the "two by two" boarding of the wild animals.</li>
          <li>The round gufa coracle is a real, documented Mesopotamian boat type, depicted in ancient art and in use on Iraq's rivers into the modern period; the tablet scales up a craft its audience knew.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="ev unsupported">
        <h4>What is not supported</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>That the 2014 television build was full-scale, or that it simply succeeded. It was built at about one-third scale, floated, leaked at the bitumen seams, and required a continuously running pump. (This archive's Flood chapter tells it more generously; the documented version stands here.)</li>
          <li>That the tablet is a historical shipwright's record of a real vessel. Its own translator is emphatic that no such ark existed: the specification is a narrative's convincing detail, not a dockyard's archive.</li>
          <li>That the ark's remains are to be found on any mountain. The tablet gives the boat no landfall at all — a coracle that only had to float — and Finkel's dry conclusion stands: there is nothing up a mountain to look for.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="ev open">
        <h4>What's genuinely open</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>The tablet's exact date within Finkel's c. 1900–1700 BCE range: it is dated by script and language, not by any internal date or recorded find-spot, and its archaeological context was lost when it entered the antiquities trade in the 1940s.</li>
          <li>How this instruction-form telling relates to the <em>Atrahasis</em> epic proper — a excerpt of the epic tradition, a scribe's expansion of it, or a parallel retelling for a practical-minded audience.</li>
          <li>Whether the full-size vessel, built exactly to specification with period workmanship, would have sealed better than the one-third-scale television build — the honest answer is that the experiment, as performed, does not decide it.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
