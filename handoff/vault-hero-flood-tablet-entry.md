    <p class="lead">A slab of baked clay about the size of a paperback — 15.2 by 13.3 centimetres, two columns of dense cuneiform on each face — dug from the ruins of Nineveh and shelved, unread, in the British Museum for years. It carries the flood story of the eleventh tablet of the <em>Epic of Gilgamesh</em>: the oldest full telling of the deluge we possess, in a copy written in the seventh century BCE for the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal. In the autumn of 1872 a self-taught museum assistant named <strong>George Smith</strong> cleaned the dirt from its surface, began to read a story he had known since childhood — a god warns a man; the man builds a boat; the waters rise; the birds go out over a drowned world — and understood that he was holding Noah's Flood in an older Bible than the Bible. It is the tablet that changed the age of the story.</p>

    <h2 id="the-reader">The banknote engraver who read it</h2>
    <p><strong>George Smith (1840–1876)</strong> was apprenticed to a London banknote engraver and taught himself cuneiform the slow way — spending his free hours in the British Museum's galleries until his talent was noticed and he was taken on as an assistant, a working-class scholar in a gentlemen's institution. In 1872, working through trays of fragments excavated at <strong>Kuyunjik</strong> — the mound of ancient Nineveh, across the river from modern Mosul — he was given a large tablet, recovered during <strong>Hormuzd Rassam's</strong> excavations for the museum in the 1850s, whose surface was obscured by dirt and encrustation. When it came back cleaned, he read the flood. He is reported to have exclaimed, "I am the first man to read that after more than two thousand years of oblivion." A later story, handed down by his colleague E. A. Wallis Budge in a book published in 1925 — nearly fifty years after the event, with no witness named — has Smith so overcome that he jumped up, began tearing off his clothes, and ran about the room. The chapter on the Flood in this archive repeats the story with its health warning attached, and so does this entry: Budge was a colourful witness, and the anecdote is told here as a story that is told, not as a verified fact.</p>
    <p>What is documented is the public sequel. On <strong>3 December 1872</strong> Smith read his paper, <em>The Chaldean Account of the Deluge</em>, before the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London — the Prime Minister, Gladstone, was in the audience — and the sensation was immediate. A Babylonian flood story, on a tablet centuries older than any Hebrew manuscript, meant that Genesis had a source, and a pagan one. Smith published his translation in the Society's <em>Transactions</em> the following year.</p>
    <p>The story has a coda that belongs in this entry because it is so rarely told whole. The <em>Daily Telegraph</em> put up 1,000 guineas to send Smith to Nineveh to hunt for the flood story's missing lines. In May 1873, within days of starting to dig, he found a fragment — seventeen lines, numbered <strong>DT 42</strong> for its sponsor — that seemed to slot exactly into the story's one serious gap, describing the command to build and fill the ark. He telegraphed his triumph home. What Smith never learned is that the fragment does not belong to the <em>Gilgamesh</em> epic at all: the British Museum now catalogues DT 42 as a piece of the older <em>Atrahasis</em> — the very poem the epic's flood was borrowed from. He had completed the Babylonian flood story, but from the other manuscript tradition. Smith returned to Mesopotamia twice more, and in August 1876, aged thirty-six, he died of dysentery at Aleppo on the journey home, leaving a wife and five children. The hero of his tablets he had known, all his short career, by the name <em>Izdubar</em> — the reading "Gilgamesh" was only established in 1890, fourteen years after his death.</p>

    <h2 id="what-it-says">What the tablet says</h2>
    <p>The eleventh tablet of the standard <em>Gilgamesh</em> is a story inside the story. Gilgamesh, broken by his friend Enkidu's death and hunting the secret of eternal life, finds <strong>Utnapishtim</strong>, the one man the gods made immortal, living far away at the mouth of the rivers. Utnapishtim tells him how he earned it. The flood account fills the first two hundred or so of the tablet's three-hundred-odd lines; the text as we read it today (Andrew George's edition is the standard modern English text) is pieced together from this tablet and its sister copies.</p>
    <p><strong>The warning.</strong> The great gods, led by Enlil, resolve to drown the world, and swear themselves to secrecy. The god <strong>Ea</strong> — too clever to break an oath outright — repeats the plan aloud to the reed wall of Utnapishtim's house at Shuruppak: <em>tear down your house, build a boat; abandon possessions, save life.</em> The man of Shuruppak hears what the wall was told.</p>
    <p><strong>The boat.</strong> The vessel Ea specifies is startling: a perfect cube, as wide as it is long as it is tall, its walls rising <strong>120 cubits</strong>, roofed over "like the Apsu" — the freshwater deep. Inside, six decks divided into seven levels and nine compartments. The text lingers on the building like a shipwright's account: thousands of units of bitumen melted in the kiln, oil by the thousand, oxen and sheep slaughtered and beer and wine issued to the workmen "like at a New Year's festival," and the whole impossible box slid down a runway of poles into the river, two-thirds of it in the water. Aboard go Utnapishtim's silver and gold, his family and craftsmen, and "all the beasts and animals of the field."</p>
    <p><strong>The storm.</strong> It comes at dawn under a black cloud, with the storm god Adad thundering inside it and the gods' own flood-works bursting the dikes. The poem does not spare its gods: terrified by what they have unleashed, they flee upward to the heaven of Anu and <strong>cower like dogs</strong> against its outer wall, while the goddess Ishtar screams like a woman in labour, wailing that she agreed to the destruction of her own people. For <strong>six days and seven nights</strong> the wind and the waters flatten the land, until the dead fill the sea "like the spawn of fishes" and every human being has turned to clay.</p>
    <p><strong>The mountain and the birds.</strong> The boat grounds on <strong>Mount Nimush</strong> (older translations read Nisir), which holds it fast. On the seventh day Utnapishtim opens the story's most famous sequence — the one Genesis kept. He releases a <strong>dove</strong>: it flies out, finds no resting place, and returns. A <strong>swallow</strong>: the same. A <strong>raven</strong>: it sees the waters falling, eats, preens, croaks — and does not come back.</p>
    <p><strong>The sacrifice and the reckoning.</strong> On the peak, on a stepped arrangement of fourteen vessels, Utnapishtim burns reeds, cedar and myrtle — and the starving gods, who have had no offerings since the world drowned, smell the sweet smell and <strong>gather like flies</strong> over the sacrifice. Ishtar lifts her great lapis necklace and swears never to forget these days. Then Enlil arrives, sees the boat, and rages: no man was to survive. Ea answers him with the poem's moral centre — how could you, the sage of the gods, bring on a flood without reflection? Punish the wrongdoer for his own wrong; do not drown the world for it. Talked down, Enlil boards the boat, takes Utnapishtim and his wife by the hand, touches their foreheads, and blesses them: formerly human, now "gods like us," to live far away at the mouth of the rivers, forever.</p>

    <h2 id="older-story">An old story in a younger epic</h2>
    <p>The flood does not belong to Gilgamesh. Scholars generally hold that the flood episode was <strong>inserted</strong> into the epic when its standard version was assembled — adapted, sometimes line for line, from the older <em>Atrahasis</em> epic, where the survivor is Atrahasis, "exceedingly wise," and the flood is the gods' final answer to a humanity grown too noisy to let Enlil sleep. The seams can still be seen: Ea, defending himself in Tablet XI, slips and calls the flood hero <em>Atrahasis</em> — the borrowing caught in the act. Behind <em>Atrahasis</em> stands a still older Sumerian telling, the flood of Ziusudra. K.3375 is thus a seventh-century Assyrian copy of a Babylonian adaptation of a story already a thousand years old when its scribe pressed it into clay — which is precisely its value. It is the best-preserved link in a documented chain: Ziusudra to Atrahasis to Utnapishtim to Noah, tablet to tablet, in the open.</p>

    <h2 id="symbology">Symbology and sacred encoding</h2>
    <p><strong>A secret told to a wall.</strong> The epic's hinge is a legal fiction: Ea keeps his oath by informing a reed wall, loudly, within the hero's hearing. The wall becomes the story's patron device — the truth is never given directly, always overheard through something thin. Berossus's Babylonian flood, six centuries later, would add its own version of the motif; Genesis removes it entirely, and God speaks to Noah face to face.</p>
    <p><strong>The cube.</strong> Utnapishtim's vessel is not boat-shaped. It is a measured cube — a piece of geometry, a world in miniature with decks and compartments, sealed against the un-creation outside it. Where Genesis gives an oblong barge and the Ark Tablet (V__, this Vault) a circle, the epic gives a cube: three ancient answers to the question of what shape safety takes.</p>
    <p><strong>Six days and seven nights.</strong> The storm's count is built on sevens, the number of completeness in the epic's world — and it frames the matching test that closes the tablet, when the immortal challenges Gilgamesh simply to stay awake six days and seven nights, and Gilgamesh fails before the first night is out. The flood hero endured the sevens; the epic's hero cannot. That is the tablet's real subject: not the flood, but the unrepeatability of surviving it.</p>

        <h4>What's well supported by the evidence</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>A clay tablet (K.3375) in the British Museum, a Neo-Assyrian copy of the 7th century BCE from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh (Kuyunjik), carries the flood narrative of <em>Gilgamesh</em> Tablet XI; it is on public display (Room 55), and its text is published in standard editions, above all Andrew George's.</li>
          <li>George Smith read the flood on it and presented <em>The Chaldean Account of the Deluge</em> to the Society of Biblical Archaeology on 3 December 1872; the sensation, and the tablet's priority over the Genesis text as we have it, are matters of contemporary record.</li>
          <li>The flood episode in Tablet XI is adapted from the older <em>Atrahasis</em> epic — in places line for line — and the wider lineage (Sumerian Ziusudra → Atrahasis → Utnapishtim) is documented on surviving tablets, not conjectured.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="ev unsupported">
        <h4>What is not supported</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>The clothes-tearing story as fact. It rests on E. A. Wallis Budge's account of 1925, written nearly fifty years later with no witness named; Britannica's biography repeats it flatly, but repetition is not corroboration. It is told in this entry as tradition, not evidence.</li>
          <li>That Smith's 1873 fragment (DT 42) completed the <em>Gilgamesh</em> flood tablet. It filled a gap in the flood <em>story</em>, but the British Museum catalogues it as a fragment of <em>Atrahasis</em> — a different, older composition.</li>
          <li>That the tablet records a real, worldwide flood. It records a story. The archive's chapter on the Flood examines the geological claims separately; nothing on this tablet is evidence for a global deluge.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div class="ev open">
        <h4>What's genuinely open</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>How much of Tablet XI K.3375 itself once held, and how the joins between it and its sister manuscripts should be weighted where they disagree — the received text is a scholarly composite and remains, in small part, an argument.</li>
          <li>The exact day in May 1873 on which Smith found DT 42 — his own published account points to 14 May; some modern summaries give 7 May. The month, the place, and the telegram are not in doubt.</li>
          <li>How the <em>Gilgamesh</em> flood reached the Hebrew writers, and when — Babylonian exile, earlier shared inheritance, or both (see ch01).</li>
        </ul>
