## 3. The absence wording (house voice — use verbatim)

**Museum room note (museum.html, Aboriginal Australian Dreaming room):**

> **Not shown — the tjurunga.** The tjurunga of Central Australia are secret-sacred, and by their custodians' law they may be seen only by those entitled to see them. This room keeps no picture of them and no rendering. Some things are not ours to show; they are held by their custodians, and that is where they stay.

**Absence page (vault/tjurunga.html):**

Title: **The Tjurunga of Central Australia — Not Shown**

> This entry is an absence, kept on purpose.
>
> The tjurunga are sacred boards and stones of the Arrernte and neighbouring peoples of Central Australia, bound to the ancestral beings of the Dreaming. They are secret-sacred: held hidden, and shown only in ceremony, only to those entitled to see them. To see one without that right is a serious offence in Arrernte law — so this archive shows none. No picture, no drawing, no rendering.
>
> What can be told is the history around them. In 1896, at the Alice Springs telegraph station, Arrernte men held ceremonies before two outsiders, the biologist Baldwin Spencer and the telegraph master Francis Gillen, who published descriptions and photographs in 1899. Those photographs are now treated as something that should never have been printed. Through the twentieth century, collectors took thousands of tjurunga into museums around the world. The largest collection, gathered by T. G. H. Strehlow, is held by the Strehlow Research Centre in Alice Springs under restricted access, and museums in Australia and overseas are returning secret-sacred objects to their communities.
>
> This archive treats the refusal to show them as part of the record, not a gap in it. The fuller history of the tradition is in the chapter on Aboriginal Australian Dreaming.

(Keep the page's Sources list from the current entry — Wikipedia "Tjurunga", Central Land Council on the Strehlow collection, NT Government Strehlow Research Centre, Spencer & Gillen site. Keep the honesty section pattern: what is supported / not supported / open can be carried over from the current page, trimmed to the absence.)

## 4. The Vault sweep — findings (all 88 checked against the same standard)

Standard applied: an item is a takedown ONLY if displaying/depicting the object itself violates the owning tradition's own restrictions on who may see it (the tjurunga class). Objects publicly displayed or published by their own custodians are NOT takedowns, however sensitive their history.

### Certain — takedown class
| Item | Restriction | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **V68 The Tjurunga of Central Australia** | Secret-sacred; may be seen only by initiated men entitled under Arrernte law; women and the uninitiated forbidden to see or touch them; museums hold them in secured keeping places and "shall not place [them] in any display… unless authorised by the relevant community/custodians." Active repatriation. | National Museum of Australia, *Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander secret/sacred, sensitive and private material policy* §4.5; Wikipedia "Tjurunga"; the live Vault page itself; Central Land Council on the Strehlow collection. |

**No other item among the 88 meets this standard.** No other restricted-viewing object class is in the Vault (no Hopi/Zuni restricted ceremonial objects, no other Aboriginal secret-sacred items).

### Judgment calls — KEPT (do not take down without a separate ruling)
| Item | Why it was looked at | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **V33 The Kartarpur Bir** | The physical volume is kept by the Sodhi family of Kartarpur and is not on public display; access is by custodian consent (granted to verified scholars at length, e.g. the SGPC collation teams). | Not the tjurunga class: the text it carries — the Adi Granth / Guru Granth Sahib — is among the most publicly printed scriptures on earth. The restriction is one family's custody of one volume, not a tradition-wide bar on seeing. Our entry is text-only and shows no image of the volume. **Lean: keep.** |
| **V72 The Book of Shadows** | Gardnerian Wicca's ritual book is initiatory and oathbound in its tradition. | Public versions have been in print since the 1970s with the tradition's own participation; the founder published extensively. No viewing prohibition enforceable as a class. **Lean: keep.** |
| **V87 The Diwan Abatur** | A sacred illustrated scroll of the Mandaeans, a small living faith; Mandaean priestly knowledge has guarded elements. | The scroll was published in full (Drower's edition and translation) and its images circulate in scholarship. Not restricted-viewing as a class. **Lean: keep;** worth a courtesy note in the entry that it is a living faith's scripture. |
| **V77 The Haitian Vèvè** | Ceremonial designs drawn to call the lwa and effaced in the rite. | Vèvè are widely published with practitioner sources (the entry itself cites Milo Rigaud's 1974 collection). They are not secret-restricted designs. **Lean: keep.** |
| **V46 The Piprahwa Relics** | Bone fragments identified by inscription as relics of the Buddha. | Displayed publicly by their custodians (Indian Museum, Kolkata; National Museum, New Delhi). The live 2025 controversy was about the *sale* of the associated gems, not about display. Fails the takedown standard. **Keep.** (Sale/repatriation history already belongs in the entry's honesty section.) |
| **V88 The Skull of "Mary Magdalene" / V16 The James Ossuary** | Human remains / a human bone box. | Both are displayed publicly by their custodians (the basilica at Saint-Maximin exhibits the reliquary; the ossuary was publicly exhibited by its owner). Human-remains sensitivity is real but is not a tradition's viewing prohibition. **Keep.** |
| **V07 The Ark of the Covenant** | The Ethiopian custodians at Aksum never display the object they hold to be the Ark. | No verified object exists and the archive depicts nothing — the entry is textual, about the biblical description and the four traditions of its fate. **Keep**, with a standing note: never commission a rendering of the Aksum claim. |

Everything else in the 88 — manuscripts, codices, monuments, inscriptions, and relics shown publicly by their holding institutions (including V27 the Sacred Tooth Relic, whose casket is carried in public procession by its custodians, and V26 the Black Stone, photographed and venerated in the open) — raises no restriction issue under this standard.
