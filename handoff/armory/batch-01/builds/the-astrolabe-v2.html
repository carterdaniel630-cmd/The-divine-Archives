The record

A sky-map shaped by many hands.

Origins. The planispheric astrolabe grew from Hellenistic work in stereographic projection. Scholars writing in Arabic inherited, tested and greatly extended that mathematical tradition from the eighth century onward.

In the Islamicate world. Makers cut a different plate for each latitude, fitted a rotating star map above it, and engraved calendars, degree scales and shadow squares on the back. Surviving instruments carry Arabic, Persian, Hebrew and Latin additions—evidence of use across languages and faiths.

What it solved. Properly set, the instrument could relate star altitude, date and time; estimate sunrise and sunset; support the determination of prayer times; survey heights; and help establish the qibla. Some instruments carried dedicated qibla tables or maps.

What you are holding here. This is a computational teaching reconstruction, not a facsimile of one surviving object. Its star coordinates and geometric readings are modern calculations presented through the operating logic of a historical astrolabe. It follows Islamic-style instruments, which carry the sighting rule on the back alone — there is no front pointer arm.
