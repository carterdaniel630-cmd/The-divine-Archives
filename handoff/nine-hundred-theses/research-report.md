# The 900 Theses (Conclusiones nongentae) of Giovanni Pico della Mirandola — Research Report

**Purpose.** Research foundation for a new top-priority museum room on Pico's 900 Theses (1486) and the events around them. Primary-source-first. No thesis in this report is invented. Where a claim could not be verified from an accessible source, it is marked as such.

**Subject.** Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (24 February 1463 – 17 November 1494), Italian Renaissance nobleman and philosopher, Count of Concordia, of the ruling family of Mirandola near Modena.

---

## 1. The 900 Theses: structure and groupings

### 1.1 What the work is

In late 1486 Pico had printed in Rome a booklet of 900 short conclusions (theses) that he offered to defend in public disputation against all comers. The theses are bare conclusions with no supporting argumentation — they were meant to be argued live, not read as a self-standing treatise. As Farmer puts it (quoted in Garcia's review of Edelheit), the theses are "loaded with ambiguities that Pico meant to resolve in the course of his debate." Edelheit calls them "a frozen and somewhat silent monument to an ambitious project which was never realized."

The 1486 title as given in the sources is *Conclusiones philosophicae, cabalasticae et theologicae* (also rendered *Conclusiones nongentae* / *Conclusiones DCCCC*). The title-page description of the contents, preserved in the scholarly literature, lists the theses as covering dialectic, morals, physics, mathematics, metaphysics, theology, magic, and Kabbalah, and as drawn from Pico's own views together with the views of the Chaldean, Arab, Hebrew, Greek, Egyptian, and Latin sages (Latin fragment verified from the scholarly quotation in Gazziero's reader, citing Farmer 1998, pp. 210–211 — see Sources).

Pico's own framing for the whole enterprise was concord: the reconciliation of the schools of Plato and Aristotle (which he believed used different words to express the same concepts), extended into a harmony of all schools of thought. His friends called him *Princeps Concordiae*, "Prince of Harmony" — a pun on Prince of Concordia, one of his family's holdings.

### 1.2 The two great divisions (verified counts)

The 900 divide into two blocks, per Farmer's edition as summarized in the scholarly literature (Garcia, reviewing Edelheit):

| Block | Count | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Theses attributed to earlier authorities ("according to the doctrine of…") | **402** | Conclusions drawn from ancient and medieval authorities — Latin scholastics, Greek Platonists, Arabic Aristotelians, Greek Aristotelians, Pythagoras, Cabalists, Hermes Trismegistus, Chaldean theologians |
| Pico's "own opinions" (*secundum propriam opinionem* / his own conclusions) | **498** | Pico's own conclusions, taking up matters of theology, metaphysics, mathematics, and magic |

402 + 498 = 900. These two totals are the only block-level counts verified in this research.

### 1.3 Subgroups with verified counts

**Within the 402 (authorities):**

- **Latin philosophers and theologians — 115 theses.** The first 115 theses are "according to the doctrine of the Latin philosophers and theologians" (*Conclusiones secundum doctrinam latinorum philosophorum et theologorum*), covering six scholastic masters: Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas, Francis of Mayronnes (Franciscus de Mayronis), John Duns Scotus, Henry of Ghent, and Giles of Rome (Garcia, reviewing Edelheit).
  - Of these, **Albert the Great: 16 theses** (Edelheit, via Garcia).
  - **Thomas Aquinas: 45 theses** (Edelheit, via Garcia). Pico's main source for the Aquinas theses appears to have been Aquinas's commentary on Peter Lombard's *Sentences* rather than the *Summa Theologiae*.
  - Counts for Mayronnes, Scotus, Henry of Ghent, and Giles of Rome were **not** verified in the sources accessible for this report (see §8).
- **Other authority groups** (Greek Platonists, Arabic Aristotelians, Greek Aristotelians, Pythagoras, Cabalists, Hermes Trismegistus, Chaldean theologians): the order of the groups as given in the scholarly summary is, "in descending quantitative order, Latin scholastics, Greek Platonists, Arabic Aristotelians, Greek Aristotelians, Pythagoras, Cabalists, Hermes Trismegistus, and Chaldean theologians" (Garcia). **Per-group counts for these seven groups were NOT verified** in the sources accessible for this report and are deliberately not stated here. The full per-authority numbering must be taken from Farmer (1998) or Copenhaver (Harvard UP) before any room display lists subgroup counts.
- **Cabalist theses — 119 total.** Per the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Copenhaver): "119 of them, including the final and culminating 72, are Cabalist theses." This means 119 of the 900 are Cabalist in content, of which the **final 72** form a culminating Cabalist block. Note: the report does **not** state which of the two great divisions (402 vs. 498) the 119 fall into, because the sources consulted do not pin that down in a way this research could verify; Farmer's and Copenhaver's numberings should be checked before the room asserts it. (One secondary account describes 72 Cabalist "theorems"; the number 72 is also the traditional number of names of God derivable from Exodus 14:19–21 — a suggestive parallel noted in the literature, not a verified claim by Pico.)

**Within the 498 (Pico's own):**

- The own-opinions block includes, among other groups, a set of **seventeen "paradoxical conclusions"** reconciling the sayings first of Aristotle and Plato, then of other masters who seem to disagree most (Klibansky, on Pico's *Conclusions*). The first of these paradoxical conclusions is that there is no natural or divine question on which Aristotle and Plato do not agree in sense and substance, even though they seem to differ in words (paraphrase; the Latin was not verified verbatim in this research).
- **NOT verified:** a claim found in one tertiary source (the Wikipedia *Oration* article) that "72 theses describ[e] what Pico believed to be a complete system of physics." This appears to confuse the 72 Cabalist theses with another group and is **not** repeated as fact here. Do not use it in the room without checking Farmer/Copenhaver.

### 1.4 Verified verbatim Latin samples (from the Theses)

Only the following short Latin fragments from the *Conclusiones* were verified verbatim against cited sources during this research. They are the only thesis texts in this report cleared for verbatim use (the Latin original is public domain; see §7):

1. **Thesis 39 (from the Aquinas group):** *"Phantasma est agens secundarium et instrumentale in productione speciei intelligibilis"* — "the phantasm is the secondary and instrumental agent in the production of an intelligible species" (Garcia, quoting Edelheit, p. 263 n. 184). Note: Edelheit finds this formulation is not found in Thomas as such but is implied in Thomas's discussion — i.e., Pico reworks his authorities rather than merely copying them.
2. **A thesis from the Henry of Ghent group:** *"amicitia est virtus"* — "friendship is a virtue" (Garcia, quoting Edelheit, p. 329, on Henry's *Quodlibet* X, q. 12).
3. **The closing announcement (subscription) of the 1486 booklet:** *"Conclusiones non disputabuntur nisi post Epiphaniam; interim publicabuntur in omnibus Italiae Gymnasiis. Et si quis Philosophus aut Theologus, etiam ab extrema Italia, arguendi gratia, Romam venire voluerit, pollicetur ipse dominus disputaturus se viatici expensas illi soluturum de suo."* — i.e., the Conclusions will not be disputed until after the Epiphany; meanwhile they will be published in all Italian universities; and if any philosopher or theologian, even from the ends of Italy, wishes to come to Rome to argue, the disputer promises to pay his travel expenses from his own funds (Klibansky, citing *Opera* I, 56; cf. the announcement quoted in the Wikipedia Pico article).

**Rule for the room:** no other individual thesis may be displayed in Latin or in English translation as "Thesis N" unless its text is first verified verbatim against Farmer (1998) or Copenhaver (Harvard UP) and its numbering confirmed. Paraphrases must be labeled PARAPHRASE.

### 1.5 What Pico claimed the Theses would do

Pico intended the disputation to demonstrate the concord of all schools of thought and, on Farmer's reading, he hinted in his letters and early texts that the debate might even trigger Christ's Second Coming and the end of the world (Farmer, *Syncretism in the West*, pp. 39–46 and *passim*, as summarized on Farmer's own site). This is Farmer's interpretation of Pico's hints, presented here as such — it should be presented in the room as a scholarly reading, not as Pico's stated programme. Pico also expected, on Farmer's account, that the debate would end with the symbolic submission of the pagan sages (Epiphany = the Magi) and the conversion of Jews who would recognize Jesus as the secret of their traditions. Again: attribute to Farmer.

## 2. The 1486 publication

- **What:** *Conclusiones philosophicae, cabalasticae et theologicae* — 900 theses, printed as a small booklet (*libellus* in the papal bull).
- **Where/when:** Rome, December 1486 (end of 1486).
- **Printer:** NOT verified in the sources accessible for this research. Do not name a printer in the room until confirmed against an incunabulum catalogue (ISTC) or Farmer/Copenhaver.
- **Form:** A printed booklet, publicly posted (*publice affigi*) in Rome and published in diverse public places in Rome and in other parts of the world, per the papal bull (paraphrase). The closing announcement stated the disputation would not take place until after the Epiphany (6 January) and that the theses would meanwhile be published in all Italian universities.
- **Circumstances / Pico's intent:** Pico, then 23, planned and offered to finance a grand public disputation in Rome, in the presence of the papal Curia, at which he would defend all 900 theses against any opponent. He invited scholars from across Europe, offering to pay the travel expenses of those who came from far away. He wanted the debate to begin on 6 January 1487, the feast of Epiphany — which Farmer reads as a symbolic date (the submission of the pagan peoples to Christ in the persons of the Magi). He had composed the *Oration* (see §3) as the planned opening speech.
- **Cost and patronage:** Pico paid for the enterprise himself; he had a third of the income of his family's estates from 1483 and transferred his Mirandola property to his nephew Gianfrancesco in 1491.

## 3. The Oration on the Dignity of Man (*Oratio de hominis dignitate*)

### 3.1 What it was

A public discourse composed in Latin in 1486 as the planned opening speech for the Rome disputation. It was never delivered: the disputation was blocked, so the speech was never given. Gianfrancesco Pico, the main source of biographical information about his uncle, says Giovanni thought little of the speech, regarding it as juvenilia.

**Title warning (important for the room):** The title *Oratio de hominis dignitate* ("Oration on the Dignity of Man") was **not Pico's**. Farmer argues this (see *Syncretism in the West*, pp. 32–34, and Farmer's essay "On the So-Called Oration 'On the Dignity of Man'"), and Copenhaver discusses the title history in his I Tatti edition of the *Life* and *Oration* (pp. XXXIII–XLIV). The room must not present "On the Dignity of Man" as Pico's own title; present it as the later, conventional title. Copenhaver (2022, *Pico on Trial*) further argues that Pico "never wrote a speech about the dignity of man" in the modern textbook sense — the speech announced different projects: persuading Christians to become Kabbalists in order to annihilate themselves in God, and convincing philosophers that their path to saving wisdom was concord rather than disputation. This is Copenhaver's contested reading; present it as a scholarly position, balanced against the traditional reading.

### 3.2 Manuscript and publication history

- Composed 1486. Not published in Pico's lifetime.
- First publication: in the collection of his works (*Commentationes*) published by his nephew Gianfrancesco Pico at Bologna in 1496 (printer Benedetto Faelli, per the Pico Project), under the plain heading *Oratio quaedam elegantissima* ("A Very Elegant Oration").
- The only separately published Latin text of the pre-modern era: 1530, as *On Man by Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, explaining the loftier mysteries of sacred and human philosophy* (paraphrase of the Latin title as given by the Stanford Encyclopedia).
- The front matter of the five collected editions/reprints between 1498 and 1521 kept the 1496 formulation (*Oratio quaedam elegantissima*).
- In 1557 a Basel collection first used *On the Dignity of Man*; a Venice edition of the same year used *A Very Elegant Oration on the High Nobility and Dignity of Man*.
- Except as part of the collected works, the Latin text was printed only once before the 1940s. The first English translation appeared in the 1940s, just after the first Italian version (1936). (Per SEP; the exact first-English-translation attribution was not further verified — see §8.)
- The Pico Project (University of Bologna + Brown University) produced a digital edition from the 1496 *editio princeps* incunabula, examining also an anonymous partial manuscript (Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale, misc. Palatino 885, discovered by Eugenio Garin) generally held to represent a first draft of the Oration, and noting that the two Lyon 1498 copies are counterfeits of the princeps. The Project's Latin text follows Francesco Bausi's 2003 critical edition.
- A widely used modern English edition: Borghesi, Papio, and Riva (eds.), *Oration on the Dignity of Man: A New Translation and Commentary* (Cambridge University Press, 2012) — **in copyright** (see §7).

### 3.3 Core arguments (all paraphrase — no verbatim quotation)

- **Form:** The Oration follows the standard academic, humanistic rhetorical pattern of the day. It falls into two parts: (1) the philosophical basis of the speaker — praise of human beings and an account of human nature; (2) the announcement and justification of the topics to be disputed (the 900 theses).
- **The human being without a fixed place:** Pico rejects the traditional basis for praising human beings — that they hold a unique central place in creation as microcosms. Instead, he argues (paraphrase) that God, having created the world and everything in it, gave every creature a fixed and immutable form — except the human being, who was created with no definite abode or form, and given free will and the use of all God's creatures. The human being is neither heaven nor earth, mortal nor immortal, but free to choose: to sink to the level of animals or to rise to the divine. This is the famous "chameleon" / self-shaping argument. In Copenhaver's and Grafton's readings, the human being as created has no special dignity of its own — only the dignity of potential: angels and animals cannot change position, but the human being can.
- **Ascent through philosophy:** When the human being philosophizes, he ascends the chain of being toward the angels and communion with God; when he fails to exercise his intellect, he vegetates. The dignity lies in the capacity for self-transformation through free will.
- **Three-stage mystical vocation:** In Pier Cesare Bori's summary, the Oration presents the human vocation as a mystical vocation realized in three stages: moral transformation, intellectual research, and final perfection in identity with the absolute reality — a paradigm Bori takes to be universal because it can be retraced in every tradition. (Attribute to Bori.)
- **Angelic hierarchy:** Pico dwells on the three highest choirs — Seraphim (charity; "burn with love for the Creator"), Cherubim (intelligence; through contemplation and meditation), and Thrones (justice; being just in ruling over "inferior things") — and argues that the philosopher, a creature of heaven and not of earth, can attain these states. He emphasizes the Cherubim as preparation for "the fire of the Seraphim and the judgement of the Thrones" (paraphrase of the tertiary summary; verify against a scholarly edition before display).
- **Defense of philosophy and the liberal arts:** The speech defends the right to practice philosophy and the liberal arts, and argues that Pico's youth should not discredit the theses.
- **Esoteric intent:** Per SEP (Copenhaver), large portions of the Oration draw on Kabbalistic texts (via Flavius Mithridates's Latin translations) in ways no contemporary Christian could have detected without the clues provided by the *Conclusions*. Pico's method in the Oration was deliberately esoteric; the speech proclaims that the highest and most sacred wisdom must not be divulged in plain language.
- **Introduction to the Theses:** The second half justifies the disputation project itself and the 900 theses as, in Pico's view, a complete and sufficient basis for the discovery of all knowledge.

### 3.4 Famous passages

**No famous passage is quoted verbatim in this report.** The well-known passages (God's address to Adam about having no fixed form; the "maker and moulder of thyself" idea) exist in many modern English translations, all of which are in copyright or of unverified status (see §7). For the room, these passages must either be (a) quoted in Pico's Latin from a verified critical edition (Bausi 2003 / Copenhaver / Farmer) — Latin is public domain — with a fresh, clearly labeled paraphrase; or (b) newly translated in-house for the room, with a licensing review. Do not lift wording from Wallis (1965), Borghesi/Papio/Riva (2012), or any other modern translation.

## 4. The 1487 Roman disputation that never happened

### 4.1 Why it was blocked

The planned January 1487 disputation never took place. Conservative theologians suspected the theses of heresy and persuaded Pope Innocent VIII to act. In February 1487 Innocent VIII halted the proposed debate and appointed a commission to examine the theses for orthodoxy. The bull itself (paraphrase; see §4.3) says that men zealous for the faith asserted some conclusions were contrary to the faith, erroneous, scandalous, ill-sounding, and suspected of unsound doctrine, and that the minds of many of the faithful were greatly scandalized.

### 4.2 The papal commission and the 13 theses

- The commission was headed by John, Bishop of Tournai, master of the papal household, together with bishops, professors of sacred theology, and doctors of both laws (canon and civil law) specially appointed by the pope and residing in the Curia, with Pico himself present for part of the examination.
- The commission's finding, per SEP (Copenhaver): it first declared **six** of the theses suspect and condemned **seven** others, then rejected Pico's clarifications and repudiated **all thirteen**.
- The 13 theses were therefore condemned; Pico agreed in writing to retract them, though he did not change his mind about their validity. Subsequently **all 900 theses** were condemned as a body — the bull condemns the booklet as a whole (see §4.3).

**The 13 condemned theses — as far as verifiable (CRITICAL GAP):**

The papal bull **does not enumerate the 13 theses individually**. It expressly declines to do so, on the ground that it would be lengthy and burdensome to insert each condemned conclusion with the reasons for its condemnation. No source accessible for this research provides a complete, numbered list of the 13 with verified texts. **Do not display a list of "the 13 condemned theses" in the room until the list is established from Farmer (1998), Copenhaver (2022, *Pico on Trial* — which translates several Questions of the *Apology*), or the *Apology* itself.**

The following topics are attested in secondary sources as among the suspected/condemned theses. Each is given as a **paraphrase of the topic**, not as the text of a thesis:

1. **Magic and Kabbalah as proof of Christ's divinity.** The most startling thesis, per the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*: that no science gives surer conviction of the divinity of Christ than *magia* (knowledge of the secrets of the heavenly bodies) and Kabbalah. (Paraphrase; the exact Latin/numbering not verified.)
2. **Christ's descent to the underworld.** That Christ descended *ad inferos* not in His real presence but *quoad effectum* (as to effect / in a virtual manner). (Britannica 1911, paraphrase.)
3. **Adoration of images and the cross.** That no image or cross should receive *latreia* (the worship due to God alone) even in the sense allowed by Thomas Aquinas. This was tied to the question of *de adoratione crucis et imaginum* discussed in the *Apology*. (Britannica 1911; Edelheit, via Garcia.)
4. **Origen's fate.** That it is more reasonable to regard Origen as saved than as damned. (Britannica 1911, paraphrase.)
5. **Faith and free will.** That it is not in a man's free will to believe or disbelieve an article of faith as he pleases. (Britannica 1911, paraphrase.) Copenhaver's *Pico on Trial* treats the will/heresy question through the *Apology*'s Questions (notably Q8, "Is heresy willful?").
6. **A thesis "in the mode of speaking of Dionysius" (negative theology).** One of the 13 accused theses concerned angels/God and was defended by Pico as Dionysian negative theology, not heresy — e.g., claims of the form "God is not intellect" / "an angel is not a man" in the Dionysian mode. (Secondary summary of the *Apology* defence; exact thesis text and number not verified.)
7. **Theses 1–7 (of the 13) — NOT individually identified** in the sources accessible for this research. The remaining condemned topics beyond the five/six above are **unknown** to this report.

Also relevant: the commission's judgment covered not only heresy but also theses "scandalous and offensive to devout ears," theses reviving "the errors of pagan philosophers," theses said to foster "the perfidy of the Jews," and theses that under a disguise of natural philosophy lent respectability to arts hostile to the Catholic faith (paraphrase of the bull's categories).

### 4.3 The bull of condemnation (Innocent VIII, 4 August 1487)

Pope Innocent VIII's bull condemning the booklet is dated **4 August 1487** (the day before the Nones of August, in the third year of his pontificate), given at Rome at St Peter's. Its provisions, paraphrased:

- It condemns and reprobates the booklet of the 900 conclusions — "notwithstanding that it may contain some which are Catholic and true" — because of the admixture of damnable assertions. (Paraphrase.)
- It forbids all the faithful, under penalty of excommunication incurred *ipso facto*, to read, write, print, or listen to the work; those possessing it in written or printed form must burn it within three days of receiving notice.
- It declares that Pico himself, who published the conclusions solely for scholastic disputation and under the correction of the Apostolic See, and who professed that they are to be held as judged by the pope and the commission, and swore never to defend such things, has incurred no mark of unfavourable estimation on account of the foregoing.
- It orders publication of the bull in churches during solemn Masses and directs ordinaries and inquisitors to restrain the disobedient.

**Notes on the bull:** (a) The bull condemns the *book*, not Pico personally — a distinction the WM Review translation notes explicitly. (b) One tertiary source (the Wikipedia *Oration* article) states the condemnation was "written on 5 August 1487, but not issued until the following December" — this conflicts with the bull's own dating (4 August 1487) and is **unverified**; do not repeat it without checking. (c) The claim, repeated by Farmer and widely, that the *Conclusions* was "the first printed book to be universally banned by the Church" (or "the first book banned for printing") is presented here as Farmer's widely repeated claim, not as an independently verified fact; the WM Review describes it as "said to be the Church's first universal ban on a book." (d) The claim that "nearly all copies were burned" is likewise a widely repeated assertion (Wikipedia, citing secondary sources); treat as traditional, not verified, in the room.

### 4.4 Pico's Apologia (his defense)

- Pico responded with an *Apologia* (*Apologia J. Pici Mirandolani, Concordiae comitis*), an elaborate and learned defense of himself and the condemned theses, **hurriedly composed within about three weeks** (UCT thesis summary). It also incorporated about half of the still-unpublished *Oration* (per SEP — though not the half that later became famous).
- **Dating discrepancy (flagged):** Scholarly treatments (Edelheit's *Ficino, Pico and Savonarola*) treat the *Apologia* as a work of **1487**. Tertiary sources (Wikipedia and derivatives) give **1489** as its publication date and describe it as dedicated to Lorenzo. The 1489 date appears to conflate the *Apologia* with **Pedro Garsia's** *Determinationes magistrales contra conclusiones apologeticas Joannis Pici Mirandulani Concordie comitis* (Rome, 1489), the official rebuttal of the *Apology*. For the room, use **1487** for the *Apologia* with a note that some tertiary sources give 1489, pending confirmation against Fornaciari's edition (2010) or Copenhaver (2022). A modern edition: Paolo Fornaciari (ed.), *Apologia: L'Autodifesa di Pico di fronte al Tribunale dell'Inquisizione* (Florence: Edizioni del Galluzzo, 2010). Copenhaver's *Pico della Mirandola on Trial* (Oxford UP, 2022) includes English translations of several Questions of the *Apology* — **in copyright**.
- **Character of the Apology:** Per Copenhaver, the defendant in the *Apology* made his case openly in an academic and belligerent voice, not a prophetic or poetic one; his main authorities were medieval clerics and theologians (Robert Holcot, Durandus of Saint-Pourçain, Henry of Ghent are the most cited scholastic masters in the *Apology*, per Edelheit), not secular Renaissance intellectuals. Pico also used the *Apology* to correct his accusers' errors — e.g., one accuser reportedly took "Cabala" for the name of a man and asserted it was a scoundrel who had written against Christ (McClintock–Strong Cyclopedia, 19th century; anecdotal — present as such if used).
- **Effect:** When the pope learned of the *Apology*'s circulation, he set up an inquisitorial tribunal, forcing Pico to renounce the *Apology* in addition to the condemned theses, which Pico agreed to do (tertiary account; the precise tribunal proceedings were not verified in this research).

### 4.5 Aftermath: flight, arrest, absolution

- **Flight and arrest (1488):** Pico left for France. At the pope's request he was detained by French authorities — arrested by Philip II, Duke of Savoy, at the demand of the papal nuncios — and briefly jailed at Vincennes. (One account says he was arrested at Lyons; the Vincennes imprisonment is the consistent detail.)
- **Release:** Through the intercession of several Italian princes, instigated by Lorenzo de' Medici, King Charles VIII of France had him released, and the pope was persuaded to allow Pico to live in Florence under Lorenzo's protection.
- **Return (summer 1488):** By the summer of 1488 Pico was back in Fiesole as Lorenzo's guest.
- **Absolution (1493):** In 1493, after the accession of Alexander VI (Rodrigo Borgia), Pico was pardoned/cleared of the papal censures. A brief of Alexander VI dated **18 June 1493** absolved him from all heresy (Britannica 1911; McClintock–Strong Cyclopedia). SEP confirms: "In 1493 Pico achieved reconciliation with a higher authority when Alexander VI pardoned him."

## 5. Pico's life

### 5.1 Dates and family

- **Born 24 February 1463** at Mirandola (Duchy of Mirandola, near Modena). Youngest son of Gianfrancesco I Pico, Lord of Mirandola and Count of Concordia, and Giulia Boiardo (daughter of Feltrino Boiardo, Count of Scandiano). Younger brother of Galeotto I. The family claimed descent from Constantine (traditional claim — present as a family claim, not fact).
- **Died 17 November 1494** at Florence, aged 31, on the same day Charles VIII of France entered Florence. Buried with Girolamo Benivieni at San Marco, Florence; Savonarola delivered the funeral oration. Ficino's lament on his death is recorded in the literature (paraphrase only in this report).
- **Nephew:** Gianfrancesco Pico della Mirandola (c. 1470–1533), editor of Giovanni's works (Bologna, 1496), biographer, philosopher in his own right, and later an opponent of his uncle's "ancient wisdom" programme (his *Examen vanitatis doctrinae gentium*, 1520). Sir Thomas More translated Gianfrancesco's biography as *Life of John Picus, Earl of Mirandola* (1510).

### 5.2 Education and circle

- **Bologna (1477, age 14):** Sent to study canon law, intending an ecclesiastical career; named a papal protonotary (probably honorary) at about age 10. He soon renounced canon law.
- **Ferrara (from 1479):** Philosophy and theology. Met Angelo Poliziano, Girolamo Benivieni, and probably the young Dominican Girolamo Savonarola; remained close to all three for life.
- **Padua (1480–1482):** A major centre of Aristotelianism. Studied Hebrew and Arabic with **Elia del Medigo**, a Jewish Averroist Aristotelian; read Aramaic manuscripts with him; del Medigo translated Judaic manuscripts from Hebrew into Latin for Pico for years. May also have studied with Nicoletto Vernia and the Scotist Antonio Trombetta (Edelheit, via Garcia — possibilities, not certainties).
- **Florence (November 1484):** Met **Lorenzo de' Medici** and **Marsilio Ficino**, who shaped his understanding of philosophy. Ficino had just published his Latin translations of Plato under Lorenzo's patronage. Lorenzo supported and protected Pico until Lorenzo's death in 1492.
- **Paris (1485):** The citadel of Aristotelian scholasticism; probably there Pico began the 900 Theses and conceived the public debate. Before leaving Florence he had written his famous long letter to **Ermolao Barbaro** (1485) defending scholastic technical terminology against humanist critics of "barbaric" scholastic Latin — a defense of philosophy in rhetorical form, modeled on Plato's *Phaedrus*.
- **Perugia / Fratta (1486):** Recovering from injuries after the Arezzo scandal (see below), he wrote that "divine Providence … caused certain books to fall into my hands. They are Chaldean books … of Esdras, of Zoroaster and of Melchior, oracles of the magi" (Pico to Ficino, paraphrase of the letter as quoted in the literature). There he was introduced to the mystical Hebrew Kabbalah.
- **The Arezzo scandal (1486):** Pico attempted to run off with a married woman, Margherita, wife of Giuliano Mariotto de' Medici; he was caught, wounded, and imprisoned by her husband, and released only through Lorenzo's intervention. (Reported by SEP and the biographical tradition; include or omit per the room's editorial judgment — it is part of the historical record, not salacious invention.)
- **Later years:** Settled in a villa near Fiesole prepared by Lorenzo; wrote the *Heptaplus* (1489) and *De ente et uno* (1491/1492); worked on the unfinished *Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem* (12 books against divinatory astrology, published posthumously, Bologna 1495/1496). After Lorenzo's death (1492) he moved to Ferrara but continued to visit Florence. He grew close to Savonarola, destroyed his own poetry, gave away his fortune, and resolved to become a monk/friar (Dominican) — prevented by his early death. He never renounced his syncretist convictions (per the Wikipedia synthesis; the exact state of his late views is debated — see §6).

### 5.3 Death — poisoning question

- Contemporary rumour: his own secretary poisoned him because Pico had grown too close to Savonarola.
- **2007 exhumation:** The bodies of Poliziano and Pico were exhumed from San Marco, Florence. Forensic tests (Gallello et al., *Journal of Forensic and Legal Medicine*, 2018) found that Pico likely died of acute arsenic poisoning (intentional or otherwise), while the evidence was insufficient to conclude the same for Poliziano (whose arsenic levels may reflect chronic exposure or post-mortem processes). Present as a forensic finding with its caveats, not as a settled murder verdict. Whether the poisoning was deliberate, and by whom, is **unknown**.

### 5.4 Intellectual sources

- **Plato and Aristotle:** Pico based his ideas chiefly on Plato (like Ficino) but retained deep respect for Aristotle; his lifelong aim was to prove their concord. *De ente et uno* (1491/1492) was meant as the first installment of the great work proving Plato's thought in concord with Aristotle's; the larger *Concordia Platonis Aristotelisque* remained unfinished.
- **Scholasticism:** Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, Henry of Ghent, Giles of Rome, Francis of Mayronnes (the 115 Latin theses); plus Robert Holcot, Durandus of Saint-Pourçain, and others in the *Apology*. Pico defended the best of the medieval commentators in his letter to Barbaro.
- **Arabic thought:** Averroes (Ibn Rushd) and Avicenna (Ibn Sina) — the Arabic Aristotelians — cited as authorities in the Theses. Pico defended Averroes and Avicenna as commentators on Aristotle.
- **Greek Platonism / Neoplatonism:** Plotinus, Proclus, the Greek commentators; the ancient theologians Zoroaster, Hermes Trismegistus, Orpheus, Pythagoras.
- **Kabbalah — Christian Kabbalah:** Pico is regarded as the founder of the tradition of Christian Kabbalah. His Hebrew teachers and informants: Elia del Medigo; **Yohanan Alemanno (Johanan Aleman)**; and above all **Flavius Mithridates**, a convert who translated (and, per Copenhaver and Grafton, mistranslated and in places doctored/forged) thousands of pages of Kabbalistic material into Latin for Pico, including seemingly pro-Christian texts. Pico's Kabbalistic sources included Abraham Abulafia and Menahem Recanati (per Wirszubski and the secondary literature). Pico also translated into Latin three Kabbalistic works obtained through Alemanno: Recanati's commentary on the Pentateuch, Eleazar of Worms's *Hokhmat ha-Nefesh*, and Shem-Tob Falaquera's *Sefer ha-Ma'alot* (1901 *Jewish Encyclopedia*).
  - **Pico's Christian Kabbalah claims (present as Pico's claims, not as facts):** Pico held that Kabbalah was an angelic revelation / oral Torah confided to Moses and passed on in secret through Esdras (Ezra) and other sages; that it was holier than the pagan wisdom Ficino traced to Zoroaster and Hermes; and that the primary doctrines of Christology and Trinitarian theology could be grounded in Kabbalah — i.e., that the mysteries of the Christian faith were corroborated by Kabbalistic teaching. He regarded Kabbalah as the holier Hebrew analog of the gentile "ancient theology." The 72 culminating Cabalist theses turn this into Christology and Trinitarian theology (SEP).
  - **Caution:** Modern scholarship (Wirszubski; Copenhaver; Busi; Idel) stresses that Pico's Kabbalah was mediated through flawed and sometimes forged translations, and that his grasp of the material was partial. The room must present Pico's Kabbalah as Pico understood it, while noting the scholarly caveat that his sources were in part unreliable. Do not present forged "pro-Christian" Kabbalistic texts as genuine Jewish sources.
- **Hermetica:** The Hermetic writings (Hermes Trismegistus) — thought in Pico's time to be as ancient as the Old Testament; modern scholarship dates the *Corpus Hermeticum* to late antiquity (a standard scholarly fact, but not verified in this research's sources — confirm before display or omit).
- **Church Fathers:** Augustine and other Fathers (Grafton notes Pico likely found inspiration for the Oration's view of human dignity in Augustine); Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (negative theology / *via negativa*), whose "mode of speaking" Pico invoked in his defense.
- **Languages:** Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Aramaic (Chaldee), Arabic, and Syriac (per the biographical tradition; the Syriac claim appears in tertiary sources and was not independently verified).

## 6. Scholarly reception

### 6.1 How the Theses are treated by modern scholarship

- The *Conclusions* are treated as a syncretic programme: an attempt to harmonize all traditions known to Pico — scholastic, Platonic, Aristotelian, Arabic, Hebrew/Kabbalistic, Chaldean, Egyptian, Hermetic — into a single "symphony of philosophies." Scholarship is divided over how to read the project:
  - **The "Manifesto of the Renaissance" reading** (Burckhardtian / idealist tradition; Cassirer, Garin, Kristeller, and textbook humanism): the Oration as a charter of human freedom and dignity, Pico as a hero of progressive humanism. This reading was shaped by 19th-century idealist historians (Tennemann's *History of Philosophy*, 1798–1819, first presented Pico as a proto-Kantian advocate of human freedom and dignity) and by the many reprints of the Oration in mid-20th-century anthologies.
  - **The esoteric/Kabbalistic reading** (Yates; Wirszubski; Copenhaver): Pico's project is fundamentally about Kabbalah, magic, and mystical ascent; the Oration's message is deliberately esoteric; the "dignity of man" title misleads.
  - **The scholastic reading** (Dulles 1941; Caroti; Edelheit 2022): Pico's engagement with scholastic sources is thorough, competent, and creative, and is fundamental to his claim to originality; the Kabbalistic sources should not be prioritized over the scholastic context.
  - **Copenhaver's revisionism (2019, 2022):** Pico's work was "in no way progressive — or 'humanist'"; his main authorities were medieval clerics and theologians; the *Apology*, not the *Oration*, is the best guide to his thought. Contested — present as one scholarly position.
  - **Farmer's model (1998):** The 900 Theses as a "laboratory" for studying syncretic processes in premodern thought cross-culturally, with a neurobiological/cognitive model of how syncretism generates correlative structures. Farmer also overturns Frances Yates's model of the origins of Renaissance magic in Pico and Ficino, arguing Pico emerges as Ficino's adversary, not his disciple.
- **Contemporary reputation in his own day:** Pico stayed famous in three ways (SEP): as a critic of astrology; as an expert on Cabala; and as the amazing Pico — the "Phoenix." His nephew Gianfrancesco's family hagiography (noble origins, beauty, prodigious learning, youthful sins, trouble with the Church, repentance, pious death) shaped his fame for centuries.
- **Later reception:** Jacob Brucker's *Critical History of Philosophy* (1742) described Pico as "a Platonizing, Judaizing syncretist" — a condemnation in Brucker's terms. German theologians and biblical scholars were still debating his theses when the young Kant began teaching. Pico's influence ran to Reuchlin and the Christian Kabbalistic tradition, and to the later Western esoteric tradition.
- **A standing scholarly caution:** The 900 Theses are "loaded with ambiguities" (Farmer) and were never meant to be read without the live debate that would have resolved them. Any room display that presents a thesis as a flat assertion should carry a note that the theses are bare conclusions whose intended sense would have been fixed in disputation.
