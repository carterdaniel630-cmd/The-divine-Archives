# VR Expert Walkthrough — The Divine Archives VR Museum Pilot

**Lens:** one senior VR experience designer who ships on Quest 2.
**Date:** 2026-11-15. **Ordered by:** Carter, after his own first walk.
**Status of the pilot as walked:** the "Enter VR" door (G1) and movement (G2) are merged and live. Carter walked them tonight on his Meta Quest 2 — the floor device, the weakest headset a real visitor brings — and they work. Not yet built: the frame-budget piece (G3), the curtain (G4), and the pilot spaces themselves (G5: Rotunda, Egypt ch02, Mesopotamia ch03).
**Basis:** the pilot spec set (interaction, comfort, performance, artistry, audio) plus Carter's five notes from tonight's walk: eye height too low standing; the glass deck over the pits reads as floating; ground at your feet must be right in flat and VR; more creatures, and creatures that react to you; day and weather.
**Nature of this document:** advice for Carter's verdict. Nothing here is a build order.

Jargon is glossed in plain words at first use, per house law.

## LENS 1 — OPERABILITY: does it run and work well on the Quest 2

The pilot's skeleton is sound: one codebase, VR as added modules, a written budget with a floor tier that certifies (Quest 2) and a rich tier that rewards (Quest 3). The danger is not the plan — it is that the current scene, as inherited from the flat build, already spends more than the Quest 2 has, in three named places. In order of what breaks first as rooms fill in:

1. **Light count breaks first.** The scene today carries 9–10 always-on lights (two hemisphere lights, a flashlight, a lantern, four pooled point lights, a spotlight). Every light is paid for by every pixel, in both eyes. The budget's floor-tier cap is **5**. This is the single most likely cause of a missed 72 fps (13.9 ms per frame) once real rooms replace test space. *Recommendation: the light diet is not an optimization pass at the end — it is a gate at the start. Ambient + one hemisphere + lantern + two pooled points on Quest 2; delete lights at zero intensity rather than dimming them (a dimmed light still charges full price); the flashlight leaves the scene graph entirely.*
2. **Unmerged relic draw calls break second.** A "draw call" is one instruction telling the chip to draw a batch of objects; the Quest 2 ceiling is ~160 per frame across both eyes. The existing merge step (`bake()`) only merges the twelve shared materials — the 40+ relic builders each emit 5–25 separate meshes, so one six-case room can carry 150+ relic draw calls before walls are counted. *Recommendation: extend the merge to relic meshes that share a material, and switch on the budget's three-step detail ladder per relic (full detail in arm's reach, half segments across the room, a flat silhouette stand-in — an "impostor" — through a doorway). The ladder exists on paper; it must exist in code before G5 fills rooms.*
3. **Through-wall building breaks third.** Today, rooms build by distance (14–48 m), so standing in the Rotunda can hold several wings' exhibits in memory at once, through walls the visitor cannot see through. *Recommendation: portal culling — only the room you are in, the rooms its doors open into, and the corridor you stand in are resident ("portal" = a doorway; the building's door graph is already in the data). This is cheap, deterministic code, and it converts an unbounded problem into a three-room problem.*

**Load behaviour.** The loading law is already written correctly: wing changes hide behind a ~250 ms fade with at most 400 ms of masked work, and if the work runs long the visitor waits in a lit, labelled holding state — never a black screen. Hold that line absolutely; the flat side's history (long black waits at pilgrimage gates) is exactly what this law exists to prevent. Two operability details worth naming: all shader programs must be compiled inside the masked window (a first-use compile mid-frame is a 50–200 ms freeze, the worst single hitch a headset can show), and the session must survive a dropped graphics context by re-entering cleanly rather than dying — both are already in the perf gate checklist; keep them as pass/fail, not aspirations.

**Stability and controls working every time.** The XR loop must run on the renderer's own animation loop (ordinary web frame timing is throttled inside a headset session — already specified). Audio must unlock on the "Enter VR" tap itself, not assume the session start did it (already in the audio brief — verify it in-headset, because silent-first-entry is the kind of fault nobody reports, they just leave). Resolution control is the biggest single Quest 2 lever and must be explicit: render scale 0.8 and maximum fixed foveation (rendering the edges of vision at lower detail, where the lenses blur anyway) on the floor tier, set once per session, never adjusted live.

**Best recommendation under this lens:** *G3 is not a report — it is an instrument plus a diet.* Build the in-headset performance overlay first (frame time, draw calls, triangles, light count, texture memory, per second, on the real Quest 2), then pass the light diet and portal culling, then certify the worst sightline in the building (the Wing II atrium looking into both pilot rooms) at p95 ≤ 13.9 ms over a 60-second walk. Every later piece gets judged by this gate. A room that misses budget is not "unoptimized" — per the artistry addendum, it is unfinished.

## LENS 2 — MOVEMENT: how getting around feels, end to end

Teleport plus snap turn is the right skeleton for a first-time, wonder-first audience, and the Comfort Warden's numbers are the right numbers. What Carter felt as "off" has one mechanical cause to hunt first (height), and a handful of tuning causes after it.

### The height problem — causes ranked

The design eye height is 1.62 m standing. The seated fallback is 1.2 m — a difference of about 0.4 m, which is exactly the size of "too low" a standing visitor reports. Ranked by likelihood:

1. **The floor reference silently fell back.** The headset session asks for `local-floor` — the reference frame in which the system knows where your real floor is. If that request isn't granted, or the seated fallback engages, the camera sits at the fixed seated height (1.2 m) while you stand. The pilot spec itself forbids this being silent ("never a silent substitution") — if the build substitutes without declaring it, that is the bug, and it matches the symptom precisely: uniformly low, everywhere, from the first second.
2. **A rig offset error.** In WebXR the camera pose already arrives at true eye height above the reference floor. If the museum's rig — inherited from the flat build, where the camera carries its own eye-height offset — adds, subtracts, or scales that offset a second time, the eye lands low (or high) by a fixed amount everywhere. Same symptom family as #1, slightly less likely, identical diagnostic.
3. **Teleport landing height.** If each teleport re-seats the rig from a floor measurement taken against the wrong surface — the glass deck instead of the pit floor, a navmesh (the map of walkable floor) whose height datum differs from the room's visual floor — height would be right at spawn and wrong after moving. Ranked third because Carter's report reads as a general lowness, not a drift; the diagnostic below separates the two.
4. **World scale mismatch.** If somewhere a room was built at other than 1 unit = 1 metre, everything is proportionally large and the eye feels low relative to doorways (3.2 m) and plinths (1.1 m). Ranked fourth: Carter said *height* felt low, not that the rooms felt giant — but the check is free (a 1.1 m plinth should sit clearly below a 1.62 m eye; if plinths read at eye level, scale is wrong).
5. **Carter's own headset floor calibration.** Ranked last on purpose: it would distort every app he owns, not just this one, and the app's own known fallback path (cause #1) explains the symptom without touching his device.

**The one diagnostic that settles it fastest:** a **spawn-and-teleport height readout** — one debug line, visible in-headset or in the perf overlay's log, showing (a) which reference space the session actually granted (`local-floor`, lesser space, or seated fallback), and (b) the camera's height above the floor at spawn, standing still, and again immediately after the first teleport. Read it once, standing in the Rotunda: granted-space wrong or height ≈ 1.2 m at spawn → cause #1 or #2, fixed in the rig. Height ≈ 1.6 m at spawn but changed after teleporting → cause #3, fixed in the landing code. Height right and plinths still read wrong → cause #4. One number and one word; one minute; no guessing.

### Tuning the teleport and the turn

- **The arc must feel instant and honest.** The press that summons the arc shares one trigger with tapping-to-select: under 200 ms is a tap, over it is the arc. That threshold is where "off" hides — if the arc appears late or a held press ever fires a select, the hand stops trusting the trigger. The arc should be visibly rising the moment the threshold crosses, and a press that became a hold must never select (already specified — test it like it matters, because it does).
- **Landing is a promise kept.** The brass ring marker shows a chevron for the direction you will face; arrival must face exactly that, never snap to a room default. The transition is the incense-smoke dissolve at 150–250 ms. **Teleport gets no comfort vignette** (the soft edge-darkening used during artificial motion) — the Warden struck it explicitly, because teleport has no continuous motion to soften. If G2 darkens the edges on teleport, that alone can read as "off"; check it.
- **Snap turn:** 30° per flick, completing within 80 ms, 250 ms cooldown between snaps so a held stick can't machine-gun the world around. The vignette belongs here and on smooth movement only, arriving within 100 ms and clearing over 300–400 ms. Keep the 15° alternate setting for sensitive visitors.
- **Distance is covered by chaining, not longer hops.** 8 m per hop in every posture, seated included. In a 3 m-wide processional court that is generous; across the Rotunda it is two calm hops. Do not raise the cap to make travel "faster" — chained teleports with a trustworthy marker feel better than one long blind jump.
- **Vertical travel is staged landings, never falling** — ziggurat stages and the burial-chamber descent pad are teleport targets. The comfort ban list (no falling, no forced camera motion, no shake, horizon never tilts) is not a limitation on this museum; it is the museum's manners. Keep it absolute.
- **The whole-museum journey:** hub-and-archways (the Rotunda reads in ten seconds), threshold steles that orient on entry, the golden thread along the floor remembering your last twelve rooms as ambience, and the one-action recovery — Y button or palm-hold — that returns anyone, from anywhere, to the Rotunda. That recovery is the load-bearing promise; everything else is scenery.

**Best recommendation under this lens:** fix height first, with the readout, before any other VR judgment is trusted — a wrong eye height quietly poisons every later opinion about scale, plaques, decks, and doorways, including Carter's own verdicts on pieces not yet built.

## LENS 3 — ENGAGEMENT: what holds a visitor, and brings them back

The pilot's engagement design is unusually honest: the hooks are sightlines, not tricks — the one warm portal among eight veiled ones, the tomb axis hidden until discovered, the blue gate hiding the summit it promises. What it lacks so far is *life*, and Carter's notes name the gap exactly. In order of value per unit of cost:

**The Raven and the Keeper — the cheapest great win.** The findability ruling is already made: the Keeper is found by following the Raven and his wake; his whole figure appears only in the museum. Neither needs an animation system to matter. The Raven is one bird on a looping flight path — Rotunda to the Keeper's Table and back — with a faint followable wake; it is simultaneously a living thing, a wayfinding device, and a story. The Keeper at his Table is a still figure: presence without performance. *Recommendation: build the Raven first (motion teaches visitors the museum is inhabited), the Keeper's placed figure second, the wake as a simple ground trail third. Nothing appears within 2 m of a visitor, ever — reveals happen at 3 m or more, with a soft sound as herald (Comfort rule, and good manners).*

**Reactive creatures — seed-built, small, and honest.** The house method fits perfectly: beings generated from one seed by rule, no rigs or animation files, appendages moved by simple physics chains. Cheap version, and the right first version: one species in one outdoor-feeling space — swallows in the Rotunda's oculus shaft, or scarabs in the Egypt court — with three reactions only: scatter when a visitor teleports in nearby, drift away from an approaching visitor, settle when the visitor is still. That trio reads as *alive* far beyond its cost. Dear version to avoid for now: skeletal animation, creatures that pathfind across rooms, per-creature spatial audio (the audio budget allows at most 3 fully spatial sources at once). Counts stay tiny — a dozen beings, drawn as one merged batch, inside the particle caps. Every population passes Carter's own headset check before it ships, per the seed-built beings ruling — and creatures are ambient invented life, labelled by context, never presented as artifacts or period fact (Accuracy Floor).

**Day and weather — mostly already designed; buy it in the cheap order.** The pilot rooms already *are* different hours: Rotunda at dawn, Egypt court at hard noon, Mesopotamia at dusk. Because the museum's light is baked (painted in beforehand, not calculated live), a true moving day cycle is dear — it fights the architecture. The cheap, honest purchases, in order: (1) sky and shaft changes — sky colour, the oculus shaft's angle and warmth — which read as "time" without touching a single baked light; (2) air — dust drift thickening or thinning, distant thunder as a sound with a matching skylight glow pulse (already the specified way to do lightning); (3) weather only where weather can truthfully be — the open courts, never the burial chamber. Rain is last and optional: particles, audio, and wet-looking materials are three budgets for one effect. *Recommendation: name three fixed "hours" per outdoor space and let a slow drift move between two of them; skip rain in the pilot entirely.*

**Reasons to keep walking, already in the design — make sure they ship:** visited wings glow faintly in the Rotunda floor mosaic; the golden thread brightens at junctions; your own light quietly grows with rooms visited (radiance — private, never displayed to others, which is the right ethics and, incidentally, the right retention mechanic: progress you feel, not a leaderboard); the veiled portals are *named as withheld*, which turns honesty into intrigue. And sound: each room's ambience bed is drawn from its real content (desert wind and distant bronze hammers in Egypt, near-silence composed in the burial chamber) — a head-turn must pan the sound field; test it, because when it works it is half of presence and it costs almost nothing (the audio is synthesized, zero download weight).

**Best recommendation under this lens:** life before weather, and the Raven before everything living — one bird that can be followed teaches the visitor more about this museum than any menu, plaque, or tutorial.

## LENS 4 — DESIGN: the visual and spatial craft

**The floating glass deck — diagnosis and fix.** The museum uses no live shadows at all (correctly — they are unaffordable); every shadow is baked. A glass deck over a pit is the one structure that most needs a shadow to believe in: glass reads by its edges and its supports, and without a contact shadow — the darkening where a thing meets what holds it — the eye files it as hovering. Fixes, all cheap, all baked: (1) a painted contact-shadow gradient under the deck's rim and along every support, darkest at the contact line; (2) a visible load path — beams or columns the eye can follow down into the pit, so the deck is *held*, not floating; (3) an edge treatment that catches the light (bronze trim, per house identity), because a lit edge against a dark pit is how glass announces itself; (4) the pit below graded darker with distance, so depth reads. Generalize this into a standing rule: **anything that bridges, hovers, or cantilevers gets a baked contact shadow and a visible load path, or it doesn't ship.**

**Ground at your feet.** In a headset, the floor at your feet is the most-studied surface in the building — it is what you look at while aiming every teleport. It must hold at half a metre: floor grain and pattern resolution near the feet, the landing ring settling *on* the floor (not sunk, not hovering), the mosaic and the golden thread inlaid *in* the floor plane. Carter asked for this in flat and VR alike, and he is right in both: on the flat side the same floor is what the camera skims. One floor standard, two modes.

**Light and grade.** Chiaroscuro here is a ratio, not a darkness level: focal points at least 3× brighter than their surround, shadows lifted, no focal area ever below the readable floor — graded for the Quest 2's dimmer panel, the weakest display in the chain. Light zones ease over 2–3 seconds as pure atmosphere around a visitor who is standing still or walking under their own power; that ease is never transit and never moves anyone. Three transitions exist and must never blur into each other: the teleport dissolve (150–250 ms), the portal swap (masked, ≤ 400 ms of work), and the light-and-sound cross-fade (2–3 s). A hard cut anywhere is a craft failure.

**Materials and the close zoom.** Hero surfaces — relics, faces, focal plaques — earn real material treatment (roughness, metalness, subtle surface relief); everything else takes the cheap matte material, per the budget. The Shiva floor applies in-headset as an *arm's-reach hold*: anything brought within inspection distance must hold the way Shiva's face holds at close zoom. And the slab rule, learned from the flat side's runestone failure: no two slabs share a generic stone finish — the Merneptah Stele, the Rosetta Stone, and the Cyrus Cylinder are three different stones from three different worlds. Distant stand-ins are silhouettes only, never fake inscription — a distant text that can't be rendered truly is rendered as stone.

**Scale and proportion.** The pilot's numbers compose well: 3.2 m arches (monumental but human), 1.1 m plinths (gaze falls naturally), 8 m hypostyle columns (a forest of stone), the 2.6 m burial-chamber ceiling as deliberate compression after monumentality. Keep the dual-scale law: every wing makes the visitor feel small at least once — by scale contrast, never by polygon count.

**Wayfinding and identity.** The Rotunda must explain the whole museum in ten seconds of looking: nine arches, keystone emblems, the mosaic floor mirroring them, the lectern between. Threshold steles orient; signage is carved, never pasted. Wayfinding never relies on colour alone — emblems differ in shape as well as hue, and the thread carries a dashed inlay as its second channel. The parchment-and-bronze identity carries into 3D as a discipline, not a texture: every interface is a physical object in the world (the lectern book, brass plaques, stone steles), one typeface treatment, one gold, no floating panels, no science-fiction chrome. A visitor should feel inside getconexto.com, not inside a game engine's demo.

**Best recommendation under this lens:** the grounding pass — contact shadows, load paths, floor-at-feet — is the highest craft-per-cost work in the whole build-out, because Carter's eye has already found its absence twice (deck, ground) in one short walk.

## LENS 5 — CONTROL: the input scheme itself

Movement is Lens 2; this lens is everything the hands do that isn't travel.

**The one risky idea, well-guarded.** Tap and hold share a single trigger: a quick press selects, a press held past 200 ms becomes the teleport arc. This is elegant and dangerous in equal measure — it is the control a first-timer is most likely to fumble, and a fumble here (selecting when you meant to travel, travelling when you meant to select) teaches distrust of every other control. The design already contains the right mitigations: the arc's appearance is itself the signal that the press changed meaning, a hold never fires select, and the Start Here practice ring teaches both halves before either matters. *Recommendation: treat the practice ring as load-bearing, not decorative — a first-time visitor should have tapped once and teleported once, successfully, inside their first minute, on surfaces where nothing can go wrong.*

**Buttons with single meanings.** X summons the book (your directory, carried in the left hand); B closes one level at a time, then opens the room's "Here" page when nothing is open; Y returns to the Rotunda — one press, from anywhere, always. Y's single meaning is sacred: an earlier draft double-bound it (long-press for a comfort break) and the amendment struck that, correctly. The comfort break lives on the settings stele and the book's comfort page instead. A/trigger taps select; grip lifts a relic's facsimile — "what is ancient stays where it rests; hold its shadow" — and two grips scale and rotate it. Releasing always returns it along an eased path; nothing can be thrown in the Archives, which is both a comfort rule and a house rule.

**Reading up close.** Plaques are read by *touch*, not by clicking: bringing a controller tip or fingertip to a plaque magnifies it to reading size; withdrawing half a metre shrinks it back. Text holds the Warden's minimums — body at least 1.3° of visual angle, read from 0.8–2.5 m, high contrast measured on the worst-lit plaque in the building. Plaques auto-face the reader. This is the museum's core verb after walking, and it is correctly designed as contact rather than pointing.

**The beam that hides itself.** The selection beam — a soft shaft of warm light, not a laser — appears only while a hand is raised above the waist, so contemplation is never interrupted by interface chrome. The risk is the inverse: a first-timer with hands at their sides faces an invisible verb. The spec answers it (every Start Here surface responds to near-field touch with no beam raised), and the answer is right — but it makes the practice ring doubly load-bearing.

**One hand, no hands, lost hands.** Every action — including directory, close, and the return-to-Rotunda recovery — is reachable on the dominant controller alone, remappable in settings; that completeness is an acceptance test, not a claim, and the pilot must pass a one-handed run and a gaze-only run of the *full* pilot, not just the core loop. Gaze-and-dwell selection (rest your eyes on a thing for a slow count) exists only as that accessibility opt-in — rightly rejected as a default, since a museum is a place for looking and dwell punishes lingering. When hand tracking is lost for more than a moment and a half, control falls back gracefully — last pose, then gaze — with the change explained on the book in plain words, never as an error code. Controllers disconnecting mid-session offers re-pairing at the Rotunda stele or continuing by hand. Nothing strands.

**Settings a visitor can actually reach.** Comfort presets (Comfort / Balanced / Adventurous) switch live, mid-session, within two seconds; settings persist on the device only. The four sound buses (master, ambience, positional sounds, interface sounds) sit on a physical panel, not a floating menu. First-session defaults are maximum comfort: teleport, 30° snap, vignette on, captions offered, seated prompt shown.

**Best recommendation under this lens:** run the two acceptance passes — one-handed and gaze-only, full pilot — early enough that their findings are cheap. Input schemes fail at the edges (one hand, tired hands, lost tracking), and the edges are where this audience lives.

## THE BUILD-OUT ORDER — next five pieces

Sized for the loop's piece-by-piece cadence. The G3–G5 frame stands; the order re-cuts where Carter's walk demands it. Each piece names the lens it serves.

1. **The height fix, with the readout built in.** *(Movement)* — Find and fix the low eye height using the spawn-and-teleport readout (granted reference space + camera height at spawn and after one teleport), and keep the readout in the debug overlay forever, so height can never silently substitute again. First because a wrong eye height poisons every later judgment — Carter's included — about scale, decks, plaques, and doorways.
2. **G3: the measuring instrument, the light diet, portal culling.** *(Operability)* — The in-headset perf overlay, the scene cut to five lights on the floor tier, doorway-based room residency, explicit render scale and foveation on Quest 2 — certified on the worst sightline (the atrium seeing both pilot rooms) at p95 ≤ 13.9 ms. Second because rooms are about to be filled, and filling them before the budget is enforced builds the overrun in.
3. **The grounding pass.** *(Design)* — Baked contact shadows and a visible load path for the glass deck; floor-at-your-feet brought to standard in VR and flat alike; the standing rule written down: nothing bridges or hovers without a contact shadow and a load path. Third because Carter's eye has already caught these two faults, they are cheap (painted textures, no new systems), and every room built afterward inherits the fix instead of the fault.
4. **G5: the pilot spaces, with G4's curtain built as Egypt's door-piece.** *(Engagement, built to Design's standard)* — Rotunda, Egypt, Mesopotamia to the spec's sightlines and light zones; the curtain ships as part of Egypt's build, since a curtain only means anything attached to the room it curtains — Egypt curtained in public VR per Carter's ruling until his preview walk clears it, and tonight's walk is on record for that conversation. The Start Here practice ring lands here too, teaching tap-and-hold before either matters. Fourth because content is what the first three pieces exist to make possible.
5. **First life: the Raven, the Keeper, one reactive population.** *(Engagement)* — The Raven on its Rotunda-to-Table loop with its wake; the Keeper standing whole at his Table; one seed-built species in one court with the three cheap reactions (scatter, drift, settle), passed by Carter's own headset check. Fifth because life animates rooms that already work, measure, and ground — and because a living museum is the thing visitors will describe to other visitors.

**Named next, after the five:** the day/weather drift for the open courts (sky and shaft first, air second, rain last if ever); the remaining G4 curtain *policy* questions for rooms beyond Egypt as they approach review; smooth-locomotion tuning for the opt-in minority; living exhibits inside their micro-budgets.
