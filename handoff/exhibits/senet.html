The game of passage

Senet · the afterlife board

Five pieces begin among the living. Cast the sticks, cross the thirty houses, and pass every soul beyond the board.

The ancient rules did not survive intact. This playable version uses Timothy Kendall’s widely used scholarly reconstruction and marks its uncertainty plainly.

Kendall reconstruction

How to pass beyond

Cast four sticks. Count the light faces. No light faces count as five. Throws of 1, 4, or 5 grant another turn.

⇄

Move one pawn along the S-shaped path. Landing on an unprotected opponent swaps your places. Two adjacent allies protect one another; three make a wall that cannot be crossed.

𓋹≋𓂀

The final houses change fate. House 26 is Rebirth. House 27 is the Waters: a pawn landing there is sent back to House 15. Houses 28, 29, and 30 require exact throws of 3, 2, and 1 to pass beyond.

→☼

First to bear off all five pawns wins. Select a glowing pawn after each cast. If no legal move exists, the turn passes.

The archaeological record

What survives

The board is ancient. Senet appears in Egypt by the late fourth millennium BCE. Four game boxes were placed in Tutankhamun’s tomb, including a finely worked ebony-and-ivory example.

Its meaning changed. By the New Kingdom, tomb scenes and the vignette accompanying Spell 17 of the Book of the Dead connect play with the deceased person’s successful passage through the afterlife.

The rules are reconstructed. No complete ancient rulebook survives. The movement, protected groups, special houses, and bearing-off system used here follow Egyptologist Timothy Kendall’s influential reconstruction. It is a serious scholarly model, not a certainty.

Primary record: senet boards from the tomb of Tutankhamun; New Kingdom Book of the Dead, Spell 17 vignettes. Reconstruction: Timothy Kendall, Passing Through the Netherworld.
