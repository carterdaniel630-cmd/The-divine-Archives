Alquerque

The ancestor of draughts — after the Libro de los juegos of Alfonso X, 1283

The board attested · 1283

  • Twenty-five points in a five-by-five lattice, joined by horizontal, vertical and diagonal lines. Pieces rest on the intersections, never in the squares.

Setting out attested · 1283

  • Twelve pieces a side. Each player fills the two ranks nearest them, plus the two rightmost points of the middle rank as seen from their own seat. The centre point begins empty.

Moving & taking attested · 1283

  • A piece steps one point along any marked line to an empty point.
  • A piece captures by leaping over an adjacent enemy piece onto the empty point immediately beyond; the leapt piece is lifted from the board.
  • If a capture is open, it must be taken. Further leaps in the same turn are compulsory, and the piece may turn at each landing.
  • The game is won by taking every enemy piece, or by leaving the enemy with no legal move.

Reconstructed detail interpretation

  • The diagonals follow the standard modern reconstruction of the manuscript diagram: one diagonal per square, alternating direction.
  • Where several captures are open, the player may choose freely among them; each chosen chain must still be played to its end. Alfonso’s text is ambiguous here — this follows the reading of the Cyningstan reconstruction.
  • Not used: huffing, forced longest-capture, promotion, or forward-only movement — later additions, absent from the 1283 account.

What is not certain unverified

  • Popular sources claim the game was played in Egypt as early as 1400 BC. No excavated board securely identified as alquerque supports this. The firm record begins with qirkat, named without rules in a 10th-century Arabic work, and the complete rules of 1283.
  • Simplified in this exhibit: no draw adjudication, and the carnelian opponent reasons only two moves deep.
