Rithmomachia

The Philosopher’s Game — arithmos (number) × mache (battle): “the battle of the numbers.”

Played in monastery schools from the eleventh century to teach the number theory of Boethius. You command the White (even) pieces against the machine’s Black (odd) pieces. White moves first, by modern convention for this board.

Vellum board, iron-gall grid, gold-leaf numbers — after the manuscript diagrams of the medieval game.

    The Rules, as this board keeps them

    The pieces attested

    Each side fields twelve numbered pieces in three shapes. White holds the evens, Black the odds — the two armies are deliberately not symmetrical, so different captures are open to each player. The numbers follow the medieval Boethian scheme: the rounds are the plain numbers, the triangles their squares, the squares their compounds.

    Movement one telling

    Rounds step one square orthogonally; triangles stride exactly two squares orthogonally; squares stride exactly three squares orthogonally. Longer strides need a clear path — no leaping over other pieces. Triangles and squares may not stop short: the full stride or nothing.

    Labelled honestly: the sources were never standardized. Some manuscripts move rounds diagonally; this board uses the orthogonal telling (after Richards, 1946, on Boissière). Triangles and squares moving exactly two and three squares orthogonally is the common medieval account.

    The three captures attested in kind simplified in letter

    Captures are the soul of the game: pieces fall not by position alone but by arithmetical relation. Encounter resolves the instant you land. Ambush and siege are claimed at the start of your turn whenever the trap stands complete — spring them with your own move, or let your opponent walk into them.

    1. Encounter. Land upon an enemy piece and take it — but only if the numbers stand in a simple ratio: the two values are equal, or the victim’s value is a multiple of your piece’s.

    Example: Black ●5 lands on White △25. Since 25 = 5 × 5, the triangle falls. simplified — the medieval Meeting asked only for equal values and did not replace the piece; the multiple-rule blends in the spirit of the old Assault so the small pieces can hunt the great.

    2. Ambush. If an enemy piece stands between two of yours on opposite orthogonal sides, and the sum or the difference of your two numbers equals the enemy’s number, it is taken.

    Example: White ●2 and ●6 flank Black ●8 — 2 + 6 = 8, taken. Or White △25 and ●9 flank Black △16 — 25 − 9 = 16, taken. simplified — old Ambuscade texts require the victim to lie within a move of both attackers; this board asks only for the flanking and the arithmetic.

    3. Siege. If an enemy piece is surrounded on all four orthogonal sides by your pieces, it is removed.

    Example: your four pieces stand north, south, east and west of Black ■66 — the square is taken. simplified — pieces on the board’s edge cannot be fully ringed on this board and are immune to siege, by our ruling.

    Captured pieces leave the board here. Some accounts instead have them change sides — noted, not used.

    Victory simplified
    Simplified victory — read before playing. The historical game’s full victories are famously intricate (see below). For a learnable game, choose one at the start:
    De bonis (“by goods”) — first to capture pieces totalling 60 or more wins.
    De corpore (“by body”) — first to capture 4 pieces wins.

    Beyond this board lie the proper victories: Victoria magna, major, and excellentissima, won by arranging pieces on the far side of the board in arithmetic, geometric, and harmonic progressions — the very proportions of Boethian number theory. The common victories de lite, de honore, and de honore liteque added further conditions on the digits and count of the spoils. Those are for your second hundred games.

    Scholar’s note — what is attested, what is ours

    Attested: an 8×16 board; numbered rounds, triangles, squares (and pyramids); White evens against Black odds; movement by shape (1 / 2 / 3); captures by arithmetical relation — meeting, assault, ambuscade, siege; victories de corpore, de bonis, de lite, de honore, and the progression victories. First described c. 1030 by the monk Asilo (perhaps the future Bishop Adalbero of Würzburg) to illustrate Boethius’s De institutione arithmetica; rules refined by Hermannus Contractus (1013–1054) and the school of Liège; printed treatises through the sixteenth century (Boissière 1554, Fulke 1563, Barozzi 1572). Modern scholarship: Ann E. Moyer, The Philosopher’s Game (2001); Arno Borst; Jurgen Stigter in the British Museum’s Ancient Board Games in Perspective (2007).

    Ours (simplified, and why): landing-capture with the equal-or-multiple rule (playability — the strict medieval meeting rarely triggers); ambush as pure flanking arithmetic (clarity); siege requiring all four sides with edge immunity (decidability); the two short victories above (the true progression victories demand a long match arc — these teach the arithmetic core in an evening); the 12-piece table without pyramids (the full 24-piece array plus pyramids is a deeper game); orthogonal rounds (one documented telling among several). Uncertain: several compound values, the exact opening array, and round movement vary between manuscripts — the rules were never standardized, and this board does not pretend otherwise.

    Choose your victory

    The historical victories fill treatises. For this board, the schoolmaster allows two short roads — both simplified:

    You play White (evens) and move first. The machine plays Black (odds).
