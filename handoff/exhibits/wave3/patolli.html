PATOLLI

The sacred race-game of beans and stones, played under the eyes of Macuilxochitl, Five Flower, patron of games

⚠ Reconstructed rules — read before playing

No complete set of patolli rules survives the conquest. The cross-shaped board on a woven mat, the five one-side-marked beans, the red and blue stones, the gambling rites, and the patronage of Macuilxochitl are attested in codices and Spanish chronicles.

The 52-square circuit, the entries, the captures, the safe houses, the “ten” throw, and the tribute in counters are modern scholarly reconstructions — honest best guesses, not the original game. Play this as an homage, never as a recovered original.

Record of the Game

The rules — what is attested, what is reconstructed

Attested

  • A cross-shaped track painted on a woven reed mat — described by Sahagún and Durán, and painted in the Codex Borgia.
  • Dice of beans — commonly five — each marked on one side with a drilled hole showing the bean’s pale heart. Chroniclers mention anywhere from three to ten.
  • Twelve small stone pieces, six red and six blue, divided between two players (Durán).
  • High gambling: cloaks, gold beads, turquoises, precious stones. Players roamed with their rolled mats under their arms, seeking opponents (Durán, Sahagún).
  • Played under Macuilxochitl, “Five Flower,” god of games — invoked by the players, his favor courted, gaming gear kept on shrines.
  • After the conquest the Spanish suppressed the game as idolatrous gambling; its gear was burned. No complete rule set survives.

Reconstructed

  • A 52-square circuit — the count echoes the 52-year Calendar Round. R. C. Bell’s 1960 reconstruction used 60 instead. The true count is lost.
  • Six stones per side on one shared loop, entering at opposite houses.
  • A cast showing no marked faces counts ten and grants another cast.
  • Exact count to bear a stone off; landing on a rival’s stone captures it back to rest.
  • The four triangular-marked houses at the arm tips are safe — the marks are attested, their meaning is our guess.
  • Entry and capture tributes paid in bean counters to the bowl and the captor.
  • The rival is a heuristic opponent; the pacing and presentation are modern.

How a turn works in this exhibit

  1. Cast the five beans — marked faces count 1–5. No marked faces counts ten, and you cast again.
  2. Move one stone forward that many houses. A stone entering the board runs straight on.
  3. Capture: landing on a rival’s stone (outside a marked house) sends it back to rest, and a counter changes hands.
  4. Bear off with an exact count. First to bear off all six stones takes the offering bowl.

For its players, patolli was not a pastime but a rite of chance. Every cast was made under the eyes of Macuilxochitl, Five Flower — patron of games, music, and dance — and the stakes were laid the way offerings are laid. Loss was borne with gravity, not shrugged off. This exhibit keeps that gravity: there are no jackpots here, only stones, beans, and the god’s attention. Play it the way they did — with respect.
