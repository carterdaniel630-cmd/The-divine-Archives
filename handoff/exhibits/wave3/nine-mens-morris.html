The Play

  1. Placement Take turns setting your nine pieces on empty points.
  2. Close a mill — three of yours in a row along a line — and remove one enemy piece. Pieces inside mills are safe, unless the enemy holds only mills.
  3. Movement Slide one piece per turn along a line to an empty neighboring point. Mills still capture.
  4. Flight Reduced to exactly three pieces, your stones may leap to any empty point.
  5. Win by leaving the enemy with two pieces — or with no legal move. Opening and re-closing a mill is lawful play.

What Is Attested · What Is Reconstructed

Attested

The board itself — three concentric squares joined at their midpoints, twenty-four points — is carved into stone across the ancient world: Egyptian temple masonry, Roman-era building stone, and the 3rd-century Abhayagiri monastery in Sri Lanka. (The exact dates of individual carvings are often uncertain; the pattern’s antiquity is not.)

Ovid’s Ars Amatoria describes a Roman board game some scholars link to this family, and Shakespeare names “nine men’s morris” outright. The board is ancient beyond doubt.

Reconstructed

No Roman rulebook survives. The rules you play here are the standard game first written down in full in the Middle Ages — Alfonso X’s Libro de los juegos (1283) — and still played today: nine placements, mill-capture, mill protection, flight at exactly three, defeat at two pieces or blockade.

We play Rome’s board by the medieval book. That continuity is our reconstruction, stated plainly. Bronze against basalt is this exhibit’s artistry — not an attested Roman pairing.

Curator’s Note

  1. Rules source: the standard game first fully documented in Alfonso X’s Libro de los juegos (1283); the board diagram is attested on ancient stone.
  2. Simplified: the house plays a 1-ply heuristic with a shallow threat check (mill completion, blocking, potential, mobility) — no deep search, phone-safe. No draw-by-repetition rule, as in standard casual play.
  3. Uncertainty: no Roman rulebook survives — flight at three, mill protection, and the defeat conditions are medieval/modern attestations; their Roman-era form is unknown.
  4. Opening and re-closing a mill (“shuttling”) to capture repeatedly is lawful in the rules implemented.
  5. Bronze-vs-basalt pieces are exhibit artistry, not an attested Roman pairing.
