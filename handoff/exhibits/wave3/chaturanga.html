Chaturanga

The fourfold army of ancient India · c. 6th century, Gupta age — the ancestor of chess

What we know, and what we rebuilt

Chaturanga’s exact sixth-century rules are lost. No complete rulebook survives from the Gupta age. This exhibit follows the standard scholarly diceless reconstruction (after H. J. R. Murray, A History of Chess, 1913, the convention most historians still use). Everything below marked Reconstructed is an educated restoration — never quote it as historical fact.

Attested

  • The 8×8 aṣṭāpada board (Bāṇabhaṭṭa’s Harṣacarita, 7th c.)
  • The fourfold army: foot-soldiers, horses, elephants, chariots — plus the rājā
  • The rājā moves one square in any direction
  • No castling; the game is won by checkmate

Later sources

  • Aśva (horse) leaps as the modern knight
  • Ratha (chariot) runs straight, any distance
  • Recorded in medieval Indian & Persian accounts

Reconstructed

  • Mantri: one square diagonally
  • Gaja: exactly two squares diagonally, leaping
  • Padāti: no double step; promotes to mantri only
  • Stalemate & bare-king wins (sources differ — see rules)
  • The plain uncheckered sandalwood board shown here

Chronicle of moves

    The laws of the game

    The board Reconstructed

    Eight squares by eight, uncheckered in the Indian manner — the plain sandalwood you see here is our presentation choice.

    Rājā — the king Attested

    One square in any direction. There is no castling — it did not exist.

    Mantri — the counselor Reconstructed

    One square diagonally. Feeble now; fifteen centuries later this piece becomes the all-powerful queen.

    Gaja — the elephant Reconstructed

    Exactly two squares diagonally, leaping over whatever stands between. Ancient texts describe several elephant-moves; this is Murray’s reading.

    Aśva — the horse Later sources

    The knight’s leap, unchanged for well over a thousand years.

    Ratha — the chariot Later sources

    Any distance along rank or file, like the modern rook.

    Padāti — the foot-soldier Reconstructed

    One step forward; captures one square diagonally forward. No double first step, so no en passant. Reaching the far rank, it is raised to mantri — and only mantri.

    Victory Reconstructed

    Checkmate the enemy rājā — or bare him: strip away every piece until the king stands alone, and the game is yours at once.

    Stalemate Reconstructed · disputed

    Here the stalemating side wins — the common modern-reconstruction convention, shared with Arab chess. But the sources genuinely differ: Murray (1903) records an early Indian rule awarding the win to the stalemated side instead. We flag the disagreement rather than hide it.

    You command ivory and move first — a modern convention, since the old texts name no first player. The bronze army is guided by a modest heuristic mind: it counts material, likes the center, and guards its king. It does not see deeply; a patient human can outwit it.

    Carved in the Gupta manner — sandalwood, ivory, bronze. The pieces are stylized abstractions, not Staunton chessmen;
