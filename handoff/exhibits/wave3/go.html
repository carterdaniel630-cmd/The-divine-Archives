How to play
  1. AttestedBlack plays first. Alternate placing one stone per turn on any empty intersection. You are Black; the white stones are the house opponent.
  2. AttestedStones of one colour joined along the lines form a group. Empty neighbouring intersections are its liberties. A group whose last liberty is filled is captured and lifted from the board.
  3. SimplifiedSuicide is prohibited. You may not play where your own group would be left with no liberties. Some historical rulesets permit suicide; this teaching game forbids it for clarity.
  4. AttestedKo: no move may immediately repeat the previous board position — a single-stone capture cannot be recaptured on the very next move.
  5. AttestedPassing is allowed. Two consecutive passes end the game, and the board is scored.
  6. SimplifiedScoring is Chinese-style area scoring: stones on the board plus empty points wholly surrounded by your stones. White receives 7.5 points komi for moving second.

The traditional full board is 19×19. This exhibit uses the 9×9 teaching size — the standard board for learning, with the same rules of capture.

About the white player

White is a beginner sparring partner, and is labelled as such honestly. It looks exactly one move ahead: it prefers captures, defends groups that are short of liberties, takes open corners early, and avoids filling its own territory late. It does not plan, does not read ladders, and understands nothing of large strategy or endgame.

Strong go play requires deep search and vast reading; this exhibit deliberately ships a modest opponent so the whole game runs instantly on a phone, with no server and no downloads. If it beats you, the fundamentals are working. If you beat it, that is the expected outcome — congratulations are in order either way.

Lore of the board

In China the game is weiqi (围棋), "the surrounding game"; in Korea baduk, in Japan igo. It is mentioned in the Confucian Analects — textual attestation reaching back some two and a half millennia — while the familiar legend crediting its invention to the Emperor Yao remains legend, not history.

Confucian tradition counted go among the four cultivated arts — lute, go, calligraphy, painting — a discipline for reading another mind and governing one's own. (A traditional association, recorded in the histories of the literati.)

A Daoist reading, offered here as contemplation rather than claim: the empty board as uncarved simplicity, black and white as yin and yang in mutual becoming, and good play as wu-wei — response without forcing. (An interpretive framing, not a historical assertion.)

The game travelled with monks and envoys to Korea and reached Japan by the 7th century CE, where — centuries later — the Tokugawa shogunate's four great go houses, the Honinbo foremost among them, raised its study to a profession.
