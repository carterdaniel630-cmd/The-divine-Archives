Reading the coil

The illuminated rings are legal destinations. Land on an opposing piece to return it beyond the tail. A lion must reach the serpent's head by an exact cast; the first player to bring all three there wins.

Reconstruction, not recovered law

No ancient rulebook survives. This playable ruleset is a modern, transparent race-game reconstruction inspired by Timothy Kendall's study of Egyptian serpent games—not a claim about exactly how Mehen was played.

Mehen: record and reconstruction

What survives

Stone boards shaped as a coiled serpent, small spherical counters, and lion or lioness pieces survive from Egypt's Predynastic and Early Dynastic periods. Images in Old Kingdom tombs also show people beside coiled-serpent boards.

What the serpent meant

Mehen—“the Coiled One”—appears in Egyptian religious texts as a protective serpent surrounding the solar god Ra during his nocturnal journey. The relation between the deity and every rule of the board game cannot be proven.

What is lost

No Egyptian instructions are known. Scholars including Timothy Kendall have proposed playable reconstructions from boards, piece sets, and comparative game structures. This version simplifies that scholarship into an inward race with captures and an exact finish. Its certainty ends where the evidence ends.

Chronology

Mehen is attested by the late fourth and third millennia BCE. It flourished before disappearing from the archaeological record around the end of the Old Kingdom.
