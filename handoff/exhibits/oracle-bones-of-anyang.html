    The surviving record

    What the bones preserve

    At Yinxu, the last Shang capital near modern Anyang, diviners prepared ox scapulae and turtle plastrons, applied intense heat, read the resulting cracks, and cut records into the surface. The inscriptions form the earliest substantial corpus of Chinese writing. They name kings and diviners and record concerns ranging from rain and harvests to warfare, hunting, illness, sacrifice, and childbirth.

    The ideal record could include a date and diviner, the charge posed to the ancestors, a royal prognostication, and—sometimes—a later note of what happened.

    Modeled specimen

    Cambridge University Library CUL.52

    The central study follows this 9 × 14 cm Yinxu ox-scapula specimen, digitized as a 1.3-million-facet surface model. Its thin blade, surviving contour, raised scapular anatomy, heat scars, prepared reverse pits, and obverse inscription field govern the model. Use the face control above the specimen to examine the inscribed obverse and the prepared, heat-scarred reverse separately.
