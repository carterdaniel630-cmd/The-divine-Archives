What you are holding — honestly

Original silver tablets lost. Text survives in Hittite cuneiform copies and Egyptian temple-wall versions. Each king sent the other his version engraved on a silver tablet. Neither silver tablet has ever been found.

This is the Hittite record form: a clay tablet in Akkadian cuneiform, the diplomatic language of the day, like the copies kept in the archives of the Hittite capital, Hattusa. Three such tablets were found there in 1906: two are in Istanbul, one in Berlin.

The script on this model is representative, not readable. The wedges are pressed the way real cuneiform is (a triangular head and a tapering tail, in horizontal, vertical, slanting and corner shapes), laid out in ruled lines with a line between sections. It does not spell out the treaty. The tablet’s shape and size are a reconstruction too, not a scan of any one copy.

What the evidence shows

Well supported

  • Ramesses II of Egypt and Hattusili III of Hatti made a formal, sworn treaty; the Egyptian copies date it to Ramesses’s 21st regnal year.
  • It survives in two independent forms: Akkadian cuneiform tablets from Hattusa, and hieroglyphic versions carved at Karnak and the Ramesseum.
  • Its terms: lasting peace and brotherhood, no attacks, help against enemies, the return of fugitives with a promise of mercy, and the gods of both lands as witnesses. The Hittite version adds Egypt’s backing for Hattusili’s chosen heir.
  • The city of Kadesh is never named in the treaty. “Treaty of Kadesh” is a modern name taken from the battle about fifteen years before.
  • A new clay fragment from Hattusa (Büyükkale) was announced in September 2026, with lines from the fugitive articles, including a warning not to destroy refugees’ homes.

Not supported

  • That any surviving tablet is the original. Every known copy is clay or stone; the silver originals are lost.
  • That the two kings met to sign it. The treaty is thought to have been settled through envoys.

Still debated

  • The exact year. About 1259 BCE on the chronology most historians now use; the UN and Turkish museum labels give 1269 BCE, from an older dating of Ramesses’s reign.
  • Who asked for peace. The Egyptian walls show the Hittites asking, as if in submission. Many historians think Hattusili did open the talks, needing security at home (his deposed nephew had fled to Egypt) and against a rising Assyria, but both sides gained, and the Egyptian framing is royal display, not a neutral record.

Who made this peace

Ramesses II, pharaoh of Egypt, and Hattusili III, Great King of the Hittites. Egypt and Hatti had fought for generations over the lands of Syria between them, and the Battle of Kadesh (about 1274 BCE) ended without a clear winner. About fifteen years later the two kings swore this peace. It held until the Hittite kingdom fell, roughly eighty years on, and around Ramesses’s 34th year a Hittite princess married him.

The Egyptian version — the temple-wall companion

Representative carving in sunk relief. Not a copy of the real Karnak or Ramesseum text.

Ramesses had his version carved in hieroglyphs at Karnak (a long text on a wall south of the Great Hypostyle Hall) and at the Ramesseum (a shorter one). The Egyptian telling puts Egypt in the grander role; the Hittite clay copies are thought to be closer to the agreed wording. A copper relief replica, a gift from Türkiye in 1970, hangs near the Security Council at the United Nations in New York.
