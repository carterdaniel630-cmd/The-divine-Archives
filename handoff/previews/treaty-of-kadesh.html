What this is — honestly

The original treaty was written on a SILVER tablet. That silver tablet is lost — it has never been found. What you are holding here is the Hittite record form: a clay tablet in Akkadian cuneiform, the kind of copy kept in the Hittite capital’s archives at Hattusa (found there in 1906).

The inscription on this tablet is representative cuneiform — wedge-shaped marks in the style of the real tablets, pressed into clay. It is not a verbatim copy of the treaty’s real text. The clause readings above give the treaty’s substance in plain words.

What survives: the Hittite copies on clay tablets — two in Istanbul (Museum of the Ancient Orient), one in Berlin, smaller fragments in Ankara and St Petersburg, and a new fragment found at Hattusa in 2026. The Egyptian version survives carved in hieroglyphs on temple walls (see below). A copper replica hangs at the United Nations in New York.

Dates: about 1259 BCE (scholars differ by a few years). The Battle of Kadesh was about 1274 BCE; the treaty came roughly fifteen years later, in the 21st year of Ramesses II. Note: the city of Kadesh itself is not mentioned in the treaty’s text.

Who made this peace

Ramesses II, pharaoh of Egypt, and Hattusili III, Great King of the Hittites. Egypt and Hatti had fought for more than two centuries over the lands between them, and the Battle of Kadesh ended in a bloody stalemate. Both kings had new enemies to worry about — Egypt the Sea Peoples, Hatti the rising Assyrians — so they made peace instead. The two kings never met in person; go-betweens carried the terms.

The Egyptian version — the temple-wall companion

Representative temple-wall carving — not a copy of the real Karnak or Ramesseum walls.

The same treaty was carved in hieroglyphs on the walls of two of Ramesses II’s temples in Thebes: the Ramesseum and the Temple of Karnak. The Egyptian carving tells the story with Egypt in the grander role — the Hittite version on clay is closer to the text the two sides actually agreed on. Two versions of one peace: the clay tablet you hold, and the temple wall.
