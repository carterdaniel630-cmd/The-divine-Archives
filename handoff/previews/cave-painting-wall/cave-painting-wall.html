HOW THEY PAINTED

Pigment. Red and yellow ochre are iron-rich earths, ground on stone; black is charcoal or manganese. Mixed with water, animal fat, or clay, they became paint that has lasted forty thousand years.

The hand. Many pressed pigment-covered palms straight onto the rock. Others held a hand flat and blew paint around it through a hollow bone — a negative stencil, the oldest signature we know: I was here.

The wall. Painters chose stone that was already alive — bulges and hollows that became an animal's shoulder, a bison's hump. They worked by firelight, deep underground, where no one lived.

The meaning. We do not know. Hunting magic, spirits, stories — every reading is a guess, and honest scholars say so. What is certain is that someone, in the dark, reached out a hand and made a mark that outlived them.

Tens of thousands of years ago, deep underground by firelight, people ground red earth and charcoal between stones, and painted the walls with their fingers, with brushes of hair, with pigment blown through hollow bone.
