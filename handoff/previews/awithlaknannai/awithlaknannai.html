About this reconstruction

  • Board. The short board drawn for Stewart Culin by Zuni consultant Nick Graham (1907): three rows — 8, 9 and 8 points — joined by leaning diagonals, the outer rows joined by lines (so three lines meet at each point), with a curve joining the ends.
  • Pieces. Culin's game used 12 small pottery discs per side, plain or with a pierced top to tell sides apart. Here: turquoise (pierced) against clay red (plain). The simpler 6-a-side set is a modern teaching setup — both are offered above.
  • Rules as documented. Move one stone along a line to a free neighbouring point; or leap over an adjacent enemy stone to the free point just beyond it, following the board's lines. Captures are compulsory, and a stone that can keep leaping must keep leaping. The first move must take the empty centre point — and is captured at once, so the first player starts at a disadvantage.
  • What the sources leave open. Culin never fully wrote down the win condition; following the standard reconstruction, you win by taking all the enemy stones, or by leaving your opponent with no legal move. "Fighting serpents" is R. C. Bell's English name (1969), not a Zuni phrase.
Sources: Stewart Culin, Games of the North American Indians, vol. 2: Games of Skill (1907); R. C. Bell, Board and Table Games from Many Civilizations (1969) and The Boardgame Book (1983); board-shape analysis after Culin's diagram and the Cyningstan reconstruction notes (2014). How to play

The board

  • Three rows of points — 8 on each outer row, 9 in the middle — 25 points in all, joined by leaning diagonal lines, with the outer rows joined end to end and a curve closing each end of the board.
  • Stones sit on the points and travel along the lines, never across empty space.

Moving

  • Step: move one of your stones along a line to an empty neighbouring point.
  • Leap (capture): jump one of your stones over an adjacent enemy stone onto the empty point immediately beyond it, following the board's lines. The jumped stone is removed.
  • Captures are compulsory. If any leap is available, you must take one — you may choose which.
  • Keep leaping. A stone that can leap again after landing must keep leaping until it cannot.
  • Leaps may change direction from one jump to the next, always following the lines.

Winning

  • Take every enemy stone, or leave your opponent with no legal move — a player who cannot move loses.
  • Note the opening trap: the only empty point is the centre, so the first move walks into a capture. The second player is favoured — Culin's sources say so too.

A note of respect

The Zuni (A:shiwi) people live in New Mexico today, and their traditions are their own. This page reconstructs a game described by outside observers in 1907; where the record is silent, the gaps are labelled as such above. Nothing here claims sacred meaning the game was never given.
