## 2. ROOM CONCEPT

**Atmosphere.** The room is a night sky made architectural. Deep indigo walls, a starfield ceiling, near-silence. The visitor steps out of the Modern wing's corridor — crowded with the manifestos and grimoires of the twentieth century — into something quieter and vaster. The governing idea of the room is *scale*: the book asks its reader to hold a cosmos of seven superuniverses and a billion perfect worlds in mind, and then to hold, in the same mind, a single inner light. The room moves between those two poles.

**Narrative arc.** The visitor's journey follows the book's own order of disclosure, framed at every step as *what the text presents*:
1. **The human mystery** — Chicago, 1911–1955: a psychiatrist who debunked mediums, an anonymous sleeper, a discussion group asking questions, papers arriving as answers. The archive presents the documented history and the movement's account side by side, clearly labeled, and leaves the verdict to the visitor.
2. **The cosmic vision** — Paradise at the center, Havona's billion worlds, the seven superuniverses wheeling around them, our local universe Nebadon with its ten million inhabited worlds. A vertical cosmology the visitor can walk beneath.
3. **The intimate turn** — the Thought Adjuster: a fragment of God lodged in every moral creature. The vastest room in the museum narrows, at its center, to a single point of light.
4. **The Jesus narrative** — the book's 775-page Part IV, presented strictly as the text's account, distinct from creedal Christianity.
5. **The living readers** — no churches, no clergy, study groups on five continents; the revelation as "leaven" for existing faiths rather than a new religion.

**Tone.** Reverent, scholarly, wonder-first — the house voice throughout. Every doctrinal claim is framed as "the text presents," "readers hold," or "the book teaches." The archive never asserts the book's claims as its own, and never debunks them in the room. Skeptical scholarship (Gardner, Gooch, stylometry, the unattributed-sources finding) is acknowledged once, in a single clearly-labeled "Questions scholars raise" panel near the exit — not as the room's voice, but as part of honest curation.

## 3. RELICS (6 display pieces)

### Relic 1 — The 1955 First Edition
- **What it is:** The book itself: 2,097 pages, 196 papers plus a Foreword, published 12 October 1955 in Chicago by the Urantia Foundation.
- **What the visitor learns:** The sheer physical scale of the revelation; its four-part architecture (Central and Superuniverses / Local Universe / History of Urantia / Life and Teachings of Jesus); that it names no human author.
- **Display notes:** Facsimile copy on a lectern, open to the Foreword's definitions of Deity and divinity; visitors may page through the facsimile. Beside it, a closed first edition under glass with a caption giving the publication facts. Touching the facsimile is encouraged; the original is not for handling.

### Relic 2 — The Grand Universe (cosmology orrery)
- **What it is:** A large hanging or slowly rotating model of the book's cosmos: the stationary Isle of Paradise at the center, the billion worlds of Havona around it, and the seven superuniverses wheeling in the outer dark.
- **What the visitor learns:** The book's signature vision — reality as an ordered, inhabited, ascending architecture, not an empty void. The seven superuniverses, our local universe Nebadon (up to ten million inhabited worlds), and Earth's place within it as "Urantia."
- **Display notes:** Suspended from the starfield ceiling at the room's center; the visitor walks beneath it. Labels use the book's own terms (Paradise, Havona, superuniverse, Nebadon), each glossed once in plain language. Slow rotation only; no flashing.

### Relic 3 — The Seven Mansion Worlds (ascension path)
- **What it is:** The book's afterlife: after death, survivors awaken on the first of seven "mansion worlds," then ascend through constellation, local-universe, and superuniverse realms to Havona and finally Paradise. No hell, no reincarnation; the persistently wicked, the text teaches, become "as though they had never been."
- **What the visitor learns:** A distinctive eschatology — salvation as graduated education rather than judgment; "morontia" as the intervening level between material and spiritual ("the warp of morontia is spiritual; its woof is physical" — Foreword 0:5.12).
- **Display notes:** Seven ascending platforms or lit steps rising along one wall, each labeled (Mansion World One through Seven), lit from below, growing brighter toward the top. The visitor climbs them bodily. Keep the annihilation teaching in the panel text, phrased as the text's teaching, without sensationalism.

### Relic 4 — The Thought Adjuster (the inner light)
- **What it is:** The book's most intimate doctrine: a fragment of God — the "Mystery Monitor" — bestowed on each person at their first independent moral decision, a non-coercive inner guide. The soul, the text teaches, is the Adjuster joined with human will; fusion with it is eternal life.
- **What the visitor learns:** That the book's vast cosmology narrows to something personal: divinity as inward presence rather than distant throne.
- **Display notes:** A small, dark alcove at the room's center — the quietest point in the Modern wing. A single point of warm light in darkness. Panel text kept short. This is the room's emotional anchor; protect its silence (no audio, no adjacent interactives).

### Relic 5 — The Forum and the Contact Commission, Chicago 1925–1955
- **What it is:** The human story of the text's arrival: Dr. William S. Sadler — physician, psychiatrist, Seventh-day Adventist minister, and professional debunker of mediums — and his wife Lena; the anonymous "sleeping subject"; the Forum discussion group (formalized 1925, 486 participants over the years) posing written questions; the five-or-six-person Contact Commission custodizing the manuscripts at 533 Diversey Parkway; publication in 1955.
- **What the visitor learns:** How the book says it came to be — and where documented history and the movement's account agree and differ (dates, authorship theories), each clearly labeled.
- **Display notes:** A period Chicago study vignette: 1920s desk, typewriter, stacked manuscript pages, a wall of the Forum's questions. The sleeping subject is represented by an empty chair — his anonymity was oath-bound, and the archive honors it by not speculating on his identity here. A single panel, set apart, notes the scholarly questions (Gardner, Gooch, stylometry, Matthew Block's source studies) under the heading "Questions scholars raise."

### Relic 6 — The Jesus Papers (Part IV)
- **What it is:** The book's final and longest part: 775 pages on the life and teachings of Jesus — childhood, ministry, crucifixion, resurrection, and post-resurrection appearances. The text identifies Jesus as Christ Michael, a Creator Son of the local universe on his seventh bestowal — explicitly *not* the Second Person of the Trinity (which the book reserves for the Eternal Son). It denies the virgin birth and the atonement doctrine as later misunderstandings; its central theme is the "religion *of* Jesus" (personal faith, the fatherhood of God, the brotherhood of man, unselfish service) versus the later "religion *about* Jesus."
- **What the visitor learns:** Why the book gives Christianity pride of place while diverging from creedal doctrine — the single most important thing for a visitor to grasp about its relationship to other faiths.
- **Display notes:** Handle with maximum care. Present strictly as "the text presents." Do not place Christian symbols in a way that implies endorsement or correction. A long reading rail with short, attributed excerpts (public-domain English text — see §5). No imagery of Jesus that could read as the archive's own devotional statement; keep it textual.

### (Descriptive element, not a relic) — The Concentric Circles
- The three azure concentric circles on white — the symbol associated with the book and a registered trademark of the Urantia Foundation — may appear **once**, small, in an educational caption explaining the movement's emblem, with a trademark notice. **It must never be used as a room emblem, logo, site identifier, or in any way implying Foundation endorsement.** See §4 (mandatory).

## 5. COPYRIGHT NOTE

- The **1955 English text is in the public domain** per the Urantia Foundation's own published statement (international copyright expired January 2006). The room may quote it as extensively as curation requires, **with attribution** (paper and section numbers).
- **Translations remain copyrighted.** Quote **only** the English text. If a translation is ever needed, it falls under the Foundation's 25,000-word quotation policy (≤5,000 words consecutively, 1:1 quoted-to-original ratio, no alteration) — or seek permission.

## 6. SENSITIVITY NOTES (from the research — binding on the build)

1. **The text is inviolate.** Readers hold the 1950 Declaration of Trust's charge — to "perpetually preserve inviolate the text" — as sacred. Quote exactly; never silently modernize, abridge-and-attribute, or rearrange wording.
2. **Reject "cult" / "channeling" / "UFO" framing.** The movement explicitly disavows mediumship and channeling (Sadler: the reception "in no way parallels… any of the phenomena of the marginal consciousness"; the book itself denies the spiritual authenticity of channeling, pp. 865, 1646). Describe reception neutrally: "the movement says…; scholars classify it as…". Never use "cult" — even Gardner regretted the word in his own book title.
3. **Don't denominationalize.** Avoid "the Urantia church," "Urantians" as a sect label, or "the Urantia Bible" without the readers' own framing (a revelation meant to leaven all faiths, not a sect's scripture). Readers call themselves "readers" and "students"; many simultaneously identify as Christian, Muslim, Jewish, etc.
4. **Handle the race / biologic-uplift passages with care.** The book contains statements about human races that critics link to Sadler-era eugenicist thought. Note the scholarly criticism once, in the "Questions scholars raise" panel — do not amplify the passages, do not make them a relic, do not present them as the book's essence.
5. **The sleeping subject's anonymity is oath-bound.** All knowers are deceased. Do not present identity theories (e.g., Wilfred Kellogg) as fact anywhere in the room.
6. **No etymology for "Urantia."** The text gives none ("Urantia — that being the name of your world"). All derivations are reader speculation — say so if the name's origin comes up, or leave it alone.

### Text panels (draft copy, house voice — respectful, wonder-first, stoic)

**Entrance panel — "The Urantia Book"**
> Chicago, 1955. A book of 2,097 pages appeared, naming no human author. It presents itself as the fifth in a series of revelations to this world — which it calls *Urantia*. What follows is the book's account of itself, and the documented history of how it reached print. The archive asserts neither; it preserves both, clearly labeled.

**Relic 5 panel — "The Forum"**
> For thirty years, a Chicago discussion group met to ask questions — about God, the universe, the life of Jesus — and papers arrived as answers. The psychiatrist who hosted them had spent his career exposing fraudulent mediums; this, he said, was the one case he could not explain. The man through whom the papers came was never named, by oath. All who knew his name are gone, and the archive honors that silence.

**Relic 1 panel — "196 Papers"**
> The book is arranged in four parts: the central and superuniverses; the local universe; the history of Urantia; and the life and teachings of Jesus — the last alone running 775 pages. Readers hold the text inviolate: it is quoted exactly, never altered. The English text of 1955 is in the public domain; what you read here is the book's own wording.

**Relic 2 panel — "The Grand Universe"**
> The text presents reality as architecture: at the center, the stationary Isle of Paradise; around it, one billion perfect worlds called Havona; wheeling beyond them, seven superuniverses. Our local universe, Nebadon, holds — the text says — up to ten million inhabited worlds. Earth is one of them. The book calls it Urantia: that being the name, it says, of your world.

**Relic 3 panel — "The Mansion Worlds"**
> The book teaches no hell and no second earthly life. After death, it says, survivors awaken on the first of seven mansion worlds — schools, not punishments — and ascend, world by world, toward Paradise. Between the material and the spiritual, the text names an intervening level: *morontia*. "The warp of morontia is spiritual; its woof is physical."

**Relic 4 panel — "The Adjuster"**
> The book's smallest teaching may be its largest. It presents every moral creature as indwelt by a fragment of God — the Thought Adjuster, a silent, non-coercive guide given at the first moral choice. The soul, in this telling, is what a human will builds together with that inner light. Fusion with it is eternal life.

**Relic 6 panel — "The Jesus Papers"**
> More than a third of the book is a life of Jesus — presented here strictly as the text's account. It identifies him as Christ Michael, a Creator Son of the local universe, on his seventh bestowal to earn its sovereignty — distinct, in this telling, from the Second Person of the Trinity. It denies the virgin birth and the atonement as later misunderstandings, and sets the "religion *of* Jesus" — personal faith, the fatherhood of God, the brotherhood of man — against the later religion *about* him. Mainstream churches have issued no official response; some evangelical writers reject the book's account as unbiblical. The archive presents the text's claims without adopting them.

**Exit panel — "The Readers"**
> There are no Urantia churches and no clergy. Readers — who call themselves students, not converts — keep the faiths they had and treat the book as enrichment: in the words of one longtime reader, not a new religion among the family of religions, but leaven for them. Study groups meet on five continents. The book is not proselytized; it is, in the readers' phrase, for those who inquire.

**Exit panel — "Questions scholars raise"**
> Scholars of new religions classify the book among revelatory scriptures in the Spiritualist lineage. They note its unattributed use of early-twentieth-century sources (the book's own Foreword says it preferred "the highest existing human concepts"), its dated science (which the book itself anticipates), and passages on human races that critics connect to the eugenicist thought of its era. Its authorship remains unresolved: a stylometric study found signs of at least nine hands. These questions are part of the record, and the archive keeps them with it.
