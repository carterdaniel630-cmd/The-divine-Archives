# Rök Runestone (Rökstenen, Ög 136) — Worked Decoding

## Summary

The Rök stone is a granite slab at Rök church, Ödeshög, Östergötland, Sweden, carved **c. 800 CE / early 9th century** by **Varinn (Varin)** in memory of his dead son **Vámóðr (Vamoth / Væmoð)**. Its inscription — **about 760 characters in 28 lines on five faces** — is the longest runic inscription in stone. Scholars agree, unusually, on *what the runes are* and on the decipherment of the cipher passages, and disagree, unusually, on *reading order, word division, and what the text means*. There are therefore **two full translations that must not be conflated**:

1. **The standard Rundata translation** (Scandinavian Runic-text Database, Uppsala) — heroic/legendary reading, with Theodoric the Great, the twenty kings, the Ingold line and Vélinn/Vilinn. This is the translation on the Swedish National Heritage Board (Riksantikvarieämbetet, RAÄ) information signage, line by line.
2. **The Holmberg–Gräslund–Sundqvist–Williams translation (2020, CC-BY)** — a complete re-segmentation of the same runes into **nine riddling questions**, five answered "the sun" and four about Odin, linked to fear of a renewed climate crisis like that after 536 CE. It *deletes* Theodoric, Ingvald, Sibbi and Vilinn as persons.

Confidence: high (multiple primary/scholarly sources, fetched 2026-11-06 via web index) for layout, transliteration, cipher mechanics and both translations; low-to-none for any single *interpretation* of the riddles — no interpretation is consensus.

**A correction to the brief as posed:** there are no "sons of Ingvald" in the inscription. The passage asks about *one of the line/descent of Ingold/Ingvald* (Old Norse *Inguldinga*) repaid by a wife's sacrifice. The sons in the text are the twenty kings (four sets of five brothers) and, in the last line, the son begotten by Sibbi at ninety.

## Findings

### 1. Structure — layout, runes, writing systems, transliteration and translation in reading order

#### 1a. The object

- **Designation:** Ög 136 (Östergötland no. 136) in Rundata / the Scandinavian Runic-text Database; RAÄ monument Rök 1:1, L2011:9303. Granite, at Rök parish beside the church where it was found built into a wall in the 19th century (first documented in the 17th century, built into a medieval building by the church). Sources: Runkartan/Rundata, https://runkartan.se/english/rune-stone-rokstenen-og136-l2011-9303-rok-1-1-in-odeshog-ostergotland.html (index, read 2026-11-06); Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 8, https://archaeology.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/FULLTEXT01.pdf (index, read 2026-11-06).
- **Size/count — flag small disagreements:** "around 760 characters" and 2.4 m (8 ft) tall, 5.1 tons (Wikipedia, citing *Archaeology* 2020, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B6k_Runestone , index, read 2026-11-06); Uppsala University says 760 characters, inscriptions on all sides, 2.5 m high *above ground*, https://www.uu.se/en/news/2022/2022-01-25-deciphering-ancient-runic-messages (index, read 2026-11-06); Holmberg et al. say "more than 700 runes and other characters," five-ton slab, "over two and a half metres high above the ground" (2020, p. 7–8); Harris says "about 750 runes" (2010, see §4). Total height including the buried part is 382 cm, of which 125 cm is underground (RAÄ table reproduced at https://www.swedishnomad.com/sv/rokstenen/ , index, read 2026-11-06). Use "**c. 760 characters, c. 2.4–2.5 m above ground (3.82 m in total)**."
- **Faces and lines:** Covered on **five sides** — all except the base underground. In the conventional lettering (§A–§E in Rundata; A–E in Wessén/Harris/Holmberg):
  - **A** = front (broad face)
  - **B** = first narrow side (the Theodoric stanza finishes here, mid-sentence)
  - **C** = back (broad face; twenty kings, Elder-Futhark lines at the bottom, substitution-cipher lines, and the final *ul niruþʀ* clause)
  - **D** = second narrow side (the coordinate-cipher *sakum ukmini þur* line)
  - **E** = top face (the cross-cipher *sibi uia uari* line)
  
  Note Rundata's §-letters mark *where text physically sits*, not reading order — §E (Sibbi) is read before the §C tail (*ul niruþʀ*, "nonagenarian") that completes its sentence.
- **28 lines:** Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 17: "twenty-eight lines, of which twenty are vertical and eight more or less horizontal." The RAÄ signage presents the text in exactly 28 numbered lines (table in §1d below). Reading begins — consensus on this point — with the two large vertical lines on A naming Vámóðr and Varinn (lines 1–2), then proceeds left-to-right through the verticals, reading horizontal lines 9–10 where syntax requires. **Beyond the front, reading order is not consensus:** "no consensus has been reached on the reading order of the inscription. More than fifteen solutions have been proposed (cf. Lönnqvist 1999)" (Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 8). Holmberg's own proposed order (his Fig. 1): A 1–8 → A/B horizontals 9–10 → B 11 → C horizontal 12 first, then C verticals 13–23 counter-clockwise round the stone finishing on D 23, then top/upper-back horizontals 24–28 read from above.
- **No word spaces, no doubled runes:** The text is *scriptio continua*; punctuation occurs only between larger blocks. The same rune is never carved twice in a row even where it ends one word and begins the next. The 16-rune row must cover far more sounds (one rune for both k/g, another for t/d, etc.), so every word division is an editorial decision — this is the single largest source of divergent translations (Wikipedia; Uppsala, both above).

#### 1b. Which writing systems it mixes

Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 18 (Fig. 2) give the cleanest section-by-section inventory, in their reading order:

| Passage (Holmberg lines) | System | Size/notes |
|---|---|---|
| A: 1–2 | Short-twig runes (Younger Futhark, Swedish-Norwegian/Rök variant) | **Large** size — the dedication |
| A: 3 – B: 11 | Short-twig runes | Smaller — Q1–Q2 + Theodoric/sun-rider stanza |
| C: 12–13 | **Runes from the older (Elder) Futhark** | The Ingold/wolf-sacrifice passage |
| C: 14–22 | Short-twig runes again | Q4–Q5, twenty kings; line 20/22 damaged |
| D: 23, E: 24, C: 25 | **Numerical (coordinate) cipher** | Cross-shaped cipher runes, §2c |
| C: 26–28 | **(Partially) substitution cipher** | Shift cipher mixed with plain runes, §2b |
| C: 28, last ten runes | Numerical cipher again | Resolves to *nit / (h)nit*, §2c |

Uppsala University summarises this as "**four ciphers (special encrypting runes)**" plus the plain short-twig text (Uppsala 2022, above). Wikipedia's summary counts the encryption more coarsely as "two ways; by displacement (Caesar cipher) and by using special cipher runes" (Wikipedia, above). **Flag:** the *count* of cipher systems (2 vs 4) depends on whether the Elder-Futhark passage and the two forms of coordinate cipher (branch/twig and cross) are counted separately; the underlying inventory above is agreed.

Decipherment history (Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 8): Sophus Bugge (1873–78; 1910) correctly read the short-twig text and decoded the substitution cipher on the back; Otto von Friesen (1920) gave "a relatively convincing reading" of the two Elder-Futhark lines and the three lines in numerical cipher. "There is general agreement on the identification of the runic characters and the decipherment of the cryptic passages (we follow the standard transliteration)."

Reading notes on the transliteration:
- Capital letters in *saGwM MOGMEni…* are the **Elder-Futhark-valued runes** (see §2a) — the same carved shapes read with their older sound values.
- *airfb…, bfrbnhn, rhfþ, rhis* are the **shift-cipher** strings before resolution (see §2b).

The metrical shape: the opening is in *ljóðaháttr* metre and the Theodoric passage in *fornyrðislag*, the metre of Eddic poetry (Wikipedia, Old East Norse transcription section, above).

### 2. The Ciphers — systems, locations, decoded passages, worked examples

**Consensus point first:** the cipher *decipherments* (which rune each sign stands for) are agreed; what the decoded words then *mean* is not. Four systems are in play:

#### 2a. Elder-Futhark-valued runes (archaic substitution) — back of the stone, lines 12–13 in Holmberg's numbering = lines 21–22 in Wessén/RAÄ numbering

Two lines on the back are carved in the **older 24-rune futhark**, already archaic c. 800. In transliteration they are written with capital/small-cap letters to show that familiar-looking signs carry their *Elder* values: *saGwM | |MOGMEni (þ)aD HOaR iGOlD(i)Ga OaRi GOlDin D GOonaR HOsli*.

- **Standard decoding:** *sakum ukmini þat, huaR Inguldinga uari goldinn at kuanaR husli* → "I say the folktale / to the young men, which of the line of Ingold was repaid by a wife's sacrifice" (Rundata, §1d).
- **Holmberg re-decoding of the same signs:** *…huaR i gyldinga uari goldinn…* → "who because of a howler [i.e. a wolf] has suffered through a woman's sacrifice?" — i.e. he denies there is any "Ingold" in the line at all (Holmberg et al. 2020, pp. 24–26). See §4.
- One sign in this passage is itself a coordinate cipher embedded in the Elder text: Wikipedia's Unicode transcription marks *[3:3 = þ]* inside *saGwM MoGMeni [þ]aD…* (Wikipedia, above).

Why Elder runes here? The traditional assumption is deliberate archaism/secrecy for "legends connected specifically to Varinn and his tribe" (Wikipedia, interpretation section, above). Holmberg offers a pointed alternative: the older futhark "was the script used 'nine generations ago'," fitting a passage about an event nine generations back (2020, p. 26).

#### 2b. Shift / displacement (Caesar) cipher — back, lines 26–28 in Holmberg's numbering (part of the Vélinn passage)

**The key:** every carved rune stands for the *next* rune in the short-twig futhark row (*fuþark hnias tbmlʀ*). To read, replace each rune by its neighbour to the right in the row. Parts of these lines are in plain runes, hence Holmberg's "(partially) substitution cipher."

##### WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — the shift cipher (verbatim from the on-site poster and from the scholarly transcription)

The poster at the stone, as transcribed in Klaus Schmeh's *Cipherbrain* report of Magnus Ekhall's visit, https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2019/05/05/magnus-ekhalls-trip-to-the-roek-runestone/ (index, read 2026-11-06):

> "On rows 23 and 24, a shift cipher [a Caesar cipher] is used. It is written with runes from the short-twig futhark. Every rune is replaced with the next letter in the runic alphabet. On the Rök stone it says: **'AIRFBFRBNHN'. With one rune displacement it becomes 'SAKUM UKMINI'**, meaning 'I tell the youth'."

Step by step, letter by letter (row *f-u-þ-a-r-k h-n-i-a-s t-b-m-l-ʀ*):

| Carved (cipher) | a | i | r | f | b | f | r | b | n | h | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Next rune in row → | **s** | **a** | **k** | **u** | **m** | **u** | **k** | **m** | **i** | **n** | **i** |
| Reads | S | A | K | U | M | U | K | M | I | N | I |

i.e. **AIRFB FRBNHN → SAKUM UKMINI** — the same *sakum ukmini* formula that opens the plain-text questions, here hidden. In the full passage (Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 31, transliteration) the shift-cipher groups resolve in sequence: *[cipher runes airfb = sakum] [fr = uk] [bnhn = mini] [finb = uaim] [an = si] [tfonhnu = buriniþ]ʀ traki…* and later *[rhfþ = knua] [rhis = knat] iatun…* — giving, standardly, "…to whom is born a relative, to a valiant man. It is Vélinn. He could crush a giant. It is Vélinn…" (Rundata). The same groups in Holmberg's re-reading give the questions Q8–Q9 of §3b.

#### 2c. Coordinate / numerical cipher (ætt system) — narrow side D, top E, and the crosses on the back; lines 23–25 and the last ten runes of line 28 in Holmberg's numbering

**The key:** the futhark is divided into three families (*ættir*, "classes"). A cipher sign does not picture a rune; it *gives its coordinates* — how many strokes/twigs on one side of a stem (or one part of a cross) = which ætt, how many on the other side = which rune within that ætt. Sources write the result as pairs, **[ætt : position = rune]**.

Three graphic forms occur on Rök:
1. **Branch/twig runes** — twigs above the stem give the ætt, twigs below give the position (poster, "row 26").
2. **Cross-shaped runes (*korschiffer*)** — three slanted crosses on the back and three on the top. Prevailing solution (Swedish Wikipedia summary of the RAÄ reading, via search index, read 2026-11-06; and von Friesen 1920 as cited by Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 29): divide each cross into upper and lower staves; the first stave gives ætt and rune; solve the upper rune, then *rotate the cross* and solve the lower part (now uppermost) the same way; finally add the plain runes carved above the crosses. Result: ***sibi uia uari ul ni ruþr***.
3. **Grouped/repeated signs for Þórr** — at the end of the twig-cipher line, a sign used first in normal orientation, then mirrored, repeated in groups gives coordinates by count (poster, below).

##### WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — the coordinate cipher: *sakum ukmini þur* (side D)

As printed in Wikipedia's Unicode transcription with resolutions, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B6k_Runestone (index, read 2026-11-06):

> ([2:5 = ᛌ]) [2:4 = ᛆ] [3:6 = ᚴ] [3:2 = ᚢ] [1:3 = ᛙ] [3:2 = ᚢ] [3:6 = ᚴ] [1:3 = ᛙ] [2:3 = ᛁ] [2:2 = ᚿ] [2:3 = ᛁ] ¶ [3:3 = ᚦ] [3:2 = ᚢ] [3:5 = ᚱ]

Step by step:
- 2:5 → **s**, 2:4 → **a**, 3:6 → **k**, 3:2 → **u**, 1:3 → **m** = **SAKUM**
- 3:2 → **u**, 3:6 → **k**, 1:3 → **m**, 2:3 → **i**, 2:2 → **n**, 2:3 → **i** = **UKMINI**
- 3:3 → **þ**, 3:2 → **u**, 3:5 → **r** = **ÞUR**

→ ***sakum ukmini þur*** — standardly "I say the folktale / to the young men: Þórr [Thor]." Holmberg reads the final word not as the god's name but as the imperative *þor* "dare!" (2020, p. 29; see §4).

The poster gives the same system in its simplest classroom form (Cipherbrain, above):

> "The next encrypted part is in row 26. The 'twigs' on the top tell which class [Swedish: ätt] the rune belongs to and the lower 'twigs' denotes which rune within that class… **Class|Rune: 2|5 2|4 3|6 3|2 1|3 3|2 3|6 1|3 2|3 2|2 2|3 → SAKUM UKMINI**"
>
> "At the bottom of row 26… The symbols spell the name of a god: þur [Thor]: *bbbddd* = Third rune of the third class = **þ**; *bbbdd* = Second rune of the third class = **u**; *bbbfffff* = Fifth rune of the third class = **r** ⇒ **þur**" [the blog substitutes b/d/f for the actual sign and its mirror image].

##### WORKED EXAMPLE 3 (bonus, short) — the cross cipher: *sibi … ul niruþʀ*

Same Wikipedia transcription, top/back crosses:

> [3:2 = ᚢ] [1:4 = ᛚ] ¶ [2:2 = ᚿ] [2:3 = ᛁ] ¶ [3:5 = ᚱ] [3:2 = ᚢ]ᚦʀ ¶ [2:5 = ᛌ]ᛁᛓᛁ ¶ [3:2 = ᚢ] [2:3 = ᛁ]ᛆ ¶ [3:2 = ᚢ] [2:4 = ᛆ]ᚱᛁ

Step by step: 3:2→**u**, 1:4→**l** = *ul*; 2:2→**n**, 2:3→**i** = *ni*; 3:5→**r**, 3:2→**u**, + plain *þʀ* = *ruþʀ*; 2:5→**s**, + plain *ibi* = *sibi*; 3:2→**u**, 2:3→**i**, + plain *a* = *uia*; 3:2→**u**, 2:4→**a**, + plain *ri* = *uari*.
Reordered in reading order → ***sibi uia uari ul niruþʀ*** — standardly "Sibbi of Vé [guardian of the sanctuary], nonagenarian, begot (a son)" (Rundata). The poster confirms the opening: "A crossed rune is located on the top of the stone… Rune 5 of the second class: **S**; Rune 3 of the first class: **i**; [the last part is] 'bi'" → **Sibi** (Cipherbrain, above). The very last ten runes of the inscription are also coordinate cipher: [2:2 2:3 1:1 = *nit*] — standardly left as the uninterpreted word "Nit"; Holmberg reads *(h)nit*, "Clash!" (2020, pp. 31–32).

### 3. The Questions the Stone Asks

The inscription is framed as a series of "let us say / I say…" prompts (*sakum…*) followed by an interrogative clause — a form Lars Lönnroth and Joseph Harris compare to *greppaminni*, the riddle/hint-game Snorri Sturluson describes in the Prose Edda (Wikipedia, interpretation section, above). **Only four prompts carry explicit numbers in the standard reading: (unnumbered first), second, twelfth, thirteenth.** The jump 2 → 12 is one of the stone's famous problems: older scholarship assumed nine riddles were supplied orally; Holmberg (2015) argued the count works if you follow the stone step-by-step round its faces; Holmberg et al. (2020) instead re-cut the text into nine questions total (numbered and unnumbered).

#### 3a. The questions in the standard (Rundata/Wessén/Harris) framework — in order, with proposed answers and status

Harris's structural version (Joseph Harris, "Varin's philosophy and the Rök Stone's mythology of death," 2010, http://www.quest-journal.net/PIP/New_Perspectives_On_Myth_2010/New_Perspectives_on_Myth_Chapter6.pdf , index, read 2026-11-06) is the clearest statement of the traditional shape: **a dedication + three narrative sections, each of two hint-questions and one answer.** Harris himself notes Sections One and Two are "less contentious than Section Three" and that "almost everything about the inscription is controversial."

**Q1 (lines 3–5) — the two war-booties**
> "I say the folktale / to the young men, which the two war-booties were, which twelve times were taken as war-booty, both together from various men."
- *Proposed answer:* **No answer is stated in the text.** The common guess is two famous precious weapons — e.g. a sword and a shield, or a helmet — passing from man to man in lost Germanic heroic poems (Wikipedia, interpretation section: "The two war-booties are likely to be two precious weapons, such as a sword and a shield or a helmet").
- *Status:* **Unsolved / debated.** No consensus identification; the story is lost.
- *Holmberg rival answer:* the sun and the moon (see §3b).

**Q2 (lines 5–8) + stated answer, the Theodoric stanza (lines 9–11)**
> "I say this second, who nine generations ago lost his life with the Hreidgoths; and died with them for his guilt." (Rundata)
> Harris: "Who became without life (died) among the Hreið-Goths nine ages ago, and yet his affairs are still under discussion?"
- *Stated answer (lines 9–11):* "Þjóðríkr the bold, chief of sea-warriors, ruled over the shores of the Hreiðsea. Now he sits armed on his Goth(ic horse), his shield strapped, the prince of the Mærings."
- *Proposed identification:* **Theodoric the Great**, Ostrogothic king (ruler in Ravenna 493–526 CE) — first proposed by Guðbrandur Vigfússon in 1879 (Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 8). Nine generations (≈9 × 30 years) before c. 800 lands in Theodoric's lifetime. The "now he sits armed on his horse" is usually explained by his famous **equestrian statue in Ravenna, moved by Charlemagne to Aachen in 801**, shield slung over the left shoulder, lance in the right hand; *Mærings* is Theodoric's family name, also in the Old English poem *Deor* (Wikipedia, interpretation section, above). The Hreidgoths are a poetic name for the Ostrogoths.
- *Status:* **The identification was the 20th-century consensus (Wessén 1958 is "the standard interpretation"), but *why* Theodoric is on a Swedish memorial was never explained** — proposals range from Varinn showing off heroic lore, to claiming kinship with Theodoric, to Vámóðr as Theodoric reborn, to Theodoric deified as Odin (surveyed in Holmberg et al. 2020, pp. 8–9). **Since Ralph (2007) and Holmberg et al. (2020) the identification itself is actively contested** — they read the same runes *raið iau, rinkʀ hinn þurmōði* "Ride the horse did the bold champion" and deny Theodoric is named at all. Present it as *traditional consensus, now challenged*, not as settled fact.

**Q3 — "the twelfth" (lines 12–14) — the Valkyrie passage**
> "I say this the twelfth, where the horse of Gunnr sees fodder on the battlefield, where twenty kings lie."
- *Decoding the kenning:* **Gunnr is a valkyrie; a valkyrie's "horse" is a wolf** (wolves, like valkyries, haunt battlefields); the wolf's "fodder" (*etu*, food) on the battlefield is corpses — so the question asks *where* the wolf finds its carrion, i.e. on which battlefield twenty kings lie (Wikipedia, interpretation section, above). Holmberg accepts the wolf kenning but reads *gunnaʀ* as the common noun "battle," not the valkyrie's name: "where the horse of the battle [i.e. the wolf] sees food on the battlefield" (2020, p. 27).
- *Proposed answer:* **Not stated.** Candidates: a lost legendary battle; Herman Lindqvist connected it to the (legendary) **Battle of Brávellir**, supposedly fought not far from Rök c. 50 years earlier (Wikipedia, theories section, above).
- *Status:* **Unsolved.** The battlefield is unidentified in the traditional reading; the myth is lost.

**Q4 — "the thirteenth" (lines 14–19) — the twenty kings, with stated answer**
> "This I say as thirteenth, which twenty kings sat on Sjólund for four winters, of four names, born of four brothers:"
- *Stated answer (the list itself, lines 17–19):* "five Valkis, sons of Ráðulfr, five Hreiðulfrs, sons of Rugulfr, five Háisl, sons of Hôrðr, five Gunnmundrs/Kynmundrs, sons of Bjôrn." — Four groups of five brothers, all five in a group bearing the same name, their four fathers themselves brothers (4 × 5 = 20).
- *Status:* **The *answer as a list* is in the text, but its meaning is lost.** "This piece of mythology seems to have been common knowledge at the time, but has been totally lost" (Wikipedia, above). *Sjólund* is disputed: Zealand/Sjælland in Denmark (site of the Lejre kingdom), or compare Snorri's name for Roslagen in Sweden. Harris's proposal: the twenty are a warband/trading *Männerbund* whose shared names and "fathers" are initiatory, so the group survives individual deaths (Harris 2010, above). Holmberg: they are Odin's warriors (*einherjar*) on the Ragnarok battlefield (see §3b). **Debated; identities beyond the names unknown.** Note also the tension *inside* the traditional reading: Q3 says twenty kings *lie dead* on a battlefield, Q4 says twenty kings *sat* somewhere for four winters — whether these are the same twenty is assumed, not proven.

**Q5 (lines 21–22, Elder Futhark) — the wife's sacrifice / Ingold passage**
> "I say the folktale / to the young men, which of the line of Ingold was repaid by a wife's sacrifice." (Rundata)
> Harris: "Who among the descendants of Ing-Vald was compensated for through the sacrifice of a woman?"
- *Proposed answers:* None commands support. Older guesses identify the woman with legendary figures of the Ingjald/Yngling cycle (e.g. Ása, daughter of Ingjald, in later saga tradition — a guess repeated in popular/German summaries and explicitly "not provable, mythical"); Åke Ohlmarks proposed *husl* could mean "baptism" as well as "sacrifice," yielding a story in which Vámóðr paid with his life for his wife's baptism by Ansgar's mission (Wikipedia, theories section, above — presented there as a hypothesis of Ohlmarks 1979).
- *Status:* **Unsolved in the traditional framework** — "never interpreted satisfactorily" is Holmberg's phrase for this passage, and on this point there is no disagreement. Holmberg dissolves the question by re-parsing *Inguldinga* as *ī gyldinga* "because of a howler [wolf]" (see §3b, Q3-Holmberg).

**Q6 (lines 23–25, partly cipher) — to whom a son/kinsman is born; the Vélinn passage**
> "I say the folktale / to the young men, to whom is born a relative, to a valiant man. **It is Vélinn. He could crush a giant. It is Vélinn …**" (Rundata; *knua knatti iatun* = "could crush a giant")
- *Status:* **Unsolved.** Who Vélinn/Vilinn is — a god, a hero, Vámóðr himself? — is unknown; Harris re-reads the answer as "Vilinn it is, whom the enemy slew. Vilinn it is: may he enjoy (this monument)" (Harris 2010, above), a wholly different segmentation of the same runes. The following word ***nit*** (the final coordinate-cipher group) "remains uninterpreted, and its meaning is unclear" (Wikipedia, above). Holmberg dissolves Vilinn too: *villi nē's þat* = "It is not a lie" (see §3b, Q8–Q9).

**Closing statements (not questions) — Thor and Sibbi (lines 26–28)**
> "I say the folktale / to the young men: Þórr. Sibbi of Vé, nonagenarian, begot (a son)."
- *Traditional sense:* an invocation of **Thor** (compare later *þórr vigi*, "may Thor hallow," formulas), followed by a statement that **Sibbi, guardian of the sanctuary (*vé*)**, was still potent enough to beget a son at ninety — held up, perhaps, as a reward of devotion (Wikipedia, above). Harris instead folds Sibbi into his §3 answer and reads (in his ordering) that the ninety-year-old kinsman "engendered Thor" (Harris 2010, above) — a reminder that even the *order* of these last lines is editorial.
- *Status:* Sibbi is **unidentified**; no consensus on who he is or why he closes the inscription.

### 4. Contested Readings

#### 4a. Holmberg, Gräslund, Sundqvist & Williams (2020), "The Rök Runestone and the End of the World," *Futhark* 9–10 (2018–2019): 7–38, DOI 10.33063/diva-401040

**The claim, in the authors' own abstract** (PDF, p. 7, above): the inscription "deals with an anxiety triggered by a son's death and the fear of a new climate crisis similar to the catastrophic one after 536 CE"; it consists of "nine enigmatic questions. Five of the questions concern the sun, and four of them, it is argued, ask about issues related to the god Odin," with "relevant parallels… especially in the Eddic poem *Vafþrúðnismál*."

**Its machinery — five re-readings, each necessary to the whole:**
1. *sakumukmini* → *Sagum Ygg minni*, "Let us say a memory for Yggr [Odin]" — not "I say the folk-tale/to the young men." (The authors acknowledge this segmentation, from Nordenstreng 1912, is one of many; at least a dozen segmentations of this string circulate — cf. the list of 13 in Henrik Williams's own 2022 presentation slides, via search index.)
2. *raiþ þiaurikR* → *raið iau, rinkʀ*, "Ride the horse did the bold champion" (following **Bo Ralph 2007**, who first argued the Theodoric reading "has led research into a dead end, and should be abandoned"). **No Theodoric, no Ravenna.**
3. *Inguldinga* → *ī gyldinga*, "because of a howler [wolf]" (*gyldir* is an attested Old Icelandic word for wolf). **No Ingvald/Ingold.**
4. *uilin is þat* → *villi nē's þat*, "It is not a lie" (following Grønvik 2003). **No Vélinn/Vilinn.** Likewise *sibi uia uari* → *Siƀi vīaværi*, "[Who is] a protector of sanctuaries for a brother?" (dative *siƀi*, "for a brother") — **no Sibbi**; and *þur* → imperative *þor*, "dare!" — **no Thor invocation**; and *ul niruþʀ* → *Ōlni rȳðʀ*, "[Whom] does the ravager redden with blood?" — **no nonagenarian**.
5. *t siulunti fiakura uintur* → *at Sīulundi fiagura vinddura*, "at the Grove of Sparks in four directions" — not "on Sjólund [Zealand] for four winters."

**Its historical scaffolding:** volcanic eruptions 536–47 CE (ice-core sulphur, dendrochronology) caused abnormally cold summers 536–50, a collapse of cultivation around Rök, and an estimated Scandinavian population fall of "50% or more" (Gräslund & Price 2012, cited p. 14); the motifs of *fimbulvetr* (the great winter), black sunlight, the wolf swallowing the sun, and the sun's daughter in *Vǫluspá*/*Vafþrúðnismál* are argued to preserve/traumatise that event. Reactivating triggers near the carving: the strongest known solar storm in **775 CE** (red skies), the exceptionally cold summer of 775, and the **almost total solar eclipse of 810 CE** shortly after dawn (p. 16). Nine generations × ~30 years back from c. 800 lands on 536 — the same arithmetic the traditional reading used to land on Theodoric (d. 526). Consolation: Vámóðr, "death-doomed," was taken so that he can fight for Odin at Ragnarok, after which the sun's daughter shines.

**The authors' own caveats (quote these when presenting the theory):** "Of course, we are not so arrogant as to believe that we have now solved every problem in the Rök text… The reading order that we propose may also be questioned, but our interpretation does not depend on its absolute accuracy" (p. 32); on the sun's gender/epithets in Q2, "this is admittedly a weaker link in our chain of reasoning. Perhaps future research will resolve the matter" (p. 24 n.); on Q6–Q7, "just one possibility" (p. 29).

**Reception / criticism — what the sources actually show:**
- **It has not become consensus.** RAÄ's runologist Magnus Källström, reviewing the year 2021 on the RAÄ blog *K-blogg* ("Att tolka Rökstenen," 2021-12-31, https://k-blogg.se/2021/12/31/att-tolka-rokstenen/ , index via search, read 2026-11-06), notes that in that single year **three** "weighty" rival reinterpretations appeared: a new Holmberg article in *Maal og Minne*, Henrik Williams's popular book *Rökstenen och världens undergång*, and **Bo Ralph's two-volume, 954-page *Fadern, sonen och världsalltet* [The Father, the Son and the Universe] — a different full reinterpretation from the very scholar whose 2007 paper started the anti-Theodoric turn.** A field producing three incompatible total readings in a year has not settled.
- **The presentism charge is on record.** BBC Future (Matilda Welin, https://www-bbc-com.pages.dev/future/article/20260202-the-viking-secrets-revealed-by-swedens-rune-stones , index, read 2026-11-06) calls Rök "the focus of one of the biggest debates gripping rune scholars today" and reports: "some of his colleagues have cautioned that the new climate interpretation may be based on reading the text in the light of our own current worries about global warming." Holmberg's reply, quoted there: "Each era has its interpretive frameworks" — nationalism fitted the Theodoric reading in the early 1900s — and pre-industrial societies did live with constant weather anxiety. The same article notes Holmberg now extends the argument to the stone's placement, font sizes and equinox alignment — claims that are his alone so far.
- **Institutional framing cuts both ways.** Uppsala and Gothenburg university news presented the 2020 paper in solved-mystery language ("one of the greatest mysteries… appears to have been solved," Uppsala, https://www.uu.se/en/news/2020/2020-01-08-legendary-runestone-bears-witness-to-climate-anxiety-1200-years-ago?id=13822&typ=artikel&lang=en , index, read 2026-11-06), while the paper itself (above) is markedly more hedged. A follow-on Gothenburg project, *Rök Runestone Revisited* (https://www.gu.se/en/research/rok-runestone-revisited , index, read 2026-11-06), treats the *survival* of the Theodoric reading as a problem of ideological bias in 20th-century scholarship (Wessén vs. Höfler) — i.e. the debate is now also about the debaters.
- **Continued technical dispute over the key segmentation:** the sequence *sakumukmini* remains under active grammatical attack and defence in *Futhark* as late as 2025 (Williams & Fridell defending *uk* as an oblique form of *Yggr* against a grammatical objection) — reported in secondary summary at https://mystery.dongriarchive.com/2026/08/the-rok-runestones-four-ciphers-still.html (index via search, read 2026-11-06; low-authority source, cited only for the existence of the 2025 exchange, which should be verified in *Futhark* itself before use).
- **No point-by-point peer-reviewed rebuttal was found** in the sources searched for this report (see Could not verify). The honest summary is: *widely publicised, seriously argued, actively contested, not adopted as the standard reading* — the RAÄ/Rundata translation in §1d remains the reference text.

#### 4b. Holmberg's earlier, different theory (2015/2016) — do not confuse with 2020

Per Holmberg, "Answers to the Rök runestone riddles," *Futhark* 6 (2015): the riddles are **self-referential** — the front concerns the daylight needed to read the runes, the back concerns the carving of the runes and the futhark itself; the stone "has nothing at all to do with the recording of heroic sagas" and Theodoric rests on "a minor reading error" (University of Gothenburg, https://www.gu.se/en/news/new-interpretation-of-the-rok-runestone-inscription-changes-view-of-viking-age , index, read 2026-11-06). The 2020 paper retains the anti-Theodoric, pro-riddle stance but replaces the self-reference answer with the sun/Ragnarok/climate answer.

#### 4c. Other major contested readings (all pre-2020, surveyed in Holmberg et al. 2020, pp. 8–9 and Wikipedia, theories section)

| Theory | Proponent | Claim | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heroic display | Elias Wessén 1958 | Varinn shows off his knowledge of hero narratives to honour his son; we cannot know *why* Theodoric appears | Long the standard frame; now challenged |
| Vengeance | Otto von Friesen 1920 | The stone incites the tribe to avenge Vámóðr's death in battle | Speculative, not adopted |
| The *þulr* (reciter) | Widmark / tradition | Varinn was his clan's ceremonial storyteller; fearing the tales would die with his son/heir, he preserved them in short form on stone | Plausible function, unprovable |
| Legitimation | — | A chief displays a long line of powerful ancestors to justify his position | Unprovable |
| Rebirth / consecration | Bugge 1910; Höfler 1952 | Vámóðr was considered Theodoric reborn; or consecrated to a deified Theodoric-Odin | Rejected by most; Höfler's leader-cult reading is now studied as ideology (GU Revisited project) |
| Sacrifice for a baptism | Åke Ohlmarks 1979 | *husl* = baptism; Varinn sacrificed his own son because the son's wife was baptised (Ansgar's mission) — explaining *faigian*, "death-doomed," rather than "dead" | Fringe; chronology (Ansgar c. 829–30) strains a c. 800 dating |
| Brávellir | Herman Lindqvist | The twenty kings' battlefield = the legendary Battle of Brávellir near Rök | Unverified legend-to-legend link |
| Riddle-chain answers | Troels Brandt 2015 (via Medievalists.net summary) | Each riddle's answer is confirmed in the next riddle; answers include the Gothic kings' sword, nine generations of ancestors, the battlefield, the Ingoldings, Thor and his giant son; Varinn's family descends from the Herulian king Hrodolphus, "weapon-son" of Theodoric | Minority, self-published tradition |

**What *is* consensus, for contrast:** the memorial opening (§5); the runic decipherment including all ciphers (§2); c. 800 dating ("the conclusion that the inscription is carved around 800 CE seems to stand," Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 8, citing Barnes 2007); and that the text is a virtuoso, deliberately difficult composition in verse and prose using kennings.

### 5. People and Facts — Varin / Vamod, who raised the stone, for whom, dates

- **Who and for whom (consensus, from the stone itself, lines 1–2):**
  > *aft uamuþ stonta runaR þaR. (i)n uarin faþi faþiR aft faikion sunu.*
  > "In memory of Vámóðr stand these runes. And Varinn coloured them, the father, in memory of his dead son." (Rundata)
  > Holmberg translation: "After Vamoth stand these runes. And Varin, the father, made them after the death-doomed son."
  - **Varinn** (also spelled Varin; Old Norse *Varinn*) is both sponsor and runemaster — Rundata/Wikipedia list "Runemaster: Varinn." Uppsala calls him a minor king/"kinglet" and a local chieftain (Uppsala 2022, above; *Archaeology* 2020 via Wikipedia: "set up by a local chieftain named Varinn"). **Flag:** "kinglet" is Uppsala's popular gloss, not a title in the inscription, which calls him only "the father."
  - *faþi* literally means "coloured/painted" — runes were painted in antiquity — hence Rundata "coloured them" vs. the common rendering "made/wrote/carved them."
  - **Vámóðr** (Vamoth, Væmoð, Vamoð) is the dead son. Nothing else about him — age, cause of death — is stated in the agreed text. *faikion/faigian* (ON *feigr*) means "death-doomed / fated to die," not simply "dead": every interpretation must decide whether this is alliterative convenience (it alliterates with *faþi*, *faþiR*) or a theological claim that Odin marked him for death (Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 20).
- **Date:** **c. 800 CE** — "probably carved in the early 9th century, judging from the main runic alphabet used ('short-twig' runes) and the form of the language" (Wikipedia, above); Holmberg et al.: erected c. 800, dating re-examined but conclusion stands (Barnes 2007). Harris gives the wider bracket "801–c. 850" / "first half of the ninth century" (Harris 2010, above). Present as **c. 800 / early 9th century**, not a precise year.
- **Find history (context, not interpretation):** first documented in the 17th century built into a building by Rök church; the church was 12th-century; the stone was removed from the church wall in the 19th century (1843 demolition revealed runes on all sides; set up free-standing in 1862, roofed in 1933 in German Wikipedia's summary). It now stands under a protective roof beside Rök church, Rök parish, Ödeshög Municipality, Östergötland, near Lake Vättern.
- **Significance claims made by sources:** "the first known piece of written Swedish literature," marking "the beginning of the history of Swedish literature" (Wikipedia, citing Gustafson and Lönnroth); "the longest extant pre-Christian runic inscription" (Wikipedia); "the most famous runic monument of the Viking Age" (Holmberg et al. 2020, p. 7).

## Could not verify

- **No direct RAÄ (riksantikvarieambetet.se / runor.raa.se) page was fetched.** The RAÄ signage translation is used here as reproduced, with attribution, at swedishnomad.com and in the Swedish/Norwegian Wikipedia summaries of the on-site information board. The line-by-line content matches Rundata throughout, but a presenter wanting to cite RAÄ *directly* should pull the board text from RAÄ's Runor platform.
- **No formal peer-reviewed rebuttal of Holmberg et al. 2020 was located** in the indexed sources searched — criticism is documented through the BBC's reporting of colleagues' cautions, Källström's RAÄ blog noting three rival 2021 reinterpretations (including Ralph's 954-page alternative, whose detailed contents were not read), and the continued *Futhark* dispute over *sakumukmini*. Do not claim "no rebuttal exists," only that none was found here.
- **The ætt tables** (which rune sits at which coordinate) are given in sources only through worked pairs (e.g. 3:3 = þ, 3:2 = u, 3:5 = r). A complete, source-verified 3-ætt table for the Rök coordinate system was not extracted; the worked examples in §2 are exactly as the sources print them and should not be extrapolated into a full table without a runological handbook (e.g. von Friesen 1920).
- **Poster row numbers vs. scholarly line numbers** for the ciphers differ (poster: shift = rows 23–24, coordinate = row 26; Holmberg: coordinate = lines 23–25, shift = lines 26–28). Both are reported above with their numbering labelled; the discrepancy appears to reflect different counting of the horizontal lines and was not resolved from photographs.
- Counts vary by source (700+ / c. 750 / 760 characters; 2.4 vs 2.5 m above ground). "c. 760" is the majority figure (Rundata-derived and Uppsala).
