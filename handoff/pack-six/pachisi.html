Rules of this table

AttestedFrom the historical game

  • The board is a symmetrical cross: four arms, each three columns of eight squares, with the great central square — the Charkoni — as the start and finish of every journey.
  • Twelve castle squares are safe from capture: four at the ends of the middle columns, and eight set four squares inwards from the ends of the outer columns. A piece may not land on a castle already held by the opponent.
  • Pieces are beehive-shaped, in black, green, red and yellow. All travel counter-clockwise around the board.
  • Landing on an opponent's piece off a castle captures it — the taken piece returns to the start, and the captor traditionally throws again.
  • Seven cowrie shells are the lots. The attested seven-shell table: 0→7 “Sat”, 1→10 “Dus”, 2→2, 3→3, 4→4, 5→25 “Pachees”, 6→35 “Paintees”, 7→14 “Chaudah” — extra throws on 0, 1, 5, 6 and 7.
  • Traditionally four players, often in partnerships; the side that brings all its pieces home first wins. Akbar is said to have played it with living pieces on the courts of Fatehpur Sikri.

House conventionThis table's simplifications

  • Two players only — Red (you, south) against Black (the house, north). The east and west arms belong to no one.
  • Simplified cowries — the count of shells landing mouth-up is the move (1–7 pips). Only a throw of 7 earns an extra throw (the traditional Chaudah); a throw of 0 is cast again.
  • Entry — a piece may enter on any throw, taking its place on your start castle; the throw is then spent.
  • The journey — each piece travels the full circuit (64 squares), then up its own home column; an exact count is needed to reach the Charkoni. Captures and homecomings each grant another throw.
  • You cast first. The house plays a short-sighted heuristic — it favors captures, castles and pieces nearest home, and shuns needless danger.

UncertainWhat varies by tradition

  • Journey length, entry throws and the worth of each cowrie count differ between regions and eras; several living versions use richer scoring than this table's reduction.
  • “Four squares inwards from the end” is rendered here as the fifth square from each arm-tip — a reading, not a certainty.
  • Nothing here is presented as the single original rule; where the record is silent, this table says so.

Sources: Wikipedia, “Pachisi” (seven-shell table, castles, Charkoni, Akbar's court); medhajournal.com, “Pachisi, Chaupar and Ludo”; gambiter.com, “Pachisi” & “Chaupar”. Board geometry follows the traditional 4 × 3 × 8 cross (sources cite a 68-square circuit as four sides of 17; the four corner squares are shared between arms, giving 64 distinct playing squares).
