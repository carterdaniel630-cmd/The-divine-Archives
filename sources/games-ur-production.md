Finkel 2007 (below) dates the tablet 177–176 BC and calls the route a proposal and the dice scheme a postulate. Ur boards c. 2600–2400 BCE (row above), so "more than two thousand years". | The gate | A board cut between the hooves of a winged bull guarding a gateway at Khorsabad, citadel of Sargon II | British Museum W_1850-1228-4_1: "a gameboard is incised between the bull's hooves"; gateway in the citadel wall, Khorsabad — https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1850-1228-4_1 . | The last players | Popular until late antiquity; forgotten except among the Jewish community of Kochi, who played "Asha" until the 1950s | Wikipedia: "remained popular until late antiquity"; "the Jewish population of the Indian city of Kochi, who continued playing a version of it called 'Asha' until the 1950s" |

Primary scholarly source for the rulebook line: Irving L. Finkel, "On the Rules for the Royal Game of Ur", in I. L. Finkel (ed.), *Ancient Board Games in Perspective* (British Museum Press, 2007). Opened as a PDF copy hosted at https://csclub.uwaterloo.ca/~pbarfuss/On_the_Rules_for_the_Royal_Game_of_Ur.pdf (not the publisher's site, so the game cites the book without a link). It gives the scribe and date ("In Babylon, on 3 November, 177–176 bc, the Babylonian scribe" …) and the tablet number BM 33333B (formerly Rm III, 6B). It does not mention Kochi or Khorsabad; those rest on Wikipedia and the British Museum record.

Not used on screen: the tablet number BM 33333B (in Finkel 2007; kept off to leave the game's existing History text as it was).
