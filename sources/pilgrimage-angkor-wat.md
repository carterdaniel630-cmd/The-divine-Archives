# Sources: the Pilgrimage, Angkor Wat

Model: `docs/pilgrimage/sites/angkor-wat/` (status **preview**: built, noindex, off the menu). Sizes are in
`dims.js`, each marked SURVEY (a published figure) or APPROX (our estimate); the bas-reliefs are drawn in
`art.js`; the info-point texts are in `site.js`. Logged 2026-10-18.

**How the facts were checked:** every page below marked *opened* was read from this session. No measured plan of
the temple could be opened, so the heights of the three levels, the places and sizes of the libraries, pools and
cruciform terrace, and the shapes of the towers and gates are APPROX. E. Mannikka's book and the *Antiquity*
papers themselves were not read; what they say is taken from the review and the news report named below, and
is labelled so on the cards.

No Wikipedia source is used.

## Sources
1. M. Glaize, *Les monuments du groupe d'Angkor* (1944; 4th ed. 1993), English translation published at
   theangkorguide.com, "Angkor Wat",
   https://www.theangkorguide.com/text/part-two/angkorwat-to-angkorthom/angkorwat.htm (*opened*, three reads; the
   page does not name its translator): the whole about 1,500 × 1,300 m with the moats, nearly 200 ha; the outer
   enclosure 1,025 × 800 m; the moats 190 m wide, about 5.5 km round, crossed by an earth bank on the east and a
   causeway on the west, 200 m long and 12 m wide; the laterite wall about 30 m from the moat; the western
   colonnade 235 m long with a three-part gopura "crowned with three towers that are unfortunately truncated",
   and pavilions at either end "which served as ground-level passageways for elephants"; the causeway inside,
   350 m long and 9.40 m wide, raised 1.5 m, with a naga balustrade and six stairways each side; the two
   "libraries" towards the middle on either side; "two square pools", the northern one keeping its stone steps
   and "always full of water"; the high cruciform "Grand Terrace" with a naga balustrade; the first gallery
   187 × 215 m, passage 2.45 m; reliefs 2 m high, over 1,000 m², followed "leaving from close to the west entrance
   and heading south", in the funerary *prasavya* order, the reverse of *pradakshina*, which keeps the monument to
   one's right; Kurukshetra in the west gallery's south part (Kauravas from the left, Pandavas from the right;
   Bhishma dying pierced with arrows; Arjuna with his four-armed driver Krishna); the procession of
   Suryavarman II, 90 m, in the south gallery's west part (the royal audience above; the king with his 15
   parasols; chiefs standing on elephants; the royal priests; the Siamese allies; 28 small inscriptions); heaven
   and hell, 60 m, in the south gallery's east part (Yama, many-armed, on a buffalo; 37 heavens and 32 hells; 36
   short inscriptions); the Churning, nearly 50 m, in the east gallery's south part (Vasuki, Mount Mandara on a
   tortoise, Vishnu presiding, a frieze of apsaras, 92 asuras and 88 devas); the cruciform cloister with four
   tanks with "richly ornamented sides and a single central stair" that "could either have been pools or lower level courtyards", and the "Prah Pean" (the thousand
   Buddhas) where most statues have been re-established; the second gallery 100 × 115 m, its corner towers
   truncated; the "two-storey thirty metre high substructure" with stairs of about 70°; the quincunx 60 m at its
   base; the central tower 42 m high, 62 m above the main causeway; the sanctuary about 5 m wide; the towers
   "like coned tiaras due to their multiple redents" with rows of lotus petals; Thomson (1866) seeing Mount Meru
   in the temple and matching the seven circles of the central tower to Meru's seven mountain chains (Glaize
   reports this as Thomson's, after "Madame de Coral-Remusat", as the card names her); the west
   orientation and its explanations (funerary: Finot, Cœdès, Przyluski, Bosch; Vishnu and the west; stone
   brought by water, which Glaize finds insufficient); first half of the 12th century, Suryavarman II,
   posthumous name Paramavishnuloka, Vishnuite; cleared by Commaille 1908–11 (no first name given; the card gives none); the temple "has always sheltered
   pagodas, as a place of pilgrimage for the Khmer"; the monks walling up the sanctuary openings after removing
   the Brahmanic idol and carving standing Buddhas, and repairing with columns from elsewhere; the early 18th
   century inscription on the east gopura on a funerary monument; the foundation deposit 23 m down the central
   well, two gold leaves 18 cm across, in laterite.

## Sensitivity notes (for Carter)
- Angkor Wat was a Buddhist shrine for centuries and is a pilgrimage shrine today (entry 7); whether Buddhist worship goes on there now is left open. The model shows no images of the
  Buddha or of Vishnu, no monks and no worshippers. The cards say only what Glaize (1944) records, that Buddhist pagodas always
  stood within it, and quote Britannica for its present standing as a pilgrimage shrine. No scholarly source on
  Buddhist worship there today was found (on 2026-10-31 Britannica described Buddhist use only in the past tense and HISTORY.com called it "no longer an active temple"; entry 7), so the
  cards do not claim it either way.
- The reliefs are our own outline renderings, not tracings; the hells are drawn only as falling figures.
- The equinox card reports the alignment as observed and APSARA's view that it was deliberate (Mannikka's too, entry 4), and says the
  builders left no statement; the calendar readings (Mannikka, Kak, Im Sokrithy's day counts) are labelled as
  interpretations.
