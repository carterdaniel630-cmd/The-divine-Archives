# Sources: the Pilgrimage, Chichén Itzá

Model: `docs/pilgrimage/sites/chichen-itza/` (status **preview**: built, noindex, off the menu). Sizes are in
`dims.js`, each marked SURVEY (a published figure) or APPROX; the info-point texts are in `site.js`; the carvings,
the stepped bodies and the pattern of light are drawn in `maya.js`. The moment shown is the site as a visitor finds
it today, with two switchable views: the pattern of light on the north stairway, and a rough outline of the inner
pyramid. Logged 2026-10-12.

**How the facts were checked:** each fact below was confirmed by opening the page named, or through search results
that quote the source, and is to be re-read against the source at review. No measured plan of the site could be
opened, so **where each building stands, the distances between them, and every size not listed below are
estimates**. The model is squared to the pyramid; the real site is turned about twenty degrees from north. The
carvings (skull rack, bench panels, serpent heads) are our own simple renderings of their subjects. No human
remains and no offerings are shown anywhere in the model.

## Sources
- UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "Pre-Hispanic City of Chichen-Itza" (site 483, inscribed 1988),
  https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/483 : the buildings of the northern group (El Castillo, the Great Ball Court, the
  Temple of the Warriors, the Tzompantli, the Group of the Thousand Columns); El Caracol; the older account of a
  Toltec conquest in the 10th century; decline after about 1440.
- *Encyclopædia Britannica*, "Chichén Itzá", https://www.britannica.com/place/Chichen-Itza : founded around the 6th
  century.
- "El Castillo, Chichen Itza", Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Castillo,_Chichen_Itza : base 55.3 m;
  24 m high plus a temple of about 6 m; about 91 steps a side and the reading of 365; the north face's azimuth
  (111.72°, sunsets of 20 May and 24 July, after Šprajc and Sánchez Nava 2013); the substructure, the chac mool
  (1935) and the red jaguar throne (1936, cinnabar, jadeite, after Juárez-Rodríguez et al. 2017); two earlier phases
  found by electrical resistivity tomography (Tejero-Andrade et al. 2018); the cenote beneath (about 25 × 35 m,
  reports of August 2015); the crowds at the spring equinox. The page's own dates for the two pyramids disagree;
  the card says so. The inner pyramid's size (about 33 m wide, 17 m high) is uncited on the page; the outline is
  marked as a rough guess.
- I. Šprajc and P. F. Sánchez Nava, "El Sol en Chichén Itzá y Dzibilchaltún: la supuesta importancia de los
  equinoccios en Mesoamérica", *Arqueología Mexicana* 149, pp. 26–31,
  https://iaps.zrc-sazu.si/sites/default/files/sprajcsol_chichen_dzibilchaltun_am149.pdf : nine stepped bodies;
  the pattern photographed daily 15–29 March 2017, changing little; six to nine triangles over the weeks; deliberate
  design doubtful; the equinox idea popularised in the 1960s–70s; the north stairway and the cenote. Flagged
  **contested**.
- I. Šprajc, "Equinoctial Sun and Astronomical Alignments in Mesoamerican Architecture: Fiction and Fact", *Ancient
  Mesoamerica* 34.2 (2023), 281–297, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0956536121000419 (abstract read).
- Guinness World Records, "Largest ball court in ancient Mesoamerica",
  https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/88083-largest-ball-court-in-ancient-mesoamerica : 166 × 68 m;
  rings carved with intertwining serpents high on each long wall; the game played for more than three millennia.
  (It gives the walls as 12 m high; the model's walls are lower, APPROX.)
- C. C. Earley, "The Mesoamerican Ballgame", The Metropolitan Museum of Art (2017),
  https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-mesoamerican-ballgame : the largest known court, 316 × 98 ft; solid rubber
  balls; rings; the decapitated player with serpents and plants; readings of the game (day and night, the
  underworld, life and death).
- Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco, "Rubbing from the Great Ballcourt at Chichen Itza" (Merle Greene Robertson),
  https://www.famsf.org/artworks/rubbing-from-the-great-ballcourt-at-chichen-itza : the victor on the left, the
  kneeling beheaded player on the right, blood as serpents, the ball with a skull.
- "Tzompantli", Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzompantli : the use of skull racks; six ball-court
  reliefs of a decapitated player; the suggested link to the ball game.
- J. K. Kowalski and C. Kristan-Graham (eds.), *Twin Tollans: Chichén Itzá, Tula, and the Epiclassic to Early
  Postclassic Mesoamerican World* (Dumbarton Oaks, 2007; revised edition later), publisher's page
  https://doaks.org/research/publications/books/twin-tollans-chicha-c-n-itza-tula-and-the : the volume sets aside
  "earlier notions of migrations and conquests" for the cities' social, political and economic relations. Flagged
  **contested**. The editors' names are from the library catalogue (WorldCat, https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/71243931), not the publisher's page.
- "Sacred Cenote", Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Cenote : about 60 m across and 27 m to the water
  (Cano 2002); Landa's account of 1566; Thompson from 1904, diving in 1909 (Sabloff 1994); the 1961 and 1967–68
  expeditions; the causeway of about 300 m (Adams 1991).
- J. Doyle, "Golden Kingdoms: the Sacred Cenote of Chichén Itzá", The Metropolitan Museum of Art (2018),
  https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/golden-kingdoms-sacred-cenote-chichen-itza : the collapsed cave; jade (some
  burned), gold and copper from Costa Rica and Panama, copal, wood and cloth; dates 700–1450; dredging 1904–1910.
- "New thinking on Mayan child sacrifice", *World Archaeology* 28 (2008),
  https://www.world-archaeology.com/more/new-thinking-on-mayan-child-sacrifice.htm : G. de Anda's study of some
  2,500 bones from the 1960s; about 79% from children aged about 3 to 11.
- *National Geographic*, 12 June 2024, https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/maya-sacrifice-dna-chichen-itza-boys :
  the ancient-DNA study (R. Barquera and others, *Nature*, 2024) of a chultun near the cenote: 64 sampled, all male,
  many related, two pairs of identical twins.
- "El dragado del cenote sagrado de Chichén Itzá, 1904–c.1914", *Historia Mexicana* 67.2,

- "El Caracol, Chichen Itza", Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Caracol,_Chichen_Itza : the name; about
  906 from a stone on the upper platform; 20 of 29 events; the lost part of the tower.
- A. F. Aveni, S. L. Gibbs and H. Hartung, "The Caracol Tower at Chichen Itza: an ancient astronomical

- P. Bíró and E. Pérez de Heredia, "El disco de El Caracol de Chichén Itzá (929–932 d.n.e.)", *Estudios de Cultura

- Exploratorium, "Ancient Observatories: Chichen Itza — El Caracol",
  https://annex.exploratorium.edu/ancientobs/chichen/HTML/caracol2.html : three openings survive; Venus's northern

- "El Caracol: Ancient Observatory", mayaruins.com, https://mayaruins.com/chichen/ElCaracol.html : a

  Venus's extremes; used only to confirm the summary above.
- The archive's own chapter on the Maya (ch29), for the six million Maya living in the region today.

## Notes for review
- **ch29 correction:** the chapter says the pyramid "is built so that, on the equinoxes" the sun casts the serpent.
  That intent is contested (Šprajc and Sánchez Nava); the serpent card says so, and ch29 needs a hedge.
- **Sensitivity (for Carter):** three cards touch human sacrifice and remains (the platform of skulls, the bench
  panels, "Those given to the well"). They describe what the sources say plainly and show nothing. The dredging card
  reports the *Historia Mexicana* view that the export was unlawful and is flagged "Still argued today". The living
  Maya are named on the first card.
- Not confirmed from a source and so left out: whether the pyramid can be climbed today, the distance to Tula,
  the size of El Caracol, and the posture or arrangement of the serpent columns.
