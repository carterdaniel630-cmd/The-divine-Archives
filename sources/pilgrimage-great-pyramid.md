# Sources: the Pilgrimage, the Great Pyramid of Khufu

Model: `docs/pilgrimage/sites/great-pyramid/`. Dimensions are in `dims.js`, where each value names its source;
the info-point texts are in `site.js`. Logged 2026-10-04.

**How the figures were checked:** this session could not open the survey texts directly (the sandbox blocks those
hosts), so each Petrie figure below was confirmed through search results that quote it, and is to be re-read against
the survey itself at review. Where a figure is our own estimate, `dims.js` marks it APPROX.

## Survey measurements

- **W. M. Flinders Petrie, *The Pyramids and Temples of Gizeh* (London: Field & Tuer, 1883).** Public domain. PDF:
  https://www.gizapyramids.org/static/pdf%20library/petrie_gizeh.pdf (Giza Archives, Harvard).
  Figures used, all in British inches:
  - entrance passage slope 26° 31′ 23″ ± 5″;
  - entrance floor 668.2 above the base, offset 287 ± 0.8 east of the pyramid's axis;
  - passage width 41.6 and height 47.3;
  - ascending passage 1546.8 long;
  - Grand Gallery 1815.5 long and 339 high, 82.0 over the ramps and 42.0 between them, with seven overlaps;
  - Queen's Chamber 205.85 × 226.47, walls 184.47 high, ridge 245.1, floor 834.4 ± 0.3 above the base;
  - the niche 41 deep, 62 wide at the base, four overlaps, 20 wide at 156, roofed at 184;
  - antechamber 116.3 long, 65.2 wide, about 149.6 high;
  - King's Chamber 412.25 × 206.13 × 230.05, floor about 1692 above the base;
  - coffer outside 89.62 × 38.50 × 41.31 and inside 78.06 × 26.81 × 34.42 (mean of some 681 measures);
  - face slope about 51° 50′ 40″.
- **Harvard University, the Giza Project (Digital Giza)**, https://giza.digitalhumanities.fas.harvard.edu/: its
  documentation and its own 3D model of the Khufu complex. Consulted as a reference for layout only; nothing copied.
- Consulted for cross-checking (in copyright, nothing copied; exact pages to be added at review):
  - V. Maragioglio and C. Rinaldi, *L'architettura delle piramidi menfite*, part IV (Rapallo, 1965);
  - M. Lehner, *The Complete Pyramids* (London: Thames & Hudson, 1997).

## History told in the info points

- **The relieving chambers and the workers' marks naming Khufu:**
  - H. Vyse, *Operations Carried on at the Pyramids of Gizeh in 1837* (London, 1840–42);
  - N. Davison found the lowest chamber in 1765;
  - both summarised in Lehner (1997).
  - The argument against the forgery claim (marks running into joints and behind set blocks) is the standard one in
    the literature. **To confirm with a page reference at review.**
- **The robbers' tunnel and its traditional attribution to al-Ma'mun (c. 820 CE):** Lehner (1997). The attribution
  rests on later Arabic writers and is stated as uncertain.
- **The Queen's Chamber shafts:**
  - Waynman Dixon cut through to them in September 1872:
    https://ancientegyptonline.co.uk/queenschambergp/
  - Rudolf Gantenbrink's Upuaut 2 climbed about 65 m (213 ft) up the southern shaft to a limestone slab with two
    copper pins in 1993: https://dialectrix.com/pyramids/door-in-the-dark.html
  - The Djedi project, 2010–11: Z. Hawass et al., first report on the video survey of the southern shaft of the
    Queen's Chamber, *Annales du Service des Antiquités de l'Égypte* 84 (2011): 203–217; summary at
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djedi_Project (a pointer only).
- **Fans fitted to the King's Chamber shafts in 1993:** Lehner (1997). **To confirm with a page reference at review.**
- **Khufu and the date (c. 2560 BCE):** the archive's Egypt chapter (ch02) and its source log, `sources/ch02-egypt.md`.

## Relics and glyphs (added 2026-10-04)

Checked the same way as above: this session could reach only search results, not the sources themselves, so every
figure below is to be re-read against its source at review. Models are our own renditions; no photograph or scan is
used.

- **The Dixon relics** (case in the Queen's Chamber; copies enlarged about twice):
  - found by Waynman Dixon, with James Grant, for C. Piazzi Smyth, in the Queen's Chamber's northern shaft, 1872;
  - Smyth's words, "a little bronze grapnel hook; a portion of cedar-like wood which might have been its handle; and
    a grey-granite or green-stone ball", and the ball's weight, 8,325 grains: Smyth, *Our Inheritance in the Great
    Pyramid* (4th ed., 1880), as quoted in the 2020 reports. **Page to be added at review.**
  - The ball's size (about 7 cm) is our estimate from that weight and the density of dolerite (about 3 g/cm³).
    The hook's size was not found; its copy is to an estimated scale, and the card says so.
  - Ball and hook: British Museum. **Registration numbers to be confirmed at review.**
  - The cedar: University of Aberdeen, "Missing 5,000-year-old piece of Great Pyramid puzzle discovered in cigar box
    in Aberdeen", news release, December 2020, https://www.abdn.ac.uk/news/14573/ (found by Abeer Eladany;
    radiocarbon 3341–3094 BCE; about five inches long). Also reported by Archaeology magazine, 17 December 2020,
    https://archaeology.org/news/2020/12/16/201217-egypt-great-pyramid/
- **The logbook of Merer** (Vestibule): P. Tallet, *Les papyrus de la mer Rouge I: le « journal de Merer »* (Cairo:
  IFAO, 2017); P. Tallet, *The Red Sea Scrolls* (Thames & Hudson, 2021). Year 27; Tura to Akhet-Khufu; about every
  ten days two or three round trips, about thirty blocks of 2–3 tonnes; the office under Ankhhaf; found 2013 by the
  mission of Tallet and Gregory Marouard; parts shown in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo.
- **The ivory statuette** (Vestibule): W. M. F. Petrie, *Abydos* II (1903); Egyptian Museum, Cairo, JE 36143; 7.5 cm
  high; head recovered after three weeks' sifting. The later dating: Z. Hawass, "The Khufu statuette: is it an Old
  Kingdom sculpture?", in *Mélanges Gamal Eddin Mokhtar* (IFAO, 1985), PDF at
  https://www.gizapyramids.org/static/pdf%20library/hawass_fs_mokhtar.pdf. Reported as contested, not resolved.
- **Khufu's ship** (Vestibule, 1:60): found 1954 by Kamal el-Mallakh; 1,224 pieces; about 43.4 × 5.9 m; moved to the
  Grand Egyptian Museum in 2021. N. Jenkins, *The Boat Beneath the Pyramid* (1980); Lehner (1997). Its purpose is
  given as debated.
- **The casing stone** (Vestibule): National Museums Scotland, "Pyramid casing stone",
  https://www.nms.ac.uk/explore-our-collections/stories/world-cultures/ancient-egyptian-collection/ancient-egyptian-collection/pyramid-casing-stone/
  (found by Dixon and Grant, sent to Piazzi Smyth, 25 inches long, given to the museum in 1955). Egypt's 2019
  question about its export: The Scotsman, https://www.scotsman.com/news/crime/egypt-warns-national-museum-of-scotland-pyramid-stone-exhibit-may-be-illegal-1423746.
  Height and depth of the copy are estimated. Smyth's "pyramid inch" is reported as not supported, after Petrie (1883).
- **Khufu's name** (board in the Vestibule; cards): sign values from A. H. Gardiner, *Egyptian Grammar* (3rd ed.,
  1957), sign list Aa1, G43, I9; names from J. von Beckerath, *Handbuch der ägyptischen Königsnamen* (2nd ed., 1999).
  The drawing is ours.
- **The relieving chambers and their marks** (outlines over the King's Chamber): heights and the 69 ft 3 in total from
  H. Vyse, *Operations Carried on at the Pyramids of Gizeh in 1837*, vol. 1 (1840), digitised by Heidelberg
  University Library, https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/howard_vyse1840bd1. The cartouche in Campbell's
  chamber and Khnum-Khufu in Lady Arbuthnot's: Vyse and Lehner (1997). The red cartouche on the outline is our
  drawing, placed approximately. **The forgery-claim rebuttal (marks into joints, upside down) still needs a page
  reference at review.**
- **The Lepsius inscription** (gable stone over the entrance): carved 15 October 1842 for Friedrich Wilhelm IV's
  birthday, eleven columns; the quoted opening of Lepsius's own translation from his letter of 17 January 1843 in his
  *Letters from Egypt, Ethiopia, and the Peninsula of Sinai* (English translation, 1853), as quoted in search
  results; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Richard_Lepsius (a pointer only). The signs on the model are
  illustrative marks, not the text, and its exact block is approximate. A study of the inscription to read at
  review: F. Onderka and C. E. Loeben, *Annals of the Náprstek Museum* (2020),
  https://publikace.nm.cz/file/801c7dd1f1c2430e623a83ec3bdc61df/24142/ANNALS_c02-2020_02_Onderka-Loeben.pdf
