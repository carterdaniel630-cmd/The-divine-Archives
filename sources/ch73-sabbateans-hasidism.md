## Notes
- Well-supported: Sabbatai's career and conversion (1666); the Dönme; the Emden–Eybeschütz controversy; Frank, the 1757/1759 disputations, Talmud burning and baptisms; the historical Besht (Rosman); the Maggid and the spread of Hasidism; the Tanya; the 1772 excommunication; Uman.
- Not supported: Sabbatai as Messiah or apostasy as redemptive (faith claims); the blood libel (false); miracles of the Besht and rebbes (faith/hagiography); the high 1648 figures of the Jewish chronicles.
- Open: causes of Sabbateanism (Luria, 1648); Eybeschütz's allegiance; actual Frankist practice; Sabbatean influence on Hasidism/Haskalah; the Holy Epistle's textual history; Sabbatai's psychology.
- Placed in era 08 (Early Modern).

## Flags for Carter
- **Glückel's marriage date (corrected 2026-10-05 on Carter's decision; the opening now reads "Born in Hamburg in 1646, ... married at about fourteen, around 1660, into ...", per her memoirs and the Jewish Women's Archive entry above).** Original note: The chapter's first sentence reads "In Hamburg in the 1640s and 1650s, a Jewish woman named Glückel married into a merchant family of the town of Hameln, raised twelve children ...". Glückel was born in 1646/47 and, by her own memoirs, married at about fourteen, around 1660, so the marriage itself falls just after the 1650s; the 1640s–1650s are her childhood. The catalogue description of Lowenthal's translation also calls her the mother of fourteen children; twelve is the usual count of those who survived to adulthood, so "raised twelve" is defensible. If you want a correction: "Born in Hamburg in 1646, ... married at fourteen into ...".
- **Sabbatai's first self-revelation (minor, no change made).** The *Jewish Encyclopedia* (1906) says Sabbatai first revealed himself as Messiah to a few followers at Smyrna in 1648. The chapter gives his public proclamation as May 1665, which follows Scholem and modern scholarship; the two do not contradict each other, but the chapter does not mention the earlier 1648 episode.
- **The 1648 casualty range (minor, no change made).** The chapter says modern estimates run "from several thousand to tens of thousands of deaths". Stampfer (2003), the standard modern study, estimates that at most half of about 40,000 Jews in the region died, i.e. no more than about 20,000 and possibly far fewer. The chapter's range is compatible with this, and already says the figures are debated.
