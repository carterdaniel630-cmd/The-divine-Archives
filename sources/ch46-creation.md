# Sources — Chapter 46: Creation & the First Order

Per the project sourcing standard, this citation log records the real, checkable sources behind the chapter's specific claims. It is derived from the Sources section published with the chapter; contested points are flagged as contested in the chapter text, and later doctrine is kept distinct from what the earliest texts say.

## Sources cited

*Chaoskampf and the combat myth*
- [Wikipedia: Chaoskampf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaoskampf) — the combat-myth pattern
  across the ancient Near East and Mediterranean (Marduk/Tiamat, Baal/Yamm, Yahweh/Leviathan and
  Rahab, Zeus/Typhon); the linguistic cognate of Genesis's **tehom** with Akkadian **Tiamat** and
  the deliberate demythologizing in Genesis 1.

*The creatio ex nihilo / Genesis 1:1 translation debate*
- [Steven DiMattei, "Genesis 1:1–2 — not a creatio ex nihilo"](https://stevendimattei.com/genesis-11-2-creation-ex-nihilo/)
  — the "In the beginning God created" vs. "When God began to create… the earth being formless and
  void" translation question (Rashi; JPS/NRSV), and creation ex nihilo as a later doctrine that
  crystallized in the early Christian centuries (sharpened against Gnostic systems).

*The cosmic-giant cosmogony*
- [academia.edu: "Is the Cosmic Giant an Indo-European myth?"](https://www.academia.edu/472488/Is_the_Cosmic_Giant_an_Indo_European_myth)
  — Ymir (Norse) and Purusha (Rigveda 10.90) and the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European primal-being
  cosmogony (Bruce Lincoln), with the paired body/world correspondences and the caveat that the
  motif also appears in non-Indo-European cultures (e.g. Pangu).

*The skeptical Hymn of Creation*
- [Wikipedia: Nasadiya Sukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasadiya_Sukta) — Rigveda 10.129, its
  "Who really knows?" close, the gods coming after creation, and Carl Sagan's use of it.

*The traditions' own accounts* are sourced in their chapters: Mesopotamia (Ch. 3, Enuma Elish),
Egypt (Ch. 2, Nun/Ptah/Ogdoad), Early Vedic (Ch. 5, Purusha/Hiranyagarbha/Nasadiya), Early Greece
(Ch. 8, Hesiod's Theogony), pre-exilic Israel (Ch. 7, tehom), Norse paganism (Ch. 23, Ginnungagap/Ymir).

*Note on sourcing:* Researched by web search against reference scholarship alongside the archive's own
sourced chapters, per the project standard, rather than from memory. The chapter keeps what a text says
separate from what a later doctrine needed it to say (creatio ex nihilo), flags the Indo-European
cosmic-giant reconstruction and the direction of Near Eastern borrowing as genuinely open, notes the
social loading of the Purusha caste verse, and lets the Nasadiya Sukta's ancient doubt stand beside the
confident cosmogonies rather than resolving it away.
