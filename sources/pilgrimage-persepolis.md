# Sources: the Pilgrimage, Persepolis

Model: `docs/pilgrimage/sites/persepolis/` (status **preview**: built, noindex, off the menu). Sizes are in
`dims.js`, each marked SURVEY (a published figure) or APPROX (our estimate); the column grids are in `layout.js`;
the reliefs are drawn in `art.js`; the info-point texts are in `site.js`. Logged 2026-10-18.

**How the facts were checked:** every page below marked *opened* was read in full from this session. E. F.
Schmidt's excavation reports (*Persepolis* I–III, Chicago 1953–70) and their plans could not be opened, so where
each building stands, the shapes of the stairs and which columns stand are APPROX.

No Wikipedia source is used.

## Sources
1. A. Sh. Shahbazi, "Persepolis", *Encyclopaedia Iranica* (online, 15 August 2009, updated 1 August 2016),
   https://iranicaonline.org/articles/persepolis (*opened*): Darius chose the site about 518 BCE; terrace about
   125,000 m², about 300 × 455 m, west and north walls about 12 m high, water channels, drains, a 26 m well; the
   double-reversed stairway, flights of 63 and 48 steps, each step 6.70 m long, 10 cm high, 38 cm deep; the Gate
   (Shahbazi's "Gate of All Lands") about 612 m², four columns over 16.5 m, three doorways, guardian bulls with four
   legs where the Assyrian ones have five, Xerxes' inscription XPa on the jambs; the Apadana on a podium 3 m high,
   hall 60 × 60 m with 36 columns, three porticoes of 12, four corner towers, columns about 19.5 m, 72 in all,
   13 standing in 1977, walls 5.32 m thick, two staircases each 81.67 m long; Krefter's 1933 find of two
   foundation boxes with gold and silver plaques about 33 cm square (DPh); the reliefs: guards and Persian and
   Median dignitaries on one wing, 23 delegations on the other (18 in three rows, 5 in a slanting line), the winged
   disc between sphinxes, the lion attacking a bull ("may have astrological symbolism"); the Tachara about
   40 × 30 m with 12 columns, model for the front of Darius's tomb at Naqsh-e Rostam; the Hall of a Hundred
   Columns about 68.5 m square, columns about 14 m, begun by Xerxes and finished by Artaxerxes I; the Treasury
   about 120 × 60 m; tombs in the Royal Hill attributed to Artaxerxes II and III; the Nowruz debate (for:
   Herzfeld, Krefter, Ghirshman, Porada; against: Nylander, Calmeyer, Mousavi); the winged symbol read as *farr*,
   with *frōhar* (fravashi) and Ahuramazda as alternatives; the deliberate burning in 330 BCE after the treasurer
   Tiridates surrendered; the Oriental Institute excavations under Herzfeld (1931–34) and Schmidt (1935–39), with
   Krefter; about 30,000 Fortification tablets.
2. UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "Persepolis", site 114, https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/114 (*opened*): founded by
   Darius I about 518 BCE; built on by Xerxes I and Artaxerxes I; a showplace for royal receptions and festivals;
   at the foot of Kuh-e Rahmat, the Mountain of Mercy; the terrace half natural, half artificial; columns about
   1.60 m thick and about 20 m high, with double-bull capitals; light timber roofs allowed slender columns;
   inscribed 1979.
3. J. J. Mark, "Persepolis", *World History Encyclopedia* (19 November 2019, updated 19 July 2022),
   https://www.worldhistory.org/persepolis/ (*opened*): the steps "wide enough for riders on horseback" and meant to
   slow the ascent; capitals of bulls and lions; Alexander's burning in 330 BCE: the feast and Thaïs (Diodorus,
   Plutarch), or revenge for the burning of the Athenian Acropolis in 480 BCE; tombs of Darius I and his
   successors at Naqsh-e Rostam.
4. J. Lendering, Livius.org, "Persepolis, Gate of All Nations",
   https://www.livius.org/articles/place/persepolis/persepolis-photos/persepolis-gate-of-all-nations/ (*opened*):
   columns about 16.5 m with double-bull capitals; bulls at the entrance and human-headed winged bulls (lamassu);
   doors to the west and east and a third to the south towards the Apadana; the trilingual inscription XPa;
   graffiti of Cornelis de Bruijn (1704), Henry M. Stanley and others.
5. Livius.org, "XPa", https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/xpa/ (*opened*): the
   English translation of Xerxes' inscription (translator not named on the page). The card paraphrases it.
6. Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures (formerly the Oriental Institute), University of Chicago,
   "Persepolis Fortification Archive", https://isac.uchicago.edu/node/2474 (*opened*): found in 1933 in two small
   rooms of a bastion in the fortification wall; tens of thousands of tablets and fragments, about 15,000–18,000
   with Elamite texts and 5,000–6,000 with seal impressions only, a few in Greek, Phrygian and Old Persian; around
   500 BC; food storage and outlays, from workers to the king's family; most came to Chicago on loan in 1936.
7. W. F. M. Henkelman, *The Other Gods Who Are: Studies in Elamite-Iranian Acculturation Based on the Persepolis
   Fortification Texts*, Achaemenid History 14 (Leuven: Peeters, 2008) (*publisher's page opened*,
   https://nino-leiden.nl/publication/the-other-gods-who-are; the book itself was not read): its subject, the gods
   and cult of the Persian heartland in the tablets, Elamite and Iranian together.
8. M. Lufkin, "Terror victims cannot seize Iranian antiquities, US Supreme Court rules", *The Art Newspaper*,
   22 February 2018 (*opened*): Rubin v. Islamic Republic of Iran, decided 21 February 2018; the Persepolis
   collection in Chicago immune from seizure; the loan dated 1937.
9. Smithsonian Freer|Sackler Archives blog, "A Colorful Past", 10 July 2012, https://asia-archive.si.edu/a-colorful-past
   (*opened*): pigment traces at Persepolis, blue from a copper compound and red from ochre; Alexander Nagel's
   work there; glass covers over the best patches.
10. A. Nagel, *Color and Meaning in the Art of Achaemenid Persia* (Cambridge University Press) (*search only*, via
    the Fashion Institute of Technology news page, 12 February 2024; year not confirmed).
11. The archive's chapter on Zoroastrianism (ch06): the winged figure as the best-known Zoroastrian emblem and its
    modern ethical reading; whether the early Achaemenids were Zoroastrians debated.

## Where sources differ
- **The year of the loan to Chicago:** 1936 (ISAC) or 1937 (*The Art Newspaper*). The card says "in the 1930s"
  and flags the difference.
- **Column height:** about 19.5 m (Shahbazi), about 20 m (UNESCO), 19 m (Mark). The model uses 19.5 m.

## Figures used in the model (dims.js)
- SURVEY: terrace height 12 m and size; the stairway's step counts and step size (the model's risers are 10.8 cm
  so that 111 of them reach 12 m: published "10 cm"); the Gate's area and column height; the Apadana's hall,
  column count, height and thickness, podium height and stair length; the Hall of a Hundred Columns; the Tachara;
  the Treasury.
- APPROX: where every building stands; the porticoes' depth; the stairs' layout and the Apadana staircase's depth;
  the jambs' sizes; which columns stand; the hill, the cliff and the tombs' places.

## Not used (could not be confirmed)
- The terrace's compass orientation, and any claim that it was laid out to the sun or stars: none was found in the
  sources opened, so the model claims no alignment.
- Which wing of the east staircase carries which procession, and the identities of the 23 delegations: the model
  puts the delegations on the south wing (APPROX) and the card says they are not meant to be identified.
- That the Gate's east door led to the Hall of a Hundred Columns, and that the walls were of mud brick: left off
  the cards.
