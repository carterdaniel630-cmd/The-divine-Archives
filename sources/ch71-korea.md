## Notes
- Well-supported: dolmens; Samguk yusa; musok ethnography and its suppression; Buddhist arrival dates; Seokguram/Bulguksa; Wonhyo, Uisang, Jinul; Tripitaka Koreana; Joseon ancestor rites; Hangul and its commentary; lay-founded Catholicism and persecutions; Donghak; census.
- Not supported: Dangun as historical and 2333 BCE; North Korea's Dangun tomb; Ichadon miracles; musok as unchanged prehistoric survival.
- Open: dolmen rites; shamanism's origins; Seokguram's design and alignment; counting multiple belonging; religion's future in South Korea.
- Wonhyo's skull-water episode is told as a story, not as documented fact.

Confirmed from search results of the indexed full text (the journal site, DOAJ and the PDF were blocked from this environment, so specific page numbers could not be checked): the History Research Institute announced the excavation in October 1993 (Taebak Mountain, Kangdong county, Pyongyang); Kim Il-sung ordered it on 8 January 1993 and gave guidance more than twenty times; the authors call it "a politically orchestrated task with a predetermined conclusion" that moved from subjective interpretation to "fabrication and forgery"; the tomb was then used to redate Old Chosŏn to 5,000 years ago. North Korea's claim that the man's bones were dated by electron spin resonance to more than 5,011 years ago appears in search results, but whether it comes from this article could not be confirmed, so it was not added to the chapter. The chapter's sentence (North Korea announced the find; independent scholars do not accept it) matches this source and was not changed. Pai (2000) remains cited for the modern nationalist uses of the myth; whether it discusses the tomb is still unconfirmed.
