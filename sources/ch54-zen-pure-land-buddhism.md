# Sources — Chapter 54: Zen & Pure Land Buddhism

Per the project sourcing standard. A tradition chapter pairing the two great East Asian Mahayana paths (self-power and other-power), keeping documented history distinct from the semi-legendary Chan lineage and from the metaphysical content of the faith.

## Sources cited
- **Zen/Chan history: origins in 6th-century China (Mahayana + Daoist sensibility), Bodhidharma (c. 520 CE), the "special transmission outside the scriptures," Huineng (Platform Sutra) and sudden enlightenment, zazen, the kōan, and the Rinzai/Sōtō schools (Eisai; Dōgen and shikantaza)** — "Zen," Encyclopædia Britannica. https://www.britannica.com/topic/Zen ; "Zen: Early Masters & Teachings," University at Buffalo Research Guides. https://research.lib.buffalo.edu/buddhism/zen-early-masters
- **Zen practice and the East Asian meditation schools (Seon, Thiền); kōan collections (Blue Cliff Record, Gateless Gate)** — "What is Zen Buddhism?", Tricycle. https://tricycle.org/buddhism-meditation-schools/
- **Pure Land: Amitābha/Amida and the vow, Sukhāvatī (the western Pure Land), the nembutsu (Namu Amida Butsu), the mappō doctrine, and the Chinese systematizers Tanluan, Daochuo, Shandao** — "Pure Land Buddhism: History, Teachings, and Practices," Tricycle. https://tricycle.org/buddhism-pure-land/ ; "The Origins and Practices of Pure Land Buddhism," Learn Religions. https://www.learnreligions.com/pure-land-buddhism-450043
- **Hōnen (Jōdo-shū) and Shinran (Jōdo Shinshū): single-hearted nembutsu, salvation by faith alone, akunin shōki (the sinner as the object of the vow), non-celibate clergy** — "Jōdo Shinshū," Encyclopedia.com. https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/jodo-shinshu ; "Jōdo Shinshū," Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8Ddo_Shinsh%C5%AB
- **Hōnen's Senchakushū drawing on Shandao and Tanluan** — "Senchakushū," Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senchakush%C5%AB
- **Supporting detail corroborated via the above and adjacent chapters:** Chan–Pure Land dual cultivation in China; the Kamakura reformation (with Nichiren); the ensō and Ten Ox-Herding Pictures; the raigō (Amida's welcoming descent); Zen's influence on East Asian and Western arts.

## Notes
- Well-supported: the historical development of Chan/Zen and its spread; the historicity of Huineng's era, Dōgen, Eisai; zazen/shikantaza/kōan; the Chinese Pure Land systematizers; Hōnen and Shinran and their schools; mappō, nembutsu, other-power, akunin shōki; the cultural legacy.
- Not supported (as fact): Bodhidharma's biography (nine years' wall-gazing) and much of the early Chan lineage (patriarchal genealogies are, in significant part, later retrospective constructions); the illiterate Huineng's awakening (sacred narrative); the metaphysical claims (Amida's Pure Land, "mind-to-mind" transmission) are faith, not history.
- Genuinely open: recoverable earliest Chan history behind the legend; dating/authorship of the Platform Sutra and Pure Land sutras; inner efficacy of the practices (documented, not adjudicated).
- Placed in era 06 (Early Medieval): the maturing of Chinese Chan/Pure Land and their transmission toward Japan (with the distinctively Japanese schools crystallizing in the Kamakura period).
- Connects to ch20 (Mahayana), ch12 (Daoism), ch16 (grace/Christianity), ch31 (Reformation/faith alone), ch25 (Shinto coexistence).
